How to Read This Guide — And Its Expiry Date

Every other guide in this section teaches principles that will still be true in ten years. This one is different: it contains a dated snapshot of where Indian markets actually stood on 21 October 2026, an analysis of what was driving them, and a structured plan for someone starting from zero. The principles at the end will keep. The numbers will not.

This is education, not adviceNothing here is a recommendation to buy or sell any security, fund or asset class. No company named below is a suggestion — companies appear only as illustrations of a market-wide pattern. Markets can and do fall further than seems reasonable; you can lose money, including all of it. Past performance says nothing reliable about future returns. For advice on your specific situation, consult a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser and a chartered accountant. Verify every number here against a primary source before acting on it.
Snapshot date: 21 October 2026All market figures in sections 2 to 8 were captured on that date from Trading Economics, which aggregates exchange and official statistical data. Index levels move daily; policy rates move every few weeks; the narrative can invert in a month. If you are reading this more than a quarter after the snapshot date, treat sections 2 to 8 as a worked example of how to read a market, not as a description of the market you are in. Section 16 shows you how to rebuild the entire analysis yourself in about thirty minutes.

What this guide is for

You have a PAN and a demat account. You have read, or are about to read, the other guides in this section. What you do not have is a sense of where you are standing — whether the market you are about to step into is cheap or expensive, calm or stressed, early or late in a cycle. That context does not tell you what will happen next. Nobody can tell you that. But it does change what a sensible first move looks like, and it changes which mistakes are most likely to hurt you.

Snapshot: Indian Equities on 21 October 2026

The headline is simple and uncomfortable: Indian equities had been falling for roughly a year. The benchmarks were down around 12 to 13 per cent over twelve months and the Sensex sat close to 15 per cent below its all-time high. This was a correction, not a crash — but it was the first sustained drawdown many newer Indian investors had experienced.

Putting a 14.5% drawdown in context

A beginner's instinct on seeing "down 13% in a year" is either panic or bargain-hunting euphoria. Both are wrong, because 13% is not a remarkable number. Equity markets are supposed to do this. The table below is the honest frame of reference.

Drawdown depthRough historical frequency in equity marketsWhat it usually is
5–10%Roughly once or twice a yearOrdinary noise. Not a signal of anything.
10–20%Roughly once every two to three yearsA correction. Often a growth scare, a rate shock or a sentiment reset.
20–35%Roughly once a decadeA bear market. Usually a recession, a policy shock or a credit event.
Over 40%A few times a centuryA systemic crisis. 2008 and March 2020 are the modern Indian reference points.
Frequencies are approximate and historicalThese are rough long-run frequencies across major equity markets, not a forecast and not a guarantee that Indian markets will follow the same distribution. They are here to calibrate your emotional response, not to predict anything. Build your own version of this table from Nifty TRI history — the method is in section 16.

So a 14.5% drawdown is firmly in "this happens regularly" territory. It is neither evidence that the market is broken nor evidence that it is now cheap. What makes this particular drawdown worth understanding is not its depth but its cause, because the cause tells you something about what might resolve it.

Index-level numbers hide an enormous amount. On the snapshot date the dispersion between individual large caps over twelve months was extreme — some of India's largest and most widely held companies had fallen 30% while others had risen.

On the snapshot day itself, the rotation was visible intraday: IT names were up two to three per cent while financials, autos, metals and FMCG were down. The market was not selling everything — it was selling a specific set of exposures and buying another. Section 8 explains what those exposures have in common.

Primary market activity

One more data point matters for reading sentiment. In the same week, Reliance Industries' digital subsidiary Jio Platforms launched a roughly US$3.2 billion initial public offering, and the public bidding portion was fully subscribed within two hours of opening. A market in genuine distress does not absorb a multi-billion-dollar listing in two hours.

The signal in thatHeavy IPO absorption alongside a falling secondary market is a classic late-cycle-but-not-broken pattern: domestic liquidity is intact and looking for a home, even while foreign capital is leaving and the index is drifting down. It tells you the drawdown is being driven by something other than a shortage of domestic money. Note also what it does not tell you — strong IPO subscription has historically been a poor predictor of listing-day or long-term returns. See the IPO section of How the Indian Stock Market Works.

The Macro Picture: Inflation and the RBI

Equity drawdowns usually have a driver sitting underneath them. On this occasion the driver was not a credit crisis or an earnings collapse. It was inflation, and the monetary tightening it forced.

Inflation was accelerating, not just elevated

September 2026 CPI printed at 5.54%, up sharply from 4.83% the month before. The direction matters more than the level. The Reserve Bank of India operates a flexible inflation targeting framework with a 4% target and a tolerance band of 2% to 6%; at 5.54% and rising, inflation was inside the band but heading for the ceiling, and commentators at the time expected the October print to breach 6%.

Why the ceiling matters: if average inflation stays outside the
2-6% band for three consecutive quarters, the RBI is required to
submit a written report to the central government explaining the
failure, the remedial actions proposed, and the time it expects
to take to return inflation to target.

That obligation is a powerful institutional incentive. A central
bank approaching it will usually act pre-emptively and visibly.

The policy response

The RBI had raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% at its October 2026 meeting, up from 5.25%, and had shifted its stance to "calibrated tightening". Interest-rate swap markets were pricing roughly 100 basis points of further hikes over the following twelve months, including the possibility of a 50 basis point move in December.

Here is the single most useful calculation you can do with these two numbers:

TEXT
REAL POLICY RATE
-----------------
Repo rate                        5.50%
CPI inflation (Sep 2026)         5.54%
Real policy rate             =  -0.04%   (essentially zero)

Interpretation: with the policy rate roughly equal to inflation,
monetary policy was close to neutral in real terms, arguably still
slightly accommodative. A central bank that wants to actually slow
inflation generally needs a positive real policy rate.

Implication: the market's expectation of further hikes was not
speculative. It followed directly from the arithmetic.
Why a beginner should care about the repo rate at allThree transmission channels. First, discounting: higher rates mean future company cash flows are worth less today, which compresses the multiple investors will pay — this hits high-growth and expensive stocks hardest. Second, competition: when a government bond yields 7.45% risk-free, equities must offer a materially better expected return to justify their risk, so money rotates out. Third, the real economy: higher rates mean costlier home loans, car loans and corporate borrowing, which slows demand and eventually earnings. All three were operating simultaneously.

The rest of the domestic picture was not weak

Importantly, this was not a recession story. Unemployment was stable at 5.00%, and infrastructure output was reported to be growing at its fastest pace in nearly three years. Bank earnings were mixed rather than bad — ICICI Bank and HDFC Bank both reported above expectations in the results season, while Axis Bank came in below. One large non-bank lender flagged early signs of stress in loans against gold, which is worth noting as a potential leading indicator but was, at that point, a single data point.

The distinction that mattersAn equity drawdown caused by rate and inflation pressure on a growing economy is a fundamentally different animal from one caused by collapsing earnings in a shrinking economy. The first is a valuation reset and historically resolves when the rate cycle turns. The second is an earnings reset and takes longer. On the snapshot date, the evidence pointed clearly to the first. That is an observation about the past, not a forecast.

The Oil Shock Driving Everything

If you want to understand this entire market in one variable, it is the price of crude. India imports the overwhelming majority of the oil it consumes, which makes a sustained crude rally one of the most reliably damaging macro events for the Indian economy and for Indian equities.

Brent had approached US$112 per barrel, a five-month high, on Red Sea shipping risks and reported threats to Saudi oil infrastructure. An 80% rise in a year in India's single largest import is not a sector story. It is a macro regime change.

The transmission chain, step by step

This is the most valuable thing in this guide for a beginner, because once you can trace a chain like this you can analyse any macro shock rather than memorising conclusions.

How an oil shock propagates through an oil-importing economy into equity prices. Every link in this chain was visible in the data on 21 October 2026.

This explains the rotation you saw in section 2IT up, financials and autos and FMCG down, is exactly what the table above predicts. The market was not behaving irrationally. It was repricing a specific macro scenario with considerable internal consistency. Being able to look at a day's sector movement and say "that is consistent with an oil-and-rates story" is a genuinely useful analytical skill, and it is available to you from free public data.
Do not turn this into a tradeThe chain above is an explanation of what has happened. It is not a prediction, and it is certainly not a basis for buying "oil beneficiaries" and shorting "oil losers". By the time a macro narrative is this legible, it is substantially priced in. Markets move on the change in expectations, not on the current state. If crude falls back to $70 next quarter, every relationship in that table reverses violently. Beginners lose money by acting on macro narratives; they make money by owning diversified assets through macro narratives.

Bonds and the Yield Curve: The Other Half of the Market

Most new investors look only at equities. That is a mistake, and never more so than in a tightening cycle, because the bond market is both the mechanism transmitting the shock and — at these yields — a genuine competing opportunity.

The 10-year G-Sec yield at 7.45% was its highest since March 2023. The curve was upward sloping across its entire length, which is the normal shape — longer money costs more — and the whole curve had shifted up by roughly 70 to 130 basis points over twelve months. That parallel-ish upward shift is the signature of a market repricing the entire future path of policy rates, not just the next meeting.

The real yield calculation

More precisely, using the Fisher relation:
  (1.0745 / 1.0554) - 1 = 1.81%

So a rupee lent to the Government of India for ten years was
earning roughly 1.8-1.9% above current inflation, before tax.

AFTER TAX, for someone in the 30% slab (interest is taxed at
slab rates, not as capital gains):
  Post-tax nominal yield = 7.45% x (1 - 0.30) = 5.22%
  Post-tax real yield    = 5.22% - 5.54%      = -0.32%

This is the single most important number for an Indian saver to
understand: a 7.45% "safe" return is, for a top-slab taxpayer at
5.54% inflation, slightly NEGATIVE in real terms.
The lesson hidden in that calculation"Safe" assets are not safe from inflation and tax. A fixed deposit or bond yielding less than inflation grossed up for your tax rate is a slow, guaranteed erosion of purchasing power. This is precisely why long-horizon money generally has to take equity risk — not because equities are better, but because the apparently safe alternative has its own, quieter, form of loss. Read the real-returns section of Investing Foundations before you decide anything about your debt allocation.

What a rising-rate environment does to bond prices

If you hold a bond to maturity, you get the yield you bought at. If you hold a bond fund, you own a continuously rolling portfolio and its NAV moves inversely with yields. This catches beginners every cycle.

TEXT
APPROXIMATE PRICE IMPACT OF A YIELD MOVE
-----------------------------------------
Price change  =  -Modified Duration  x  Change in yield

Example A: a gilt fund with modified duration 7.0
  Yields rise 50 bps  ->  -7.0 x 0.50%  =  -3.5% NAV impact
  Yields rise 100 bps ->  -7.0 x 1.00%  =  -7.0% NAV impact

Example B: a short-duration fund with modified duration 1.5
  Yields rise 50 bps  ->  -1.5 x 0.50%  =  -0.75% NAV impact
  Yields rise 100 bps ->  -1.5 x 1.00%  =  -1.5% NAV impact

With swap markets pricing ~100 bps of further hikes, the duration
you choose was the dominant risk decision in the debt sleeve.
Long-duration funds carry equity-like volatility in a hiking cycle.

(Convexity makes the true move slightly better than this linear
approximation in both directions. The approximation is fine for
understanding the order of magnitude.)
Where to go deeperDuration, accrual versus duration strategies, and the post-2023 taxation of debt funds are covered properly in Mutual Funds, SIPs, ETFs & Index Investing. The point here is only that in a tightening cycle, the type of debt instrument matters far more than it does in a stable one.

Currency and the Global Context

India does not trade in isolation. On the snapshot date, the most striking feature of the global picture was divergence: US equities were near record highs while Indian equities were 15% off theirs. Understanding why is understanding capital flows.

The rupee

₹94.81USD / INR
+8.05%USDINR, 1 YEAR
₹106.37EUR / INR
₹126.84GBP / INR

The rupee was trading around 94.8 per dollar — roughly 8% weaker than a year earlier, though it had just put in a seven-session winning streak to a six-week high, helped by expected dollar inflows from the Jio Platforms IPO and continued RBI intervention. The central bank was reported to be selling dollars and tightening scrutiny of large overseas investments to curb capital outflows.

Why the rupee matters to your portfolioThree effects. Imported inflation: a weaker rupee makes an already expensive barrel of oil more expensive in rupee terms, which is why currency and oil reinforce each other. Sector effects: exporters (IT, pharma) benefit, importers suffer. Foreign returns: a foreign investor in Indian equities earns the local return minus the currency depreciation, which is why a falling rupee tends to accelerate foreign selling in a weak market — a self-reinforcing loop. It is also why holding some international assets is a currency hedge for an Indian investor, a point developed in Building & Managing a Portfolio.

This is the key insight of the whole snapshotWhen US risk-free yields are above 5% and the rupee is depreciating, global capital has very little reason to sit in Indian assets. The drawdown in Indian equities was, to a significant degree, a capital flow phenomenon rather than a judgement on Indian companies. Flow-driven drawdowns reverse when the flow reverses — which typically requires the US rate cycle to turn or the rupee to stabilise. Neither is predictable, which is exactly why you accumulate systematically rather than trying to time the turn.

An inconvenient observation about goldGold fell roughly 4.5% over a year in which oil rose 80%, inflation accelerated and geopolitical risk was elevated — precisely the conditions in which gold is popularly supposed to shine. Reading the Situation: Five Honest Interpretations

Here is where most market commentary goes wrong: it picks one story and sells it with confidence. The intellectually honest approach is to lay out the plausible readings of the same data, note what evidence would support each, and accept that you cannot know which is correct in advance.

1. Mid-cycle correction, resolves with the rate cycle

The economy is growing, unemployment is stable, infrastructure output is strong, bank earnings are broadly fine. This is a valuation reset caused by rates, not an earnings collapse. When oil normalises and the hiking cycle ends, multiples recover. Supporting evidence: no credit event, solid IPO absorption, mixed-to-good earnings. What would break it: earnings actually rolling over.

2. Early innings of something worse

Inflation is accelerating, the RBI is behind the curve, 100 bps of hikes are coming, and the lagged effect of tightening on demand and credit has not shown up yet. The gold-loan stress flagged by one large lender is an early warning. Supporting evidence: negative real policy rate, oil still rising. What would break it: crude falling back, inflation rolling over.

3. A pure capital-flow event

With US 10-year yields above 5% and a 233 bps India-US spread against an 8% rupee depreciation, the carry trade is dead. Foreign money is leaving for arithmetic reasons unrelated to Indian fundamentals. Domestic flows are absorbing it, slowly. Supporting evidence: the spread maths, persistent FPI outflows, IPO absorbed domestically. What would break it: the Fed easing, or the rupee stabilising.

4. A healthy deflation of prior excess

Markets that have run hard for years build up valuation excess, particularly in small and mid caps. A drawdown concentrated in the most widely held, most crowded large caps looks like positioning unwinding rather than fundamental deterioration. Supporting evidence: the dispersion in section 2. What would break it: evidence the falls are tracking downgrades, not de-rating.

5. All four, simultaneously, in different proportions

This is almost always the correct answer, and it is why confident single-cause market narratives should make you suspicious. Markets are the aggregation of millions of decisions driven by different reasons. The oil shock is real and the flow story is real and some of the fall is positioning. The honest conclusion is that the drawdown has identifiable, largely macro causes, that those causes are visible in public data, and that the timing of their resolution is genuinely unknowable.

What you must not take from this sectionDo not conclude "the market is cheap, time to go all in" and do not conclude "worse is coming, stay in cash". Both are forecasts, and the evidence on retail (and professional) forecasting accuracy is dismal. The correct response to genuine uncertainty is not a better forecast — it is a process that does not require one. That is what sections 9 to 14 describe.

What is deliberately missing from this analysis

Being explicit about the gaps is part of doing this honestly. On the snapshot date, three things that would materially sharpen the picture were not in the data captured:

  • Valuation. Nifty trailing and forward P/E, P/B and earnings yield versus the bond yield. Without this, "down 13%" cannot be converted into "cheap" or "still expensive". Get it from the NSE's own index factsheet — see section 16.
  • Broad-market breadth. Index levels for the Midcap 150 and Smallcap 250, and the advance-decline picture. Large-cap benchmarks can mask far deeper damage, or far greater froth, in the broader market.
  • Actual flow numbers. "Continued foreign fund outflows" is a qualitative statement. The daily FII/DII figures are published free by NSE and the depositories and would quantify it.
Make this your first exerciseGo and fill in those three gaps yourself for today's market. It will take under thirty minutes using the sources in section 16 and Market Data, Research & News Sources. Doing it once will teach you more than reading ten market-outlook articles.

What This Environment Means If You Are Starting Now

Now the practical part. The market context above does not change the fundamentals of how you should invest, but it does change which risks are most live and which mistakes are most likely.

The genuinely good news for a beginner

You are not buying a top

Starting a multi-decade accumulation programme roughly 15% below a peak, with sentiment poor, is structurally more comfortable than starting during euphoria. You get more units for the same rupee. This is not a prediction that the bottom is in — it is simply the arithmetic of a lower entry price for someone who will be buying for thirty years.

You will learn volatility early and cheaply

Experiencing a drawdown on a ₹50,000 portfolio is a far better teacher than experiencing one on a ₹50 lakh portfolio. You will discover your actual risk tolerance — which is almost never the same as your imagined risk tolerance — while the stakes are small.

Debt and cash finally pay something

Short-duration debt and fixed deposits at these yields are a genuine, if modest, alternative for near-term goals, rather than the near-zero-return proposition they are in an easing cycle. Your emergency fund is not being punished for existing.

The honest summaryIf you are starting a thirty-year accumulation programme, the state of the market this month is close to irrelevant to your final outcome — your savings rate, your costs, your asset allocation and your ability to not interfere will matter vastly more. The market context matters mainly because it determines which psychological trap is currently open in front of you. Right now that trap is "this is falling, I should stop" and its twin, "this has fallen, I should bet big on a bounce".

Where to Actually Start: The Order of Operations

You asked where to begin. The answer is a sequence, and the sequence matters more than any individual choice within it. Do these in order. Do not skip ahead because a later step is more interesting — it always is.

  1. Gate 0 — Clear the prerequisitesEmergency fund of six months of expenses in a sweep-in FD or liquid fund. Term life insurance if anyone depends on your income. Health insurance beyond your employer's cover. Zero credit-card and personal-loan balances. Until all four are true, investing is premature regardless of how attractive the market looks. Worked sizing for each is in Investing Foundations.
  2. Gate 1 — Write down the plan before you buy anythingA one-page Investment Policy Statement: your goals and their horizons, your target asset allocation, how much goes in each month, your rebalancing rule, and — critically — a pre-committed statement of what you will do if the portfolio falls 30%. Writing this before you have money at risk is the single highest-return thirty minutes in this entire section. Template in Building & Managing a Portfolio.
  3. Gate 2 — Fix the plumbingVerify your broker's SEBI registration. Add nominations to the demat account and every mutual fund folio. Confirm you are buying direct plans, not regular. Set up a dedicated investing bank account. Turn on the auto-debit mandate so the decision is made once, not monthly.
  4. Gate 3 — Start with a broad, boring, cheap coreYour first investment should be a diversified, low-cost, broad-market equity index fund bought through a monthly SIP, plus a short-duration debt allocation sized to your near-term goals. Not a stock. Not a sectoral fund. Not an NFO. The reason is not that this maximises returns — it is that it is the only approach that is robust to the fact that you currently know very little, including about yourself.
  5. Gate 4 — Run it untouched for six monthsDo not add holdings. Do not switch. Do not check it daily. The objective of the first six months is not returns; it is to observe your own behaviour through a period of fluctuation and to prove to yourself that you can leave it alone.
  6. Gate 5 — Only then consider a satelliteAfter six months of boring execution, and only after working through Analysing a Stock, you may allocate a strictly capped satellite sleeve — a sensible ceiling for a beginner is 10% of the equity portion — to individual stocks or a thematic position. Money in the satellite must be money you can watch fall 50% without changing anything in the core.
  7. Gate 6 — Derivatives, if ever, come lastNot in year one. Read the evidence first. If after a year you still want to, start with six months of paper trading and a written plan, and never with naked short options.
On asset allocation specificsThis guide deliberately does not tell you "put X% in equity". That number depends on your age, income stability, dependants, existing assets, goal horizons and genuine risk tolerance — none of which it knows. Building & Managing a Portfolio gives you several allocation shapes expressed in asset-class terms, each with its assumptions and its worst historical drawdown, so you can choose deliberately rather than copying a number off the internet.

A note on how much, and the emotional trap of "waiting for a better level"

TEXT
THE "WAIT FOR THE BOTTOM" ARITHMETIC
-------------------------------------
Suppose you plan to invest 20,000 rupees a month for 30 years.
Total contributions = 72,00,000 rupees.

You decide to wait six months for "a better entry", holding the
money in a savings account, then start.

What you have actually done:
  - Delayed six contributions (1,20,000 rupees) by 30 years
  - At 10% compounding, those six contributions would have grown
    to roughly 20-21 lakh rupees by year 30
  - You have gained, at best, a few percent on a single-digit
    fraction of your lifetime contributions

And that is the GOOD case, where the market obligingly falls.
If it rises 10% while you wait, you have lost on both counts.

The uncomfortable conclusion: for a long-horizon accumulator,
TIME IN the market dominates TIMING the market, and the cost of
waiting is paid in the most valuable years of compounding --
the earliest ones.
The honest caveatThat arithmetic argues against waiting with money you have already decided to invest. It does not argue for investing money you need within three years, or for borrowing to invest, or for abandoning your asset allocation. And if you are deploying a large lump sum rather than a monthly flow, the staggering question is genuinely more balanced — the evidence and the worked comparison are in Mutual Funds, SIPs, ETFs & Index Investing.

The paper-trading discipline

A paper portfolio only teaches you something if you treat it seriously: real position sizes, real entry and exit rules, written theses with dates, and an honest post-mortem. A paper portfolio where you quietly forget the losers teaches you nothing except overconfidence. Record every decision before the outcome is known.

What to deliberately ignore

Daily market commentary, price targets, "top 5 stocks for Diwali" lists, intraday calls, anything with a WhatsApp or Telegram join link, anyone showing profit screenshots, and any claim of a win rate above 70%. None of this is information. All of it is a product being sold to you.

Verify before you trust anyoneUnder SEBI regulations, anyone giving personalised investment advice for consideration must be a registered Investment Adviser, and anyone publishing research recommendations must be a registered Research Analyst. Registration numbers can be checked directly on the SEBI website's intermediary search. An unregistered "advisor", however convincing, is operating illegally — and you have essentially no recourse when it goes wrong. Verify, every time, before money moves.

Scenario Playbook: What To Do If…

Decide now, in a calm moment, what you will do in each of these situations. A decision made in advance is a plan; the same decision made during the event is a reaction, and reactions are reliably worse.

Refresh This Analysis Yourself: The 30-Minute Quarterly Routine

The most valuable thing this guide can leave you with is not its conclusions but its method. Here is exactly how to rebuild the entire snapshot above for whatever market you are actually standing in. Do it once a quarter. It takes about half an hour.

  1. Equities — 5 minutesRecord the Nifty 50 and Sensex levels, and their 1-month, 1-year and 3-year returns. Note the distance from the all-time high. Do the same for the Nifty Midcap 150 and Smallcap 250, because the broad market often tells a very different story from the benchmark. Source: NSE and BSE index pages.
  2. Valuation — 5 minutesRecord the Nifty 50 trailing P/E, P/B and dividend yield from the NSE's own index data page. Compare each with its own 10-year range — that comparison, not the absolute number, is what tells you whether the market is expensive. Compute the earnings yield (1/PE) and compare it with the 10-year G-Sec yield; the gap is a crude but useful equity-versus-bond attractiveness gauge.
  3. Inflation and policy — 5 minutesRecord the latest CPI print and its direction, the repo rate, and the RBI's stated stance from the most recent monetary policy statement. Compute the real policy rate (repo minus CPI). A negative real policy rate with rising inflation means more tightening is likely; a strongly positive one means the cycle may be near its end. Source: mospi.gov.in and rbi.org.in.
  4. Bonds — 3 minutesRecord the 10-year G-Sec yield and at least the 2-year, so you can see the shape of the curve. Compute the real yield and the post-tax real yield at your slab. Note the change over the last quarter — the direction matters more than the level.
  5. Currency and commodities — 3 minutesUSD/INR and its 1-year change. Brent crude and its 1-year change. Gold. For India, crude is the single most important external variable; for your personal portfolio, the rupee is the second.
  6. Global — 4 minutesUS 10-year Treasury yield, the Fed funds rate and US CPI. Compute the India–US 10-year spread. The narrower the spread and the weaker the rupee, the less attractive Indian assets are to foreign capital, and the more likely persistent outflows become.
  7. Flows — 3 minutesMonth-to-date and year-to-date FII and DII net equity flows. Published free by NSE and by the depositories. This quantifies what commentary describes only qualitatively.
  8. Write three sentences — 2 minutesOne sentence on what the data says, one on what you think is driving it, and one on what would have to change for that driver to reverse. Date it and file it. After four quarters you will have something far more valuable than any market outlook you could buy: a record of your own reasoning, which you can score against what actually happened.

Frequently Asked Questions

The market is down 13% over a year. Is this a good time to start?
For a long-horizon accumulator, the honest answer is that it is a better starting point than a peak, and that this matters far less than you think. Starting roughly 15% below the high means you buy more units per rupee than someone who started a year ago, which is genuinely helpful. But nobody knows whether the next 15% is up or down, and over a thirty-year horizon the entry level in month one is swamped by your savings rate, your costs and your discipline. The practically useful version of the question is not "is this a good time" but "do I have a process that does not require me to know". If the answer is yes, start now. If the answer is no, build the process first — that takes a week, not a market cycle.
Should I wait for the RBI to stop raising rates before investing in equities?
That would require you to correctly identify the end of a hiking cycle in advance, which professional rates desks routinely fail to do. More importantly, equity markets are forward-looking: by the time it is obvious that the cycle has peaked, a substantial part of the re-rating has typically already happened. The practical answer is that if you are investing monthly over decades, the rate cycle is noise — you will live through many of them. If you are deploying a large lump sum, staggering it over a defined period is a reasonable way to manage regret, but choose the period in advance and stick to it regardless of what the RBI does.
Large caps I recognise are down 30%. Isn't that an obvious opportunity?
It is an obvious candidate for research, which is a very different thing. A price decline tells you nothing about value on its own — the question is whether the business's future cash flows have deteriorated more or less than the price has. Sometimes a 30% fall is an overreaction; sometimes it is the market correctly recognising that growth has structurally slowed, in which case the stock can fall another 50% and still not be cheap. Familiarity is also a trap: you recognise the name because it is widely held and heavily advertised, not because you understand its economics. If you want to act on this, work through Analysing a Stock, write a thesis, and size the position so that being wrong does not matter much.
With the 10-year government bond at 7.45%, why take equity risk at all?
Because 7.45% is a nominal, pre-tax number. Interest is taxed at your slab rate, so a 30%-bracket taxpayer nets roughly 5.2%, against inflation of 5.54% — a slightly negative real return. You would be locking in a guaranteed, slow erosion of purchasing power for ten years. That said, the comparison is not pointless: at these yields, debt is a far more useful component of a portfolio than it is when yields are low, and for goals inside three to five years it is clearly the right instrument. The answer is allocation, not a binary choice. Note also the duration warning in section 5 — if you access that yield through a long-duration fund rather than by holding a bond to maturity, further rate rises will hit your NAV.
Foreign investors are selling. Shouldn't I sell too?
Look at why they are selling before copying it. The arithmetic in section 6 shows that with US 10-year yields above 5%, a 233 basis point India–US spread and an 8% annual rupee depreciation, a dollar investor earns a negative dollar return on Indian bonds and a heavily currency-dragged return on Indian equities. They are responding to a currency-and-carry problem that you do not have, because you earn, spend, save and retire in rupees. Foreign selling creates price pressure that affects you, but their reason for selling does not apply to you. Blindly mirroring a different investor's constraints is one of the more subtle ways retail participants hurt themselves.
IT stocks were rallying while everything else fell. Should I buy an IT fund?
The rally is consistent with a weak rupee helping dollar-revenue exporters, plus defensive rotation — so there is a real mechanism, not just momentum. But three problems. First, you are observing a move that has already happened; the repricing is substantially done. Second, sector funds concentrate risk precisely when you most need diversification, and sector rotation in India is fast and unforgiving. Third, the mechanism reverses the moment the rupee strengthens. Sectoral and thematic funds have a well-documented pattern of attracting the most money near the end of the cycle that made them attractive. If you want exposure to IT, you already have it through a broad-market index fund, weighted by the market's own judgement rather than yours.
Gold fell while inflation rose and there was geopolitical risk. Why?
Because the popular story about gold is much simpler than gold's actual behaviour. Gold is a non-yielding asset, so it competes directly with real yields: when real interest rates rise — as they did globally in this period, with US 10-year yields above 5% — the opportunity cost of holding gold rises and it tends to underperform, regardless of inflation headlines or geopolitics. The useful lesson is broader than gold: asset classes do not reliably obey the narratives attached to them. Hold gold as a modest, genuinely uncorrelated diversifier if your allocation calls for it, not because you have been told it protects against a specific event.
Should I invest in international funds given this?
There is a real structural case — Indian equities are roughly a low-single-digit share of global market capitalisation, your human capital and property are already entirely rupee-denominated, and the divergence in this snapshot (US near highs, India down 13%) is exactly the sort of thing diversification is for. There are also real frictions: the Liberalised Remittance Scheme limit and TCS on remittances if you invest directly abroad, Schedule FA disclosure obligations, less favourable tax treatment for most international funds compared with domestic equity funds, and the fact that SEBI has at times capped overseas investment by Indian mutual funds. A modest allocation is defensible for most investors; the specifics, including the tax and compliance treatment, are in Building & Managing a Portfolio and Taxation & Regulation. Verify the current LRS, TCS and fund-level limits before acting — they change.
How much of my salary should go into investing?
After the Gate 0 prerequisites are met, a commonly cited target is 20% or more of take-home pay, and more if you started late or have ambitious goals. But the right number is derived, not borrowed: list your goals, their amounts in today's rupees, their horizons, inflate them, and work backwards to a monthly requirement at a conservative assumed return. The worked method is in Investing Foundations. One strong practical rule — set the base amount at a level you could sustain through a bad year, then direct a fixed fraction of every raise and bonus into increasing it. Step-ups are worth more over thirty years than almost any other decision you will make.
Index fund or actively managed fund, especially when the market is falling?
The argument that active managers protect you in a downturn is popular and weakly supported. The honest position: the evidence from SPIVA India scorecards has consistently shown a large majority of active large-cap funds underperforming their benchmark over longer horizons, with a less clear-cut picture in mid and small caps; and the funds that outperform in one period show poor persistence into the next. Costs are the most reliable predictor of relative performance, and index funds win decisively on cost. For a beginner, a low-cost broad-market index core is the default that requires no skill to select and no skill to maintain. Check the current SPIVA India scorecard yourself rather than taking this on trust — it is published free.
Can I start trading options with a small amount to learn?
Not in your first year, and the reason is the evidence rather than conservatism. SEBI's own studies of individual F&O participants have repeatedly found that the large majority — of the order of nine in ten — lose money, with substantial aggregate losses. That is not a skill gap you close by starting small; it is a structural feature of a leveraged, zero-sum, high-cost market in which your counterparties are professionals. If you want to learn the mechanics, read Futures & Options Demystified, paper trade for six months with real position sizes and a written log, and then re-read your log. Most people who do that honestly decide not to proceed, which is itself a profitable outcome.
My portfolio is down since I started. Did I do something wrong?
Almost certainly not. A portfolio being down in its first year is an expected outcome, not evidence of a mistake — roughly a third of calendar years are negative for equities. The questions worth asking are process questions, not outcome questions: Is my allocation consistent with my written plan? Am I in direct plans with low costs? Am I diversified rather than concentrated in whatever was popular? Is the money I have invested genuinely long-horizon money? If the answers are yes, you did everything right and the market did what markets do. The only real mistake available to you at this point is reacting to the drawdown.
How do I know when this analysis has gone stale?
Assume it is stale. Index levels change daily; the repo rate changes every few weeks; a crude price can move 30% in a quarter. Treat sections 2 to 8 as a frozen worked example of how to read a market, and rebuild the picture yourself using the thirty-minute routine in section 15. A specific trigger list: any RBI policy decision, any CPI print that changes direction, a 20% move in crude, a 5% move in the rupee, or a 10% move in the index. If any of those has happened since 21 October 2026 — and by the time you read this, several will have — the narrative above no longer describes your market.
What single thing matters most in my first year?
Not stopping. The mechanical act of continuing to contribute through a period when it feels wrong is the behaviour that separates investors who compound for thirty years from those who have three false starts and conclude the market is rigged. Everything else — fund selection, allocation tweaks, stock research, tax optimisation — operates at the margin compared with simply still being invested in year ten. Automate the contribution so the decision is made once, write down what you will do in a crash before the crash, and then make the first year as boring as you possibly can.

  • Concentrated more than a small, pre-capped share of your portfolio in any single stock
The closing thoughtThe market you start in is the market you remember, and starting in a drawdown is genuinely uncomfortable. But the investors who do well over decades are not the ones who picked a good year to begin — they are the ones who built a process they did not have to revisit every time the news changed, and then left it alone. The analysis in sections 2 to 8 will be obsolete within months. The process in sections 9 to 17 will not be.
