The engineers who get promoted, influence roadmaps, and build great teams are rarely those who write the cleverest code — they are the ones whose ideas consistently land in other people's heads. Communication is a learnable engineering skill with structures, frameworks, and drills, exactly like algorithms or system design. This guide is your playbook.

Why Communication Is a Multiplier for Engineers

Think of communication as a force multiplier on every other skill you have. An engineer at the 80th percentile technically who communicates at the 90th percentile consistently outperforms a 95th-percentile technical engineer who cannot explain their decisions. Why? Because software is a team sport. Value is created by ideas that other people understand and act on, not by ideas that live silently in one head.

Core insight Clarity is a courtesy you pay the listener in advance. Every minute you spend structuring a message saves ten minutes of their collective confusion — and in a meeting with ten engineers, that is 100 minutes per minute invested.
Fig 1 — Communication as a lossy channel. Your job is to maximise fidelity between intent and decoded meaning, then close the loop.

Promotes careers

Senior and staff ladders are explicitly about influence and leverage — both are communication in disguise. You cannot lead without being understood.

Scales your work

A design doc understood by 50 engineers beats a brilliant idea trapped in one head. Communication is how ideas reproduce.

Reduces rework

Most "bad engineering" is a misunderstood requirement. Clear communication is cheap insurance against expensive rebuilds.

The multiplier compounds with seniority. At the IC5/Staff level roughly 40% of your impact comes from communication: design reviews, RFC documents, mentoring, cross-team alignment. Plan accordingly.

Structuring Ideas: Pyramid Principle and BLUF

Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle is the highest-leverage structuring tool for technical communicators. The rule is simple and counterintuitive to engineers: answer first, support later. Lead with your conclusion, group supporting arguments beneath it, put data and evidence at the base.

Engineers are trained to build arguments bottom-up — "I measured X, then Y, therefore Z." That is how you discover a conclusion. It is the worst way to deliver one. Your audience wants Z first so they know why they should care about X and Y at all.

Fig 2 — The Minto Pyramid. Audiences get the answer first, then drill into detail only as far as they need. Works equally well in docs, emails, and spoken presentations.

BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front

BLUF is the military-derived email/doc convention that enforces the Pyramid Principle in one sentence. Your very first sentence is the bottom line. Every following sentence supports it.

BLUF template [Action needed / Decision / FYI] + [because key reason] + [by when if urgent]. Example: "I recommend we delay the v2 launch by two weeks because the p99 latency on the new inference path is 4× over SLA — I need a go/no-go decision by Friday EOD."
PatternBottom-up (engineer default)BLUF (audience-first)
Email opening"I've been looking at the metrics and found something interesting…""Our cache hit rate dropped 40% yesterday — here's why and how to fix it."
Standup update"Yesterday I worked on the auth service, had some trouble with JWT expiry…""Auth service is blocked on a JWT expiry edge case — needs a decision on token renewal strategy today."
Design doc intro"Background: our current search uses Elasticsearch version 6…""This doc proposes replacing Elasticsearch 6 with OpenSearch to save $80k/yr and cut search latency by 60%."

Storytelling for Technical Topics

Facts alone do not move people to action. Stories do. The most persuasive technical communicators wrap data in a narrative that creates tension and resolution. The Situation / Complication / Resolution (SCR) framework is the fastest way to do this.

Situation

Shared context the audience already knows (or needs to know) to understand why the complication matters. Keep it brief. Example: "We have 2M active users hitting our recommendation service."

Complication

What changed, broke, or creates tension. This is why the audience should care. Example: "Recommendation latency spiked from 120ms to 900ms after last week's model update."

Resolution

What you recommend or have done. Actionable. Specific. Example: "We roll back to v4 today, fix the embedding cache in v5, and gate the next rollout behind a latency budget check."
Show example: SCR for a quarterly review

Situation: "Our notification system delivers 50M push notifications per day across iOS and Android. It has been stable for three years."

Complication: "In Q3 we onboarded three new partners whose notification volumes are spiky — causing the delivery queue to back up during peak hours. Users on some accounts are seeing 20-minute delays on time-sensitive alerts, and two partners have flagged this in their renewal conversations."

Resolution: "I'm proposing we add a priority queue tier for partner SLA notifications in Q4. The change is isolated, reversible, and my estimate is 3 engineer-weeks. This resolves the partner renewal risk before the contract review in December."

The Narrative Arc in presentations

Longer presentations benefit from a three-act arc: set up the world (and a tension), develop the tension, resolve it. Every great technical talk follows this pattern whether or not the speaker planned it.

Act 1: The World Has a Problem->Act 2: We Explored Solutions->Act 3: Here Is What We Built & Learned
Hook in the first 90 seconds Audiences decide whether a talk is worth their attention in the opening 90 seconds. Lead with a striking number, a question the audience cares about, or a short story — not your agenda slide or your name/role.

Explaining Complex Concepts to Non-Technical Audiences

Engineers often make two complementary mistakes: over-explaining mechanism to people who only care about outcome, and under-explaining assumptions to people who need the full picture. The solution is a two-step approach: analogy-first, then progressive disclosure.

Analogy-first

An analogy creates a bridge between what someone already knows and what you are trying to explain. The bridge must be accurate enough not to mislead, but simple enough to be immediately understood. Test your analogy by asking: does it break if pushed even one step further? If so, acknowledge the limit.

Technical conceptWeak explanationAnalogy-first explanation
Vector embedding"It's a high-dimensional representation of semantic meaning.""Like a GPS coordinate for meaning. Words that mean similar things have similar coordinates, so 'king' and 'monarch' are neighbours on the map."
Rate limiting"We throttle requests above a threshold using a token bucket algorithm.""Like a nightclub bouncer with a clicker. After 100 people per hour the club is full — you wait outside until someone leaves."
Eventual consistency"Writes propagate asynchronously; reads may return stale data within the consistency window.""Like a bank balance on a Sunday — your app shows one number, but the official cleared balance might differ by a few pending transactions. They will match by Monday morning."
CI/CD pipeline"Automated build, test, and deployment orchestrated by a YAML-defined workflow.""A factory assembly line for software. Code goes in one end, gets checked at every station, and rolls out the other end as a deployed update — automatically."

Progressive disclosure

Never front-load all the detail. Give the audience the minimum they need to follow, then add layers only when needed or asked. A useful mental model: imagine you are briefing three different people — the CEO (30 seconds), the VP (3 minutes), and the lead engineer (30 minutes). Have all three versions ready, and start with the CEO version unless context says otherwise.

Watch for the curse of knowledge The more deeply you know something, the harder it becomes to remember not knowing it. Regularly explain your work to someone outside your team. Their confusion is not a sign that they are slow — it is data about your explanation.

Active Listening and Asking Good Questions

Most engineers are trained to think while the other person talks — forming a response, spotting the technical flaw, preparing a counterpoint. This is the opposite of active listening. Active listening is a discipline: be fully present, suspend judgement, reflect back what you heard, and ask questions to deepen your understanding rather than to confirm your preconceptions.

Fig 3 — The active listening cycle. Most engineers short-circuit from Attend straight to Respond. The full loop dramatically improves understanding and trust.

Asking good questions

Questions are the most powerful tool for surfacing requirements, building rapport, and discovering the real problem behind the stated problem. The quality of your questions determines the quality of your understanding.

Open questions (expand)

"What would success look like for you?"
"What have you tried so far?"
"What concerns do you have about this approach?"
"Can you walk me through what happens when it breaks?"

Clarifying questions (narrow)

"When you say 'slow', what latency are you seeing?"
"Is this happening for all users or a subset?"
"Which of these three things is most important to you?"
"What does 'done' look like here?"
The 2-second rule Before you respond, pause for two full seconds. It signals that you heard them (not just waiting for your turn), and it gives you time to formulate a better response. In video calls, count silently — the pause that feels awkward to you is barely noticeable to others.

Presentation Design Principles

Most engineer-built slides fail the same way: walls of text, diagrams copied from documentation, no visual hierarchy, no clear takeaway per slide. Good presentation design is not about making things pretty — it is about reducing cognitive load so the audience can focus on your argument, not on decoding your slide.

One idea per slide

The most important rule in presentation design. If you can state the single point of a slide in one sentence, you have it right. If you need two sentences, you probably have two slides. The title of each slide should be its conclusion, not its topic. "Latency" is a topic. "Our p99 latency is 4× over budget" is a conclusion — and a reason to pay attention.

Design principleDoDon't
Text densityOne key point per slide; 20-word maximum per bulletPaste in paragraphs from the design doc
Slide titlesAssertion: "Cache misses cause 60% of latency"Topic label: "Cache Performance"
Data chartsShow one metric, circle the insight, add a one-line annotationShow a dashboard screenshot with 12 metrics and no annotation
ColourUse 2-3 consistent colours with semantic meaning (red = bad, green = good)Use whatever PowerPoint defaults to
Font sizeMinimum 24pt body — if it's smaller, cut the text insteadShrink font to fit more content on one slide
Speaker notesFull sentences for anything you might forgetBlank notes forcing you to improvise every time

Visual hierarchy

The eye moves to the largest, brightest, or highest-contrast element first. Use this deliberately. The element on a slide that your audience should notice first should be the visually dominant element — not a logo or a background image.

1. One dominant element->2. Supporting context (smaller)->3. Source/detail (smallest)
The squint test Squint at your slide until it blurs. Whatever you can still make out is what the audience will focus on first. If it's not your main point, redesign.

Delivery Skills: Voice, Pace, and Body Language

Your slides are a prop. You are the presentation. Delivery — how you use your voice, body, and presence — accounts for more of your audience's experience than the content itself. The good news: delivery is a set of learnable, practicable skills, not an innate personality trait.

Fig 4 — The presentation arc. Engagement rises through the hook and context, peaks at the moment of tension ("the problem"), and resolves at the solution. The call to action lands when energy is still high.

Voice

Volume, pitch, and pace are your three primary levers. Most engineers default to speaking too fast and too quietly when nervous (adrenalin compresses both). Consciously slow down by 20% from your natural pace — it will feel very slow to you and normal to the audience. Project your voice as if speaking to the back row even in small rooms.

Volume

Project — don't shout. Fill the room. Speak to the person furthest from you. Dropping volume at the end of a sentence signals lack of confidence; hold it level.

Pace

120–150 words/min is comfortable for technical content. Slow to 90 wpm for critical points. Fast-talking signals nerves; use it deliberately for momentum-building transitions.

Pause

The pause is the most under-used tool in speaking. A 2–3 second pause after a key point lets the idea settle. It feels infinite to the speaker; it feels deliberate and confident to the audience.

Body language

Before you say a word, your body has already communicated your confidence level. Stand with feet shoulder-width apart — not locked together (looks rigid) or widely spread (looks aggressive). Hands at your sides is neutral and reads as confident; clasped in front is defensive; behind the back is aloof. Use open gestures to emphasise points, but avoid repetitive fidgeting (it signals anxiety).

Eye contact

In a room: pick one person, complete a full thought (a sentence), move to another. Do not scan back and forth — it reads as nervousness. In a video call: look at the camera lens, not at the faces on screen. Most people look at the video grid and appear to be looking down — camera eye contact is the single easiest upgrade for virtual presence.

Managing Nervousness: Reframe, Prepare, Breathe

Presentation anxiety is universal among competent people — it is a sign you care about the outcome. The goal is not to eliminate nervousness but to redirect it. Adrenalin is energy; the question is whether it collapses into panic or expands into performance.

Reframe

Nervousness and excitement are physiologically identical. The label is a choice. Internally say "I am excited" rather than "I am nervous." Studies show this simple switch measurably improves performance.

Prepare

Anxiety grows in the gap between "what might happen" and "what I'm ready for." Close the gap. Rehearse aloud (not in your head), time yourself, and prepare for the three most likely hard questions.

Breathe

Box breathing (4 counts in, 4 hold, 4 out, 4 hold) activates the parasympathetic nervous system within 90 seconds. Do 4 cycles backstage or in the minutes before a call. Slow your breath; your heart rate follows.
Confidence trick In the 2 minutes before presenting, stand in a power pose (arms open, taking up space) in a private space. Research is mixed on hormonal effects but consistent on subjective confidence — it works well enough to be worth 2 minutes.
FearUnhelpful thoughtReframe
Forgetting your place"Everyone will think I'm incompetent.""I can say 'let me check my notes' — it looks organised, not unprepared."
Tough question"What if I don't know the answer?""'Great question — I'll look into that and follow up' is a complete, professional answer."
Technical failure (demo crashes)"This is a disaster.""I prepared a fallback. This is a chance to show how I handle adversity — which is what engineers actually do every day."
Audience disengagement"They hate this.""They're processing. I'll ask a question to re-engage."

Running Effective Meetings and Standups

The average knowledge worker loses 15-20 hours a week to meetings that could have been an email, or meetings where nothing was decided. Engineers can distinguish themselves by being the person who consistently runs meetings that end on time with clear decisions and actions — regardless of their seniority.

The meeting charter

Every meeting should have four things defined before anyone joins: a purpose (why this meeting, not an email?), a clear agenda with timings, the pre-work required, and the decision/output the meeting exists to produce. Distribute this 24 hours in advance.

Purpose defined->Agenda + timings->Pre-work distributed->Meeting runs->Actions + owner + date

Stand-up excellence

The daily standup degrades into a status report for management when it should be a coordination mechanism for the team. The three-question format (What did you do? What will you do? Blockers?) works only if people answer briefly and blockers are addressed, not just listed.

ElementDoDon't
Duration15 minutes maximum; timebox each person to 90 secondsLet one person run 10 minutes of context while others zone out
BlockersState the blocker specifically; nominate who can unblock; take discussion offline"I'm blocked on some stuff" (vague) or deep-dive into the solution in the standup
PreparationKnow what you will say before the call startsRead through Jira tickets in real-time during the meeting
FocusSpeak to the team, not to the managerTreat it as a performance review for your manager's benefit
ActionsEvery blocker gets an owner and a time ("I'll Slack you by noon")Blockers are named and then forgotten
Decision meetings Every decision meeting should end with someone documenting: the decision made, the alternatives considered, and the reasoning. A 3-sentence decision log saves hours of re-litigation in the future.

Written Communication: Emails, Slack, and Pull Requests

Written communication leaves a permanent record and operates asynchronously — it cannot rely on body language, tone of voice, or immediate clarification. This makes clarity, brevity, and intentional tone more important in writing than in speech. The same principles apply whether you are writing a Slack message or a multi-page RFC.

The clarity-brevity-tone triad

Clarity

One idea per paragraph. Use active voice. Avoid hedge-stacking ("it seems like it might potentially be possible that…"). State your claim directly, then support it.

Brevity

After drafting, cut 30%. Most first drafts are 40% longer than they need to be. Every word that doesn't earn its place takes the reader's attention away from the words that do.

Tone

Written tone skews colder than intended. Read your message as if you received it from a stranger. Would it feel terse? Add a sentence of context. Would it feel passive-aggressive? Rewrite the framing.
ChannelLength normKey ruleAnti-pattern
Slack DM1–3 linesState the question/request immediately, not "hey, got a minute?"The bare "Hey" followed by waiting for a response
Slack channelParagraph maxThread replies; use @mentions sparingly; link docs rather than pasteNovel-length messages that nobody reads past line 2
Email3–5 paragraphsBLUF opening; action items in bold; one ask per emailBurying the ask in paragraph four after extensive context
Pull request descriptionStructured templateWhy this change (motivation), what changed (summary), how to test it, screenshots if UIPR title "fix stuff" with empty description
Design doc / RFCAs long as neededExecutive summary on page 1; decisions up front; alternatives considered sectionStream-of-consciousness exploration with no structure or recommendation
Show example: great vs. poor PR description

Poor PR description: "Fixed the bug with the cache."

Strong PR description:

Why: Cache entries for authenticated users were being evicted on every deployment because the cache key included the build hash. This caused a full cache cold-start on every deploy, adding ~400ms to authenticated page loads for the first minute post-deploy.

What changed: Removed the build hash from the cache key prefix. Added a separate cache-busting mechanism for schema changes (new `CACHE_SCHEMA_VERSION` env var).

Testing: Load tested with k6 — p95 latency is stable at 120ms across a simulated deployment. No regressions in the cache unit tests. Added a regression test for the key format.

Risk: Low. The cache schema version is set to v1 by default (same behaviour as before). No migration needed.

Giving Live Demos

A live demo is one of the highest-impact communication formats available to engineers — and one of the riskiest. Done well, it makes abstract concepts immediately real and builds enormous credibility. Done poorly, it destroys a narrative in minutes. The secret is that every great "live" demo is actually a highly rehearsed performance with multiple fallback layers.

Preparation: the demo checklist

Before the demo

Run the demo end-to-end at least three times, including once on the exact machine and network you will use.

Prepare a screen recording as a fallback if the live demo fails.

Set up a clean environment: close unrelated tabs, hide notifications, set your browser to a clean profile.

Seed realistic-looking test data — "Alice Smith" and "acme-corp" land better than "asdf" and "test123".

During the demo

Narrate what you are doing and why before you do it ("Now I'll click Save — watch how the validation runs…"). Do not let silence fill while things load.

Slow down. Engineers move through familiar UIs at full speed; your audience needs time to track.

Highlight the value, not the feature. "Notice it found the anomaly automatically" beats "this is the anomaly detection tab."

Handling failure gracefully

Something will go wrong. The question is not whether but when. The engineers who look most competent in demos are not the ones whose demo never fails — they are the ones who handle failure with composure and preparation.

Acknowledge calmly->Switch to fallback->Narrate what would have happened->Follow up with recording
The golden sentence "That's a great demonstration of exactly the kind of edge case we're hardening — let me switch to my recording and show you the full flow." Said calmly, this is barely a bump. Said with panic, it becomes the story everyone remembers.

Communication in AI and ML Projects

AI/ML projects introduce communication challenges that don't exist in traditional software: non-determinism, probabilistic outputs, evaluation metrics that non-engineers find opaque, and a cultural tendency to over-promise because the technology is genuinely impressive. Miscommunication about model capabilities is one of the top causes of failed AI deployments.

Setting expectations about model uncertainty

Stakeholders often experience AI as magic — it either works perfectly or it's broken. Part of your job as an engineer is to build an accurate mental model in their minds: AI outputs are probabilistic, not deterministic. The model is always "guessing," just with different confidence levels.

SituationWhat stakeholders often thinkHow to reframe it
Model makes an error"The AI is broken — it got one wrong.""Our model has a 94% accuracy rate. On a dataset of 10k records that means ~600 errors. We catch the high-stakes ones via the human review queue."
Model confidence score"0.95 means it's 95% sure, right?""It means the model assigns a 95% probability to that class given this input. But the model's calibration needs to be validated — a score of 0.95 may or may not correspond to 95% real-world accuracy."
LLM hallucination"It made something up — it lied to us.""LLMs generate the most statistically plausible next token. Without grounding (RAG, tool use, verification), they can generate confident-sounding but incorrect output. This is expected behaviour, not a bug."
Evaluation metrics"What does 0.87 F1 mean in English?""Out of every 100 cases it should flag, it catches 87. Of everything it flags, 87% are actually real cases. We've set the threshold to balance catching more real cases versus generating false alarms."
Never demo your best examples When demoing an AI feature, show the model's average performance — including realistic failure modes — before you show the highlight reel. Stakeholders who see only the best-case demo will calibrate incorrectly and be disappointed in production.

The AI project communication lifecycle

AI projects have a characteristic communication pattern: excitement in discovery, confusion in evaluation, frustration in hardening, and satisfaction in production. Navigate each phase explicitly with your stakeholders.

Discovery: define the problem, set baseline metrics->Prototype: communicate limits early->Evaluation: explain metrics in business terms->Production: set monitoring expectations

Practice Drills and Common Interview Questions

Communication improves through deliberate practice — not just through doing it more, but through focused exercises with feedback. These drills are organised by skill, and they work best with a practice partner or recording device.

1-minute BLUF drill

Pick any technical topic from your recent work. Explain it to a non-technical person in exactly 60 seconds, starting with the conclusion. Record yourself. Listen back for jargon, filler words, and buried punchlines.

Analogy workshop

Take three technical concepts you use weekly. Write two analogies for each — one for a child, one for a business executive. Test them on someone outside engineering. Iterate until both land cleanly.

Question paraphrase drill

In your next three meetings, before answering any question, paraphrase it back: "So you're asking about [X] — is that right?" Track how often your paraphrase is confirmed versus corrected.

PR description audit

Look at your last five PR descriptions. Score them: Does each have a Why, What, How to test? Could a new engineer understand the motivation from it alone? Rewrite the weakest one.

Meeting debrief

After any meeting you run, write down: Was there a clear agenda? Did it end with decisions and action owners? Was it the right format (could it have been async)? One improvement for next time.

Shadow record

Screen-record yourself giving a 5-minute technical explanation. Watch it back without sound first — what does your body language communicate? Then with sound — count your filler words ("um", "like", "you know").

Interview Q&A: Communication under the microscope

Communication questions appear in system design interviews, behavioural rounds, and cross-functional interviews. Here are the 10 most common, with strong answer frameworks.

Tell me about a time you had to explain a complex technical concept to a non-technical stakeholder.
Use SCR: Situation (the concept, the audience, the stakes), Complication (why the gap in understanding was causing a problem), Resolution (the analogy or framework you used, how it landed, and the outcome). Always end with the impact — a decision made, a misunderstanding resolved, a project unblocked.
Describe a time when a miscommunication caused a problem on your team. What did you learn?
Be specific and honest — interviewers can tell when this is a theoretical answer. Describe the actual miscommunication, the cost, what you did to resolve it, and the concrete change you made (a template, a new check-in, a different meeting format). The learning should be a repeatable behaviour, not a vague "I now communicate better."
How do you handle disagreement with a technical decision made by someone more senior than you?
Signal that you use data, not status, to resolve disagreements — but that you know when to escalate versus when to commit and move. A strong answer: "I write up my concern with the specific data that drives it and propose an alternative. If we disagree after that, I ask for an explicit decision so we're aligned on the direction. I then commit fully to the agreed path."
Walk me through how you would structure a design document for a new feature.
State that you lead with the executive summary (problem, proposed solution, key tradeoffs). Follow with context, requirements, design options with tradeoffs, the chosen approach with rationale, testing plan, and open questions. The goal is to get to a decision efficiently — not to document everything you considered.
How do you give difficult feedback to a colleague?
Use the SBI model: Situation (when/where it happened), Behaviour (the specific observable action, not the personality), Impact (what happened as a result). Deliver it directly and privately; pair it with a concrete alternative behaviour. Avoid sandwich feedback — it dilutes the message.
Describe your process for running an engineering design review.
Pre-read distributed 48h in advance (no cold reading). Meeting opens with a 5-minute author summary, then structured Q&A. Close with explicit decisions logged: what's approved, what needs revision, and who owns the open questions. Follow up async for minor feedback. The goal is a decision, not a brainstorm.
How do you communicate project status when things are going badly?
Early and specifically. The worst communication pattern is silence until a crisis. Strong answer: "I flag a risk as soon as I see it with a concrete estimate of impact and the options to address it. I separate what I know (current state), what I believe (projection), and what I'm uncertain about. I come with a recommendation, not just a problem."
How do you adapt your communication style for different audiences?
Lead with the outcome they care about. For executives: business impact, cost, risk. For PMs: user behaviour, scope, timeline. For engineers: architecture, tradeoffs, implementation. The underlying content is the same; the frame changes. Strong candidates give a specific example of adapting in real-time within a meeting.
Tell me about a presentation you gave that didn't go well. What would you do differently?
Be honest and reflective. The best answers show genuine learning: "I front-loaded too much context before stating the recommendation. Three minutes in, one executive interrupted to ask what I was proposing. Now I always state the recommendation in my first two sentences." Showing you learned more than showing you never fail.
How do you manage up — keeping your manager and stakeholders informed without over-communicating?
Establish a weekly written update: three bullets — progress (what moved), blockers (what's stuck, what you need), next week (what you're committing to). Keep it under 200 words. This gives stakeholders a reference point that reduces ad hoc check-ins. When something material changes (scope, timeline, risk), signal it proactively — do not wait for the weekly cadence.

30-Day Communication Practice Plan

Communication improves through small, consistent, deliberate practice — not through occasional all-day workshops. This plan is designed to fit alongside a normal engineering workload: each activity takes 5-15 minutes per day.

Compare it to a similar recording from day 1. The improvement should be audible and visible. Share it with a trusted colleague for feedback.

Long-term improvement strategies

The 30-day plan builds a foundation. Sustained improvement over a year looks like this:

Join a speaking group

Toastmasters or a similar group provides safe repetitions and structured feedback. Five short talks at Toastmasters deliver more improvement than two years of occasional presentations at work.

Study great technical communicators

Watch talks from communicators you admire (Andrej Karpathy's lectures, Bryan Cantrill's talks, TED talks with 10M+ views). Identify specific techniques — not just "they're good." What exactly are they doing?

Write publicly

A blog, a newsletter, or even LinkedIn posts force you to structure ideas for a public audience. The discipline of publishing what you know is one of the fastest ways to sharpen thinking and writing simultaneously.

Seek feedback deliberately

After a significant presentation, ask one specific question: "What was the one moment where you lost the thread, if any?" Specific feedback beats "it was great." Find people willing to tell you the truth.
The compound effect An engineer who practises communication for 15 minutes per day for a year accumulates over 90 hours of deliberate practice. That is the threshold researchers associate with measurable skill improvement in complex domains. Communication is no different — start small, be consistent, keep the bar rising.
