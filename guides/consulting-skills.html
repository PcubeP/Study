Why an Engineer Needs Consulting Skills

A Forward Deployed Engineer is paid twice: once for the software, and once for the judgement about what software to build. The second payment is larger, harder to replace, and almost never taught. An engineer who can write a clean service but cannot run a discovery session will spend a career building the wrong thing beautifully. The consulting skill set — structured problem solving, disciplined questioning, stakeholder navigation, scoping, and honest communication — is what converts raw engineering capability into outcomes a customer will pay to repeat.

What the FDE role actually is

The title varies — forward deployed engineer, solutions engineer, delivery engineer, field engineer, embedded engineer — but the shape is constant. You are an engineer placed inside a customer's organisation, with their data, their constraints, their politics and their deadlines, and you are expected to produce working software plus the understanding that makes the software correct. You sit between three worlds and translate between them continuously.

The FDE sits on a boundary. Most of the job is deciding what crosses it, in which direction, and in what words.

The value an FDE adds beyond code

Problem selection

Choosing which of eleven candidate problems to solve first is worth more than any implementation choice. A mediocre solution to the highest-value problem beats an elegant solution to the fourth-ranked one, every time, in every engagement.

Constraint discovery

Nobody writes down that the warehouse team cannot use a phone on the floor, or that finance locks the ledger on the 3rd working day. These facts determine whether your design is usable. Only sustained presence surfaces them.

Decision forcing

Enterprises are decision-starved, not information-starved. An FDE who can take a fuzzy debate and turn it into "three options, here's my recommendation, I need an answer by Thursday" moves a project further than a week of coding.

Credible estimates

Because you have seen the data and talked to the users, you can say "four weeks" and be believed. Sales cannot do this. Remote architects cannot do this. It is a structural advantage of being embedded.

Risk surfacing

Saying "the PDFs are scanned images, so OCR quality caps our accuracy around 85%" in week one is worth more than any feature. Risks found late become crises; risks found early become scope decisions.

Transferable capability

You leave. What remains is documentation, trained users, runbooks and a team that can extend the system. An FDE who leaves behind a system nobody can operate has failed, regardless of how well it works on the last day.

The consulting loop

Understand→ Frame→ Prioritise→ Scope→ Deliver→ Prove value→ Hand over

Every section of this guide is a stage of that loop. The loop runs at multiple timescales simultaneously: once over a nine-month engagement, once per two-week sprint, and once inside a single forty-minute meeting. Learning to recognise which stage you are in — and refusing to skip stages because you are impatient to code — is most of the discipline.

A self-diagnostic

  • Can you name the economic buyer of your current project, and the last thing they said to you directly?
  • Can you state, in one sentence with a number in it, what the business gains if you succeed?
  • Do you know who, specifically, would be annoyed if your project succeeded?
  • Is there a written document, agreed by the customer, that lists what you will and will not deliver?
  • If you disappeared tomorrow, could someone else operate what you have built from written material alone?
  • Has your sponsor heard bad news from you before they heard it from anyone else?

Six "no" answers is normal for a strong engineer new to the role. Six "yes" answers is what separates an FDE from a contractor with a laptop.

Structured Problem Solving

Consulting firms teach structured problem solving because it is the only reliable defence against two failure modes: boiling the ocean, and jumping to the first plausible cause. The toolkit is small — decompose exhaustively, hypothesise early, test cheaply, cut ruthlessly — but it has to be practised until it is reflexive, because the pressure in a customer room always pushes toward unstructured flailing.

MECE: mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive

A decomposition is MECE when every item belongs to exactly one branch and the branches together cover everything. It is a quality test for a list, not a philosophy. Non-MECE thinking is how you end up double-counting savings, missing an entire category of root cause, or arguing in circles because two people's categories overlap.

Not MECE

"Why are orders delayed? Because of the warehouse, because of bad data, because of the night shift, because of SAP." Warehouse and night shift overlap; SAP and bad data overlap. You cannot size any branch without double-counting.

MECE

"Order delay = time to enter + time to validate + time to pick + time to ship." Four disjoint spans that sum to the whole. Each can be measured independently and the parts add to the total.

The lazy cut"People, process, technology" is the decomposition engineers reach for when they have not thought. It is barely MECE (a bad process is usually enforced by technology and tolerated by people) and it produces generic findings. Prefer a cut whose branches can be measured in the customer's own data: minutes, rupees, tickets, orders, defects.

The issue tree

An issue tree takes a single question and decomposes it, MECE, level by level, until the leaves are things you can actually measure or test in days. The goal is not completeness for its own sake; it is to make the whole problem space visible so that you can prune confidently and say to a customer "we are deliberately ignoring these four branches, and here is why."

Worked example. A B2B distributor's customer-service team is drowning. The stated problem: "our agents take too long to answer customer emails." The sponsor wants a chatbot. Before agreeing to build a chatbot, decompose.

TEXT
QUESTION: Why does it take 26 hours (median) to answer a customer email?

Total response time = QUEUE TIME + HANDLE TIME + APPROVAL TIME + SEND DELAY
                      (all four measured from the mail server + CRM timestamps)

       1.2.2 Misrouted to wrong region   -> 6% of mails, re-queued once

       1.3.2 No priority signal from CRM

You walk out of week one with a measured tree showing that 73% of the delay is queueing, that a chatbot addresses at most 15%, and with a concrete recommendation that costs less and delivers more. That is the difference between an order-taker and a consultant. Crucially, you did not say "no" to the chatbot — you showed the arithmetic and let the sponsor choose.

Hypothesis-driven work

The slow way to solve a problem is to gather all the data, then analyse it, then conclude. The fast way is to form a specific, falsifiable hypothesis early and then design the cheapest possible test of it. Being wrong quickly is the mechanism; a hypothesis you cannot disprove is useless.

TEXT
HYPOTHESIS CARD

Hypothesis:   Off-hours arrival is the dominant driver of response latency.
Falsifiable?  Yes.
Prediction:   If true, >=60% of median queue time is accumulated between
              18:00 and 08:00 local.
Disproof:     If off-hours accounts for <30%, the hypothesis is dead and
              we move to branch 1.2.
Cheapest test: Export 14 days of message headers (received_at, first_open_at)
              from the mail server. 1 CSV, ~2 hours of work. No access
              requests needed beyond the mail admin.
Owner:        Me
By:           Thu 14:00
If confirmed: Price up (a) 24/5 follow-the-sun coverage via the Manila team,
              (b) auto-acknowledge + auto-triage bot.
If refuted:   Test routing hypothesis (1.2.1) using CRM assignment logs.

Rules that make this work in practice:

  1. Write the hypothesis before you look at the data. Otherwise you will find a pattern in anything and call it a finding. Writing it down also lets you be publicly wrong, which builds far more credibility than quiet rightness.
  2. State the number that would disprove you. "A lot" and "most" are not predictions. "At least 60%" is.
  3. Choose the test that is cheapest, not the one that is most rigorous. A two-hour CSV analysis that moves you from 50% to 85% confidence is worth more than a two-week study that reaches 95%. You are making a decision, not publishing.
  4. Timebox the test before you start it. "If I cannot get this data by Thursday, I will go and watch three agents work for an hour instead." Data access in enterprises fails often; always have the observational fallback.
  5. Kill hypotheses out loud. Telling the sponsor "I thought X, I tested it, I was wrong, here's what I now think" is one of the strongest trust-building moves available.

The 80/20 cut

Pareto is not a slogan, it is an instruction to go and count. In almost every enterprise dataset the distribution is brutally skewed, and the skew is where your scope lives.

Day-one answer and the hypothesis pyramid

Consultants write the answer on day one, knowing it is wrong, because an explicit wrong answer organises all subsequent work. The "day-one answer" is a one-page straw man: what we think the problem is, what we think the solution is, what the value is, and what we would have to believe for that to be true. Every piece of fieldwork then either supports or breaks a specific belief.

TEXT
DAY-ONE ANSWER (straw man) - Distributor CS engagement - v0.1 - Mon

SITUATION  CS team of 9 handles ~13,500 customer emails/month. Median
           response 26h vs a 4h commitment in their customer charter.
           Two key accounts have cited responsiveness in QBRs.

COMPLICATION Volume +14% YoY with flat headcount. Hiring is frozen until Q3.

ANSWER     We believe response time is a QUEUEING problem, not a DRAFTING
           problem, and that auto-triage plus off-hours acknowledgement
           cuts median response from 26h to under 6h within 8 weeks,
           without new headcount.

WHAT WE MUST BELIEVE FOR THAT TO BE TRUE
  B1  Most queue time is accumulated outside business hours.      [test: mail logs]
  B2  Intent can be classified from subject+body at >90% accuracy. [test: label 300]
  B3  Customers accept an automated acknowledgement.              [test: ask 2 accounts]
  B4  The mail platform permits API access for assignment.        [test: ask IT]
  B5  Agents will trust and use an assigned queue.                [test: shadow 3 agents]

KILL CRITERIA  If B1 is false AND B2 is false, this answer is wrong and we
               pivot to the drafting-assistant branch.

NEXT 5 DAYS    B1 Tue, B4 Tue (long lead), B2 Thu, B5 Wed, B3 Fri.
Say "straw man" out loudWhen you present a day-one answer, label it: "this is a straw man, I expect to be wrong in at least two places, and I would like you to attack it." People engage far more honestly with something presented as provisional, and you get the corrections in minute three instead of week six.

Drill: build a tree in ten minutes

  1. Write the question as a single sentence with a number in it. "Why is our median response time 26 hours when our charter promises 4?" Not "why is CS slow?"
  2. Pick a cut and justify it. Prefer additive cuts where branches sum to the measured whole.
  3. Split to three levels maximum. Depth beyond three is usually procrastination.
  4. Size every branch, even roughly. Guess if you have to, and mark guesses with a ?. An order-of-magnitude guess is enough to prune.
  5. Mark the two branches you will pursue and strike through the rest. Show the struck-through branches to the customer; it proves you considered them.
  6. Write one hypothesis card per pursued branch.

Framing the Problem

More engagements are destroyed by a badly framed problem than by any technical failure. A bad frame is invisible: everyone nods, work begins, and six weeks later two people discover they were solving different things. Framing is the discipline of writing the problem down so precisely that disagreement becomes impossible to hide, then pressure-testing the statement until it earns the work.

The problem statement canvas

Seven fields. If you cannot fill all seven, you do not yet understand the problem well enough to scope it — and that gap is itself the most useful finding of week one.

TEXT
PROBLEM STATEMENT CANVAS

1. WHO          Who has this problem, specifically? Named role, named team,
                how many of them.
2. WHAT         What is happening that should not be, or not happening that
                should? Observable behaviour, not interpretation.
3. SIZE         How big is it, in a unit the business already tracks?
                Money, hours, tickets, defects, delay, risk exposure.
4. SINCE/WHEN   When does it occur? Always, month-end, peak season, since a
                system change? Frequency and trigger.
5. EVIDENCE     How do we know? Which system, which report, which sample.
                If the only evidence is someone's opinion, say so.
6. IMPACT       What is the consequence of not fixing it? Who feels it, and
                what happens over the next 12 months if nothing changes.
7. CONSTRAINTS  What is off the table? Budget cap, no new headcount, must
                run on-prem, cannot touch SAP, regulatory deadline.

SUCCESS         What is true at the end that is not true now, measured how,
                by when, verified by whom.

Worked examples: bad versus good

Bad

"We need to improve our data quality."

Why it fails

No actor, no size, no evidence, no constraint, and no definition of improved. It licenses infinite work and permits no acceptance test. Every stakeholder will fill the blanks with their own project.

        price review because country codes, VAT ids and parent-company links
        are inconsistent between SAP and Salesforce.

        At 7 min/record that is 222 hours per quarter = 0.55 FTE.
        Separately, 3 mispriced contracts in FY24 traced to stale parent
        links; combined credit notes EUR 184,000.
WHEN    Every quarter, concentrated in the 9 working days before the review.
EVIDENCE SAP CDHDR change log export (Q1-Q4 FY24); Salesforce field history;
        interview notes with 4 analysts; the 3 credit-note cases from finance.
IMPACT  Analysts work weekends in review weeks; two resigned citing it.
        Pricing accuracy risk is now on the CFO's control register.

        (Oct). No new headcount. Must not require analysts to learn SQL.

        Reviewed by the Head of Commercial Ops.

A second pair, from a different domain:

Bad

"The warehouse system is slow and the team hates it."

Good

"Pickers at the Bhiwandi DC wait a median of 11 seconds after each scan before the next item appears, on a handheld that scans ~340 times per shift. The number does most of the workNotice that in both good statements, the moment a number appears the conversation changes character. "Data quality is bad" invites opinion. "222 hours per quarter, from the change log" invites verification and then action. If you can only improve a problem statement in one way, add a defensible number and say where it came from.

The "so what?" test

After every statement of fact, ask "so what?" until you reach something a budget-holder cares about. If you cannot get there in three steps, the finding is not worth presenting.

TEXT

SO WHAT? "Those require manual key-in by the AP team."
SO WHAT? "AP spends 1,400 hours/year keying and correcting them, and the
          error rate on keyed invoices is 2.3% vs 0.1% on XML."
SO WHAT? "That is 0.7 FTE plus roughly EUR 90k/yr of duplicate-payment
          recovery work, and it is why month-end close slips two days."
          <-- STOP. Month-end close slipping is a CFO-level pain.

SO WHAT? "There is a security risk."
SO WHAT? "...we should upgrade it."
          <-- Never reached a business consequence. Either find the real

Symptom, cause and the Five Whys done properly

The Five Whys is usually performed badly: a single chain of plausible-sounding assertions, none verified, terminating wherever the facilitator wanted to arrive. Done rigorously it has three rules — branch where there are multiple causes, verify each step with evidence before descending, and stop at the first cause you can actually act on rather than drilling to philosophy.

TEXT
FIVE WHYS - RIGOROUS FORM
Incident: 1,100 customers received a duplicate shipping notification on 3 Feb.

WHY 1  Why did customers get two notifications?
       -> The notification job ran twice for the same batch.
       EVIDENCE: job scheduler log shows two executions, 02:14 and 02:51.

WHY 2  Why did it run twice?
       -> The 02:14 run appeared to fail and an operator re-ran it manually.
       EVIDENCE: ops chat transcript 02:46-02:50; manual trigger in audit log.

WHY 3a Why did it appear to fail?            [BRANCH A - detection]
       -> The job exited non-zero after sending, during the cleanup step.
       EVIDENCE: exit code 1, stack trace in /var/log/notify/2024-02-03.log
       shows failure in temp-file deletion AFTER the send loop completed.

WHY 3b Why did a manual re-run duplicate?    [BRANCH B - idempotency]
       -> The job has no idempotency key and no record of what it sent.
       EVIDENCE: code review, notify_batch.py has no sent-ledger write.

WHY 4a Why did a cleanup failure exit non-zero for the whole job?
       -> Cleanup is inside the same try block as the send; any exception
          fails the job regardless of stage.
WHY 4b Why was there no sent-ledger?
       -> Written in 2019 as a one-off; never hardened. No design review.

WHY 5  Why was a one-off script running a customer-facing nightly process?
       -> No inventory of production jobs; no ownership assigned.
       EVIDENCE: no entry in the service catalogue; no on-call owner.

ACTIONABLE STOPPING POINTS (do not go further - "why is there no process
culture?" is not actionable this quarter):
  A. Separate cleanup from send; cleanup failure must not fail the job.   [1 day]
  B. Add a sent-ledger keyed on (batch_id, customer_id); skip duplicates. [3 days]
  C. Runbook entry: never re-run notify jobs manually; escalation path.   [2 hours]
  D. Add notify_batch to the service catalogue with a named owner.        [1 hour]

NOTE A and B are independent fixes to independent causes. A single-chain
Five Whys would have found only one of them.
Two abuses to refuseFirst, the chain that terminates at "human error" — that is where analysis stops being useful, because people will always eventually err; ask why the system permitted the error to have that consequence. Second, the chain that terminates at "we need more budget/headcount" on the first why, which is politics wearing analysis as a costume. In both cases say: "that may be true, but it is not actionable at our level. What is the nearest cause we can change?"

Reframing moves

When a problem statement feels stuck, these six moves usually unstick it. Each is a question you can ask out loud in a meeting.

MoveQuestion to askExample shift
Widen"What is this a symptom of?""Reduce report build time" → "Why does anyone need this report weekly?"
Narrow"Which 10% of cases cause most of the pain?""Automate claims" → "Automate motor claims under INR 25,000"
Invert"What would we do to make this much worse?"Exposes the levers: more handoffs, more approvals, less visibility
Change the actor"Who else could do this step?""Agents are slow" → "Why is the customer not doing this themselves?"
Change the unit"Per what? Per order, per hour, per rupee?"Cost per ticket hides that 3% of tickets are 40% of cost
Delete the step"What if this activity simply did not happen?"The best automation is elimination; ask before you optimise
The question that saves projects"If we do nothing at all, what happens?" Ask it in the first week, with a straight face. Sometimes the honest answer is "nothing much", and you have just saved everyone six months. More often the answer is specific and urgent — an audit, a contract renewal, a regulatory date, a competitor — and now you know the real driver, which is almost never what the kickoff deck said.

Discovery Interviews

Discovery interviewing is the highest-yield activity available to an FDE and the one engineers do worst. The failure mode is predictable: you arrive with a solution in mind, ask questions that confirm it, write down conclusions instead of words, and leave with a validated misunderstanding. Done well, an hour with the right person collapses weeks of speculation — but only if you treat it as fieldwork with a protocol, not as a chat.

Before the interview

  1. Know what you are trying to learn. Write the two or three things you must leave knowing. If you cannot write them, cancel and think first.
  2. Research the person. Role, tenure, what they are measured on, who they report to, what they said in any prior meeting. Five minutes on their LinkedIn and the org chart prevents the insult of asking what they do.
  3. Send a short pre-read. Two paragraphs: why you want to talk, what you will ask about, how long, and that there is no preparation needed. Reduces defensiveness enormously.
  4. Choose your opening and your closing. The first ninety seconds set whether this is an interrogation or a collaboration. The last two minutes are where you get referrals and artefacts.
  5. Arrange note-taking. Ideally a second person writes while you talk. If alone, ask permission to type, or record with explicit consent — and never record without it.
  6. Budget silence. Plan 40 minutes of questions for a 60-minute slot. The gold arrives in the gaps.

Silence, and other mechanics

Count to five

After they stop talking, say nothing for five seconds. It is excruciating and it works. Most people fill the gap with the qualification, the exception, or the honest version of what they just said.

Echo the last phrase

"...and then it goes to compliance." / "Compliance?" Repeating three words as a question is the lowest-effort way to go a level deeper without sounding like an interrogator.

Ask for the artefact

"Can you show me?" beats any amount of description. Screens, spreadsheets, printed forms, sticky notes on monitors — the physical evidence of the real process.

Timebox the rant

Let people vent for three minutes; it is both data and relationship-building. Then steer: "That's useful. Can you give me a specific example of when that bit you?"

Interview two, not six

Group interviews produce consensus, not truth. The quietest person in a six-person session knows the most and says the least. Prefer pairs or individuals.

Stop at 55 minutes

Ending early builds more goodwill than any amount of extra content, and the follow-up meeting you get is better than the fifteen minutes you took.

Observation and Process Mapping

People cannot accurately describe what they do. This is not dishonesty; it is how expertise works — once a task is automatic it stops being narratable, and the exceptions that consume most of the time feel too trivial to mention. Watching beats asking by a wide margin, and a process map built from observation rather than from a workshop whiteboard is the single most credible artefact you can put in front of a steering committee.

Shadowing: the protocol

  1. Get consent and explain the purpose clearly. "I'm not assessing you, I'm assessing the system you have to work inside." Say it to them and, critically, to their manager, so nobody fears a time-and-motion study aimed at headcount.
  2. Sit beside, slightly behind, and shut up. Watch at least one full cycle without interrupting. Write down questions rather than asking them.
  3. Ask in the gaps, not during the task. When they finish an item: "you paused there — what were you checking?"
  4. Record every switch. Application switches, tab switches, physical movements, phone calls, interruptions. The cost of a process is mostly in its seams.
  5. Observe across time, not just across people. Monday morning, month-end, the night shift, peak season. A Tuesday-afternoon-only view of a seasonal process is a lie.
  6. Ask for the next three to be narrated aloud. Think-aloud protocol surfaces decision rules — "I'd normally check the limit but this customer is fine" — that no documentation contains.
  7. Close by playing it back. "So the steps are... did I miss anything?" They will correct you immediately and precisely.

Stakeholder Analysis

Projects do not fail in code review, they fail in a corridor conversation you were not part of. Stakeholder work is not politics-as-cynicism; it is the recognition that a solution only creates value if a specific set of named humans choose to adopt, fund and defend it. Mapping those humans — their power, their interest, their private incentives — is an engineering activity with the same rigour as capacity planning.

The six roles you must identify by name

Economic buyer

Controls the money and can say yes alone. Often not in your meetings. If you cannot name them in week two, you have no project, you have an experiment someone is tolerating.

Champion

Wants this to succeed for their own reasons and will spend political capital. Your job is to make them look good and arm them with material they can forward without editing.

Blocker

Has a reason — often legitimate — to stop you. Security, architecture review, a rival initiative, a budget line they own. Blockers are usually solvable; being surprised by one rarely is.

End user

Must actually use the thing. Has almost no formal power and complete practical veto: quiet non-adoption kills more systems than any committee.

Gatekeeper

Controls access to data, environments, people or the sponsor's calendar. Low formal rank, enormous ability to add six weeks. Treat with disproportionate respect.

Technical authority

Must bless the design — enterprise architect, security officer, DPO. They cannot usually approve your project but they can absolutely stop it. Engage in week one, not at go-live.

The question that exposes a dead projectAsk your main contact: "If this works, whose budget pays for it next year?" If the answer is vague, hedged, or "we'd have to see", you are in a pilot that has no path to production and you should either get the economic buyer into a room within three weeks or manage your own expectations accordingly. A champion without a buyer is a pleasant six months leading nowhere.

Power/interest mapping

Position people by real power, not title. The CISO has high power and moderate interest — classic "keep satisfied": brief early, never surprise.

Reading the room

Signals you are winning

They start inviting you to meetings you did not ask for. People forward you things unprompted. Someone uses your terminology in a meeting you are not running. A stakeholder defends your project when you are not present. You get asked about something out of scope — that is trust.

Signals you are losing

Meetings get shorter or moved. Your champion stops replying within a day. Decisions are "still being discussed". Someone new appears in the governance chain. You are asked for a "business case refresh" in month four. You hear a project update from a third party.

When the signals turn bad, act immediately and directlyDo not wait and hope. Ask your champion, face to face: "I've noticed the steering meeting has moved twice and I'm not sure we still have momentum. What's changed that I'm not seeing?" People almost always tell you. The alternative — discovering in month five that the budget moved in month three — is unrecoverable.

RACI, Governance and Operating Cadence

Governance is unfashionable among engineers and indispensable on enterprise engagements, because it is the machinery that converts "someone should decide this" into a decision with a date and a name. Light governance done consistently beats heavy governance done performatively. The goal is a small number of forums, each with one job, each with a written output.

Responsible does the work. Accountable is the single name who answers for the outcome and can approve. Consulted is asked before. Informed is told after. One A per row, always; two A's means nobody is accountable.

Scoping and the Statement of Work

The statement of work is the document that decides whether month five is a collaboration or an argument. Engineers under-invest in it because it feels like paperwork; experienced FDEs treat it as the primary design artefact, because every ambiguity you leave in it will be resolved, later, in the direction that costs you. Write it as though a stranger will read it in six months to adjudicate a dispute — because that is exactly what happens.

Estimation techniques that survive contact

  1. Decompose to units of 0.5–3 days. Anything estimated at "two weeks" is an un-decomposed lump concealing unknowns. If you cannot break it down, that is a research task, not a build task — estimate the research.
  2. Three-point estimate per item. Optimistic, likely, pessimistic. Expected = (O + 4L + P) / 6. The spread is more informative than the midpoint: items where P is 4× O are your real risks.
  3. Apply a discovery multiplier honestly. On a first engagement with a new customer, multiply by 1.5–2.0 for access delays, environment surprises, and the three meetings per week you did not plan. This is not padding; it is the measured cost of working inside someone else's organisation.
  4. Estimate the non-code work explicitly. Meetings, documentation, demos, security review, test data chasing, training. On enterprise engagements this is 30–50% of total effort and engineers routinely cost it at zero.
  5. Record your estimates and review them. After each milestone compare estimate to actual per item. Within three engagements you will know your personal multiplier, which is worth more than any estimation framework.
  6. Never estimate in a meeting. "I'll come back to you tomorrow with a number" is always the right answer. Numbers given verbally under social pressure become commitments.

Running a Discovery Workshop

A workshop is an expensive instrument — twelve people for three hours is roughly a consultant-week of salary — and it is the wrong tool for gathering information you could get in interviews. Use a workshop for the three things only a group can do: build shared understanding, make collective trade-offs visible, and produce a prioritised list that everyone in the room has publicly endorsed. Anything else is a meeting with a flipchart.

Design principles

Decide the output first

Write the artefact you intend to leave with — "a ranked list of 8 opportunities with owners" — before designing a single exercise. Every activity either produces it or gets cut.

Interview before you workshop

Do your one-to-ones first. Walking in with a pre-built straw man draft of the findings makes the room far more productive than starting from a blank wall.

Divergent then convergent

Never mix. Generate broadly with no criticism, then filter against explicit criteria. Rooms that evaluate while generating produce four safe ideas.

Everyone writes

Silent individual generation before any discussion. It cancels seniority bias and triples idea count. This single technique is the biggest quality lever in facilitation.

Timebox visibly

Put a timer on the screen. It depersonalises the interruption — the clock is cutting you off, not the facilitator.

Capture as you go

Nothing goes on a sticky note that is not photographed and transcribed the same day. Workshop output that is not written up within 24 hours never gets written up.

Quantifying Business Value

Every engineering proposal competes for money against other proposals, and the one with a defensible number wins. The skill is not financial modelling — it is intellectual honesty under pressure to inflate. An FDE who presents a conservative, auditable, clearly-assumption-labelled value case earns credibility that compounds across every subsequent conversation; one who presents a heroic number gets it dismantled in public by a finance analyst.

Baselining — before you change anything

  1. Choose a metric that already exists in their systems. A metric you invent will be disputed at measurement time. A metric from their own BI report will not.
  2. Write the query or the method down, in full, and share it. Measuring "the same way" six months later is impossible unless the method is literally written out.
  3. Capture a long enough window to cover variation. One month minimum; a full quarter if there is month-end or seasonal effect. A baseline taken in a quiet fortnight will make your results look like a miracle, and nobody will believe it.
  4. Record confounders at baseline time. Headcount, volume, a system change, a pricing change, a public holiday pattern. In six months nobody will remember that three agents were on leave.
  5. Get the baseline agreed in writing by the person who will judge the result. This is the single step that is always skipped and always regretted.
  6. Keep the raw extract. Not the summary — the raw rows, in your own storage, with a date in the filename.

Prioritisation Frameworks

Prioritisation frameworks are not calculators that produce an answer; they are devices for making a group's implicit disagreements explicit so they can be argued about openly. The number a framework produces matters far less than the conversation it forces. Choose the lightest framework that will survive the political weight of the decision being made.

Impact versus effort

The default, and usually correct, choice. Two axes, four quadrants, ten minutes. Its weakness is that both axes are guesses, which is why it should be used for ordering within a workshop, not for a board-level investment case.

The chatbot the sponsor originally asked for sits bottom-right: high effort, low measured impact. Plotting it beside the alternatives makes that argument without you having to make it.

The "one thing by Friday" test

The most useful prioritisation question ever devised, and it fits on one line: "If we could only ship one thing, and it had to be live a week on Friday, which one?"

It works because it collapses three dimensions at once — value, feasibility and urgency — and because the artificial deadline prevents the comfortable answer. People who will happily rank ten items politely will suddenly reveal a strong preference when forced to pick one. Ask it after any formal scoring exercise. When the answer differs from the model's ranking, the gap is the most informative thing in the session, and you should spend five minutes on it.

   phase 2 which was the original plan.

 - Supplies the information they lacked (it is not the same mechanism)
 - Converts "yes/no" into "which trade-off", which is the real question
 - Hands the decision back explicitly, which is where it belongs
 - You have not said no. You have made the cost visible.

TEXT

  "Anita, I know you're stretched and this isn't your priority - that's

   issue, because that's what it is. Is there anything I should add about
   what you're carrying, so the ask lands on the right desk?"

  "Blocker for decision: we need 2 days of Finance IT effort to expose
   one field (block_reason) on the credit-check output.

   Requested 6 Feb through the standard ticket queue, currently 19 days
   old against a 10-day SLA. This is not a responsiveness problem on
   Anita's side - her queue has 40 open tickets and three P1s.

   go-live by a week. We are currently 2 weeks from losing the March date.

   Finance IT review it, which needs 2 hours of Anita's time rather

   codebase."

  outcome. And when it is unblocked, credit her publicly in the next
  status report, by name.

WHY IT WORKS
 - No surprise and no ambush; the relationship survives
 - Defends the person while escalating the problem, which costs you
   nothing and buys an ally
 - Offers a cheaper alternative (2 hours vs 2 days), which gives the
   executive an easy yes
 - A specific ask with a date; "please help with Finance IT" achieves
   nothing
The universal shape of a hard conversationFact, impact, options, recommendation, ask. Five beats, in that order, and it works for a slipped deadline, a rejected design, a budget overrun or a resignation. The two beats people omit are impact (so the listener invents their own, usually worse) and ask (so the conversation ends with nobody owning anything).

Expectation Management and Status Communication

Customers tolerate delay, cost and imperfection far better than they tolerate surprise. Almost every relationship breakdown an FDE experiences traces back to a moment when someone learned something late that you knew earlier. Status communication is therefore not reporting — it is the systematic prevention of surprise, and it is worth more of your attention than any single technical decision.

The no-surprises rule

Bad news travels first

The sponsor hears it from you, before the steering committee, before their boss, before the corridor. Same day you know it. This rule has no exceptions and no "I'll have better information tomorrow".

Early and small

A two-day slip reported immediately is a non-event. The same two days reported in week eight, as part of a four-week slip, is a crisis of confidence. Report the two days.

Never let them be embarrassed

Your champion presenting your project to their peers must have current information. The fastest way to lose a champion is to let them say something in a meeting that you knew was no longer true.

Delivering bad news

  1. Within hours, not days. The decay rate on bad news is brutal: the same fact, delivered a week later, reads as concealment rather than reporting.
  2. Verbally first, in writing second. Call, then send the written version within the hour so there is a record. Never deliver significant bad news by email alone.
  3. Lead with it. No warm-up, no three paragraphs of context. "I need to tell you that the March date has moved." Burying it is read as manipulation.
  4. One sentence of cause, no defence. Resist the urge to establish that it was not your fault. If some of it was your fault, say that plainly and once; it costs you nothing and buys enormous credibility.
  5. State the impact precisely. Unquantified bad news gets catastrophised. "Two weeks, no cost impact" is a much smaller event than "there's a problem with the timeline".
  6. Arrive with options. Never deliver a problem without at least two paths and a recommendation.
  7. Say what you have already done. "I've already booked the alternative slot" converts you from the messenger into the person handling it.
  8. Ask who else needs to know, and offer to help them tell those people. You are protecting your sponsor's position, not just reporting a fact.
The counter-intuitive resultEngineers who report problems early and precisely are trusted more than engineers whose projects never visibly have problems — because everyone knows every project has problems, so an unbroken run of green reports reads as either luck or concealment. Visible, well-handled bad news is a demonstration of competence. It is also the only reliable way to be given a harder engagement next time.

Handling Conflict and Difficult Stakeholders

Almost nobody is difficult without a reason, and the reason is almost never the one they state. Behind resistance there is usually a rational interest — a threatened budget, an unacknowledged workload, a previous failure they were blamed for, a genuine technical objection nobody has listened to. Diagnosis before response is the whole discipline. The engineer's instinct, which is to out-argue the objection, is the one response that reliably fails.

Diagnosis first

TEXT
THE DIAGNOSTIC QUESTIONS - ask yourself before you act

1. What does this person lose if I succeed?
   Budget, headcount, status, a system they own, a narrative they have
   been telling, a programme they are running, their indispensability.

2. What have they been burned by before?
   Ask explicitly: "has anything like this been tried here before?"
   The answer reframes most resistance as pattern-matching, not hostility.

3. Are they objecting to the WHAT or the HOW I ARRIVED?
   Very often the objection is procedural - they were not consulted - and
   the content is fine. This is cheap to fix and expensive to misdiagnose.

4. Is their objection actually correct?
   Sit with this one honestly for a full minute. Hostile stakeholders are
   right surprisingly often; they are just rude about it.

5. Do they have the power to stop me, or only to slow me?
   This determines whether you must convert them or merely route around
   them. Spending three weeks converting someone who cannot stop you is
   a waste; routing around someone who can is fatal.

6. What would a win look like for them?
   If you cannot answer this, you have no strategy, only an argument.

The resistant SME

TEXT
PRESENTATION
Knows the domain better than anyone. Answers questions in monosyllables.
Says "it's more complicated than that" without elaborating. Does not attend
sessions. Finds a fatal flaw in everything you propose, late.

LIKELY CAUSE
 - Their expertise IS their job security, and you are proposing to encode it
 - They have watched three consultants simplify their work and get it wrong
 - Nobody has asked their opinion before deciding, only after
 - They are genuinely right that it is more complicated

RESPONSE
 1. Make them the authority, visibly and in writing. Name them as process
    SME in the SOW. Say "this rule is Martin's, I just implemented it."
 2. Ask them to find the flaws, formally. "I need you to break this. What
    are the cases where this rule gives the wrong answer?" Being asked to
    criticise is the role they want and are good at.
 3. Show them everything FIRST, before any other stakeholder. The status
    of being the first reviewer is worth more than any argument.
 4. Never present a simplification of their work to management without
    them in the room.

WORDS THAT WORK
 "You've been doing this eleven years and I've been here three weeks.
  I'm going to get things wrong and I need you to tell me when I do -
  ideally before I show it to Priya rather than after."

 "I've written down the rule as I understood it. I'm fairly sure it's
  wrong in at least two places. Where?"

WHAT NOT TO DO
 Do not go around them to a more cooperative junior person. The SME will
 find out, will be right that you did it, and will stop cooperating
 permanently. Their knowledge is not replaceable.

TEXT
PRESENTATION
Enthusiastic at kickoff. Cancels two of the next three 1:1s. Replies to

wait weeks.

LIKELY CAUSE

 - The project was someone else's idea and they inherited it
 - They do not actually control the budget and are avoiding saying so
 - You are asking for too much of their time, or asking badly

RESPONSE
 1. Reduce the cost of engaging with you. Replace the 30-minute meeting
    with a 5-line email requiring a one-word answer. Make decisions
    binary and deadline them with a default.
 2. Use the default-decision mechanism explicitly (see below).
 3. Find the deputy. Ask directly: "when you're not available, who can
    make this call?" Get that in writing.
 4. Diagnose the real cause, directly and without accusation, in person.
 5. If there is genuinely no sponsor attention available, escalate the
    structural issue once, in writing, and then manage your own exposure.

TEXT
PRESENTATION
Access requests sit for weeks. "That's not how we do things here."
Architecture review raises new requirements each time. You hear "we weren't
consulted on this" in month three.

LIKELY CAUSE - usually one of these four, and they are all legitimate
 1. They were genuinely not consulted, and a business function hired you
    to route around them. They are correct to be annoyed.
 2. They will own support for whatever you build, and nobody asked them.
 3. They have real standards and have watched a dozen business-led
    projects dump unsupportable systems on them.
 4. Capacity. They are not hostile, they are drowning.

RESPONSE
 1. Go to them early, before you need anything, and ask for their
    standards. "What do I need to satisfy to get this through your

 2. Accept their standards wherever you possibly can. Each accepted

 3. Name their support model as a deliverable. "You'll own this from

 4. Give them a seat in the working group and credit in the status report.
 5. Never win an architecture argument by escalating to a business

    engagement in a queue.

The political land-grab

TEXT
PRESENTATION
A peer leader asserts that your work belongs under their programme, their
CoE, their platform, their governance. Appears in your steering committee
uninvited. Proposes a "merge" that would make you a workstream.

LIKELY CAUSE
 Their programme needs visible wins, or your success makes their
 investment look slow, or they genuinely have a mandate you were not told
 about.

RESPONSE - in order of preference
 1. GIVE AWAY THE CREDIT, KEEP THE DELIVERY. This is almost always the
    right answer and engineers hate it. Offer co-branding, offer that the
    component becomes a reusable asset in their catalogue, offer them the
    steering committee slot to present it. You lose nothing you actually
    need.
 2. Find the genuine overlap and formalise it. If they have a rules
    engine, use it. Dependency is cheaper than war.
 3. Clarify the mandate upward, once, neutrally: "Rachel, Sanjay and I
    both think this sits in our area. Rather than negotiating it between
    us, can you tell us where you want it to sit?" Then accept the answer.
 4. Only if 1-3 fail: document the duplication and its cost, present it
    factually, and let the organisation decide. Never frame it as a
    personal dispute.

WHAT NEVER WORKS
 Competing on technical merit. Being right about architecture has no
 bearing on an organisational boundary dispute. You will produce an
 excellent comparison document that nobody reads and lose anyway.

TEXT
PRESENTATION

    every delivery review into a negotiation.
 2. Maintain a visible "not in this product, but noted" list and review

    they can see their item written down and dated.
 3. Be honest fast about hard noes. "We will not be building a CRM. If
    that is what you need, I can tell you who does it well." Saying this
    in month one costs you nothing; saying it in month eight after
    letting them hope is a breach of trust.
 4. Find the version of their request that IS in scope. "We can't do
    forecasting, but we can expose the exception data so your existing

Influence Without Authority

An FDE has no line authority over anyone whose cooperation they need. Everything you achieve inside a customer organisation is achieved through influence, and influence is not charisma — it is an accumulation of mundane, repeatable behaviours: being reliably useful, being easy to say yes to, pre-wiring decisions so that meetings ratify rather than debate, and making other people's success the mechanism of your own.

Credibility is built from small, kept promises

Competence

Demonstrated, not claimed. One working thing beats any credential. The fastest credibility move available to an FDE is to solve a small, visible, annoying problem in week one that nobody asked you to solve.

Reliability

You do what you said, when you said. This is 80% of trust and it is entirely within your control. Track every commitment you make in a list, including the trivial ones made verbally in corridors.

Intimacy

People tell you things. Earned by discretion: never repeat a confidence, never be the source of gossip, never use something told to you privately in a public argument.

Low self-orientation

The strongest trust multiplier. Visibly optimising for their outcome rather than your invoice. Telling a customer not to buy something is the purest form of this.

Specificity

"About 48%, measured on 300 cases from January" is credible. "Roughly half" is not. Precision signals that you have actually done the work.

Admitting limits

"I don't know, I'll find out by Tuesday" raises your credibility. Confident guessing destroys it the first time you are caught, and you will be caught.

Reciprocity, used honestly

  • Write the SQL that someone has been asking IT for since November.
  • Send a relevant article, a tool, or an introduction with no ask attached.
  • Credit someone by name in a status report that their manager reads.
  • Take the note-taking job in a meeting you did not call.
  • Explain something technical to a non-technical person patiently, when it is not your job.

No significant decision should be made for the first time in the meeting where it is formally taken. If a steering committee is the first time a stakeholder hears your recommendation, you have given them a public stage on which to object, and public objections harden into positions.

Coalition building

  1. Start with the person who already agrees. Not the hardest to convince. Early, visible support makes later conversations easier because people assess positions socially, not just logically.
  2. Make the coalition visible. "Thomas has reviewed this and is comfortable; Martin has validated the rules" removes the fear of being the first to commit.
  3. Give each member a role. Reviewer, presenter, pilot user, data provider. People defend what they have contributed to far more strongly than what they merely approved.
  4. Let others present. When your champion presents your work as theirs, you have won. Resist the urge to be the one holding the clicker.
  5. Keep the coalition informed even when nothing is happening. A two-line update in a quiet month keeps people attached.

Closing an Engagement

The last two weeks determine what the engagement is remembered as. An FDE who builds something excellent and leaves it undocumented, unadopted and unowned has produced a liability for the customer and a reference they will not give. Closing well is a distinct skill with its own artefacts, and it must be planned from week one — not improvised when the budget runs out.

Train the trainer

  1. Pick the trainer early — ideally in week three — and make it someone respected by peers rather than the most technical person.
  2. They shadow you. They sit in on design sessions and build sessions so they understand why, not only what.
  3. You deliver session one; they observe and take notes.
  4. They deliver session two; you sit at the back and say nothing. This is the hard part and the whole point.
  5. They answer the questions. When an attendee looks at you, look at the trainer. If they get it wrong, correct it afterwards in private, never in the room.
  6. Hand over the materials in editable form — not a PDF — with a note on what to update when things change.
  7. Be publicly available for two weeks and then stop. Announce the end date so the transition is explicit rather than a slow fade.

Consulting Ethics and Boundaries

An FDE sits inside a customer's organisation with access to their data, their failures and their internal politics, while being paid by someone else. That position creates obligations that no contract fully specifies. The reputational asset you are building is not technical — it is the belief that your assessment is honest even when honesty costs you revenue. It takes years to build and one engagement to destroy.

Honest assessment

The pressure

Your pipeline needs a phase 2. The sponsor wants to hear that the pilot succeeded. Your own account team has a number. Every force in the system pushes toward a more favourable reading of ambiguous results.

The practice

Report the measurement you agreed, by the method you agreed, before you look at whether you like it. Pre-register the method in writing. If the result is poor, present it with the same structure and confidence as a good result.

TEXT

hours; we got 9.4.

Confidentiality in practice

  • Customer data never leaves agreed systems. Not to your laptop for convenience, not into a personal cloud drive, not into a third-party AI tool that is not covered by the agreement.
  • Never name a customer to another customer without written permission, including in casual conversation and including in interviews.
  • Anonymise examples properly before reuse. "A European steel distributor with nine CS agents" is not anonymous to anyone in that industry.
  • Personal information told to you in confidence — a resignation, an illness, a dispute — is never repeated, never used as leverage, and never appears in a note.
  • Screen-share one window, never your desktop. Close your stakeholder register, your other customers' folders and your email first, every time.
  • Assume anything you write in their systems may be read by anyone in their organisation, including the person it is about.
  • When you leave, delete what you agreed to delete, and confirm in writing that you have.

Interview Questions

Forward-deployed and solutions-engineering interviews probe judgement under ambiguity far more than algorithms. The strongest answers are specific, show a structure, include a number, admit a mistake, and end with what you would do differently. Vague process descriptions fail; worked examples pass.

A customer tells you in the kickoff meeting exactly what they want built. What do you do?
I take the request seriously as a signal, but I treat it as a hypothesis about the solution rather than a statement of the problem, because what a customer asks for is usually the solution they could imagine with the information they had. So I would say "that's helpful, and before I commit engineering time I want to understand the problem well enough to confirm that's the best use of the budget" — which almost nobody objects to, because it is obviously in their interest. Then I run a short discovery: interview the people who do the work rather than only the people who manage it, get a measured baseline from their own systems, and build an issue tree so I can size each branch. In one case a customer asked for a chatbot to speed up customer-service email; two weeks of measurement showed 19 of the 26-hour response time was queueing before an agent ever opened the mail, which a chatbot does not touch. I did not refuse to build it — I showed the arithmetic and let the sponsor choose, and they chose routing and assignment rules, which cost a fifth as much. The key is that I never said no; I made the cost of each option visible and handed the decision back. If discovery confirms the original request, that is also a good outcome: it costs two weeks and buys a confident, defensible scope.
How do you handle a stakeholder who is actively obstructing your project?
I start from the assumption that they have a rational interest I have not understood yet, because people are rarely obstructive for sport. So the first move is diagnostic: what do they lose if I succeed, have they been burned by something similar before, are they objecting to the substance or to the fact that they were not consulted, and — the one people skip — are they actually right? On one engagement the automation CoE lead kept reframing our work as duplicating his programme. The real interest was that his programme needed visible wins and ours would land first. Competing on technical merit would have been irrelevant to that, so I gave away the credit and kept the delivery: our rule shipped as a reusable component in his catalogue, with his team's branding, and he presented it at steering. It cost me nothing I needed and converted a blocker into a sponsor. The two things I avoid are escalating silently over someone — which permanently ends cooperation — and winning an argument publicly, because public defeats harden into permanent positions. The exception is when someone is factually wrong in a way that will cause real harm; then I say it once, calmly, in writing, and if I am overruled I record that it was overruled and by whom.
Your project is going to be three weeks late. Walk me through what you do.
I tell the sponsor the day I know, by phone, before anyone else hears it and before I have a complete recovery plan, because the decay rate on bad news is brutal — the same fact a week later reads as concealment. The structure I use is fact, impact, options, recommendation, ask. Fact: the security review slot moved from the 25th to the 11th, which is a two-week shift on a gate everything else sits behind, and I should have submitted a week earlier than I did. Impact: stated precisely, because unquantified bad news gets catastrophised — three weeks, no cost impact, go-live moves from 28 March to 11 April. Options: full scope on 11 April; or a reduced go-live on 1 April with the two highest-value components and the rest following; or hold the original date by cutting the composite view entirely, decided this week. Recommendation: the second, because it protects the four-week measurement window they need before a September renewal conversation. Ask: what do you want to do, and who else needs to hear this from you before Thursday's steering, so my sponsor is never surprised in public. Then I hit the new date, because a missed recovery date costs far more than the original slip. I would also check whether anything in the SOW's assumptions section covers this, because a documented assumption failure turns an apology into a contractual formality.
How do you estimate work in an environment you have never seen before?
I decompose into items of half a day to three days, because anything estimated as "two weeks" is an un-decomposed lump concealing unknowns — and if I genuinely cannot break something down, I treat it as a research task and estimate the research instead of the build. Each item gets a three-point estimate and I pay more attention to the spread than the midpoint: the items where pessimistic is four times optimistic are where the budget actually gets consumed, and in enterprises those are almost always data access, security review and anything that depends on another team's queue. Then I explicitly estimate the non-code work — meetings, documentation, demos, chasing test data, training — which is 30 to 50 per cent of effort on an enterprise engagement and which engineers routinely cost at zero. Finally I apply a new-customer multiplier, typically 1.35 to 2.0 on a first engagement, which is not padding but the measured cost of working inside an organisation whose access processes and change windows I do not yet know. I never give a number verbally in a meeting; "I'll come back to you tomorrow" is always correct, because numbers given under social pressure become commitments. And I record estimate versus actual per item after each milestone, because within three engagements that gives me my own personal multiplier, which is worth more than any framework.
A customer asks for something you know is a bad idea, and they are senior. How do you respond?
I never contradict a senior person in public, and I never agree to something I will later have to unpick, so the move is to convert an opinion dispute into a measurable question and buy forty-eight hours. First I reframe to intent: "let me make sure I have the goal — you want fewer customers needing to contact us at all, is that right?" That separates the objective, which is usually sound, from the solution, which may not be. Then I offer one number if I have one — "of the 26 hours a customer waits, 19 is queueing and four is agent handling; a chatbot works on the four" — and propose coming back with two sized options and the expected effect of each, so the decision rests on numbers rather than on my opinion versus theirs. I always include their option in that comparison, properly costed and honestly argued, and I say explicitly that if its numbers are better I will build it. That gives a senior person a way to change their mind without losing face: they are not retreating from a position, they are choosing between two options I brought them. Occasionally the analysis shows they were right, which is a good outcome for everyone and does a lot for my credibility the next time I disagree.
How do you quantify the business value of something that is hard to measure?
I separate what I can defend from what I cannot, and I refuse to invent numbers for the second category, because a value case that gets dismantled in public by a finance analyst destroys your credibility for every future number you present. Concretely: I build the hard case conservatively — hours returned at the company's own fully-loaded rate, run costs subtracted, adoption ramped rather than assumed at 100 per cent from day one — and I present the soft benefits as counted facts without a currency attached. On one engagement the hard saving was €23,400 a year against a €101,000 investment, which is a 5.7-year payback and does not pass any reasonable hurdle. I said that out loud before finance found it. Then I presented the case that actually justified the spend: 1,900 fewer delayed deliveries a year, elimination of a single-person dependency with no cover, and two key accounts worth €2.1 million in renewal value that had specifically cited responsiveness. I told the COO I could not price those but she could. I also always baseline before changing anything, using a metric that already exists in their systems, with the query written down and signed off by the person who will judge the result — because measuring "the same way" six months later is impossible otherwise, and an unbaselined success is an argument waiting to happen.
Tell me about a time you got a requirement badly wrong.
On an order-management engagement I built an address-matching rule from the documented process and a workshop, and it was wrong in two ways that only surfaced when the domain expert reviewed it in week six: Austrian postcodes have a different structure that broke the normalisation, and "c/o" lines were being treated as part of the street address, which produced false mismatches on about 4 per cent of cases. The root cause was mine — I had interviewed four managers and no practitioners, so I knew the process as documented, which is to say I knew fiction. What I changed, immediately and permanently, was that I now shadow before I design: I sit next to someone doing the actual work for at least a full cycle, I log every application switch and every pause, and I compare observed timings against reported ones, because the gap between them is usually the most valuable number of the week. I also made the SME the formal reviewer of every rule before anyone else saw it, framed as "I need you to break this" — which gave him the role he wanted, surfaced the remaining errors early, and turned a sceptic into the person who co-presented the solution to his own team. The cost of that mistake was about four days of rework. Caught in production it would have been a credibility event.
How do you manage scope creep without damaging the relationship?
I make the cost of every addition visible at the moment it is requested and then hand the decision back, because the damage comes from unilateral refusal, not from honest trade-offs. The mechanism is a change request with three options, an assessment and a recommendation — never a single option, which reads as a bill. When a sponsor asked to add the Austrian subsidiary mid-phase, the options were: include it fully for six extra days and an eleven-day slip; defer it to phase 2 as planned; or build only the reason-code mapping now for two days with no schedule impact, which turns phase 2 for Austria from a six-day project into a three-day deployment. I recommended the third, and finding that cheaper middle path is the part that makes you valuable rather than obstructive. The other half of the defence is the out-of-scope section of the SOW, which I write deliberately long — every line there is an argument I will not have in month five — and when a customer asks why it is so long I tell them honestly: so neither of us is surprised, and if any of it should be in scope, now is the cheapest moment to add it. I also log the small asks and show the running total in status, because creep is rarely one dramatic demand; it is forty half-day favours.
You have been dropped into a customer site with a vague brief and no documentation. What do you do in week one?
Week one is about three things: orientation, a measured baseline and visible output. On day one I find out who the economic buyer is — not the person who booked my flight, the person whose budget pays for this next year — and I ask my main contact directly, because if that answer is vague I am in a pilot with no path to production and I need to know that in week one rather than month five. I spend the first days interviewing across layers, deliberately including practitioners rather than only managers, and I shadow at least one person doing the real work for a full cycle. In parallel I start the long-lead items immediately: data access requests, VPN, security review submission, and anything that goes through someone else's ticket queue, because those are almost always the critical path and they do not care how urgent my project is. By the end of the week I write a one-page day-one answer — a straw man of what I think the problem is, what I think the solution is, and what we would have to believe for that to be true, with a test for each belief — and I present it labelled as a straw man and ask people to attack it. I also try to ship one small, visible, useful thing nobody asked for, like fixing a broken report, because demonstrated competence in week one buys more cooperation than any amount of credentials.
How do you get a decision out of an organisation that will not decide?
Three levers, in order. First, find out who actually decides, by asking the question literally — "who makes this call?" — because a surprising amount of enterprise paralysis is simply that nobody knows, and the question has never been asked out loud. Second, shrink the decision until it is reversible, because most deferral is risk aversion about irreversibility rather than disagreement about substance: "approve the programme" is a career risk, "approve a four-week pilot with nine users that we can switch off in ten minutes" is not, and once the small thing is working the large decision gets made retroactively by ratifying reality. Third, use default decisions with a deadline, agreed as a working practice up front: a five-line email with two options, a recommendation, and "unless you tell me otherwise by Friday I will proceed with A and record it as a default decision." The default must always be the safer and more reversible option, or the mechanism is manipulation rather than momentum. Underneath all of that is pre-wiring: no significant decision should be made for the first time in the meeting where it is formally taken. I spend about two hours before a steering committee walking the recommendation past the champion, the likely objector and the technical authority, asking each "what would make you say no to this?" — which surfaces in private the objection that would otherwise be raised in public.
What makes a good handover, and how do you know it worked?
The test of a handover is not whether a document exists; it is whether a named person who was not on the project can perform real scenarios using only the written material. So I run it as an observed test: I pick three realistic failure modes, I sit in the room while their operator works through the runbooks, and I say nothing. Everywhere they hesitate is a gap, and I fix those and repeat before I call the handover accepted — that acceptance criterion goes in the SOW, phrased as their person succeeding rather than my document existing. The pack itself covers architecture, environments, a credentials inventory with what breaks when a named person leaves, runbooks per failure mode, monitoring and who alerts route to, known issues and accepted limitations, the deferred-debt register with estimates, data lineage and PII locations, the support model with hours and escalation, and the decision log. I also plan adoption from week two rather than week twenty, because a system nobody uses is a handover failure regardless of documentation: pilot with volunteers rather than big-bang, have the customer's own respected person deliver the second training session while I sit at the back silently, and watch for the shadow spreadsheet that reveals an unhandled edge case nobody wanted to mention. The best ending is their team demoing the system to another part of the business, correctly, without me present.
When would you tell a customer not to buy from you?
When the value case does not work and I cannot honestly make it work; when the real blocker is organisational and software will not fix it; when they are buying software to avoid a decision somebody is reluctant to make; when I cannot staff it properly, because an under-resourced engagement fails with my name on it; or when another vendor genuinely fits better. The way I say it is factual rather than noble: "I don't think you should spend money on this yet. Three of the four causes of your 26-hour response time are decisions — coverage hours, who owns the shared inbox, and whether supervisors can approve credit notes — and those cost nothing. Fix them, re-measure, and if there's still an engineering problem underneath I'd be glad to come back." Roughly half those customers buy something later with a clearer problem and a bigger budget; the other half become people who take my call for the rest of my career. Both outcomes beat taking money to build around a free decision, which produces a project that cannot succeed and a reference I would not want. The same principle governs declaring conflicts of interest unprompted — if my employer earns margin on a product I am recommending, the customer hears that from me before the recommendation, not from someone else afterwards.

Drills and a 30-Day Practice Plan

Consulting skills are motor skills. Reading about laddering does nothing; asking "what does that mean concretely?" fifty times in real conversations changes how you listen permanently. The drills below are designed to be run inside whatever work you already have — no customer engagement required — and each produces an artefact you can review.

Weekly drills (45–90 minutes)

  1. Build an issue tree for any problem in your own work. Three levels, MECE, size every branch even if you guess, strike through the branches you are not pursuing. Then show it to someone and ask where it is wrong.
  2. Write a problem statement canvas for something you are currently building. All seven fields. The fields you cannot fill are your week's research list.
  3. Run one real discovery interview with a colleague about a process you do not understand. Use the question bank. Write the full write-up template, including the "my inferences" section, and send it to them for correction.
  4. Shadow someone for an hour using the observation log format. Compare their reported task time to your measured time and note the ratio.
  5. Draft a weekly status email for your current work even if nobody asked for one. Include a RAG with an explicit trigger and exit condition, and one decision request above the fold.
  6. Write a three-option change request for the last scope change you absorbed silently.
  7. Pre-wire one decision. Before your next team decision, speak to three people individually and ask "what would make you say no to this?"

The compounding skillOf everything in this guide, the habit with the highest return per unit of effort is the post-meeting summary email: what I heard, what we decided, what I will do, what I need from you, by when. It takes six minutes. It creates the written record, surfaces misunderstandings while they are still cheap, makes you the author of the agreed version of events, and — because almost nobody does it consistently — marks you within a month as the person in the room who can be relied upon. Start there, today, and let the rest follow.
