What Analysis Is Actually For

Most people believe stock analysis is about predicting what a share price will do. It is not. Analysis is about estimating the range of cash a business can generate over its remaining life, assigning rough probabilities to the outcomes in that range, and then comparing that estimate against the price the market is asking. You will be wrong constantly. The discipline is in being wrong in ways that are survivable, and in knowing which of your assumptions broke.

This is financial education, not financial adviceNothing in this guide is a recommendation to buy or sell any security. Every company used for worked financial examples is hypothetical and invented. Where a real listed company is named, it is only as a neutral illustration of a structural point, never as a suggestion to invest. Past performance does not predict future returns. Equity investing puts your capital at risk and you can lose all of it — individual listed Indian companies have gone to zero, and broad Indian indices have fallen more than 50% from peak to trough within living memory. For advice specific to your circumstances, consult a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser (RIA) and verify their registration number on the SEBI intermediary portal before paying for anything.
Every rate, rule, threshold and accounting standard: as at FY 2025-26 / late 2026Ind AS requirements, SEBI LODR disclosure obligations, tax rates, the corporate tax rate used in the worked examples, G-Sec yields, equity risk premium estimates and disclosure formats all change. Verify before relying on any number here at sebi.gov.in, mca.gov.in, incometax.gov.in, rbi.org.in, nseindia.com and bseindia.com. Treat every figure in this guide as illustrative of a method, not as a current quotation.

The only equation that matters

Everything in fundamental analysis — every ratio, every margin, every screening filter — exists to help you populate the terms of one equation.

You are estimating a distribution, not a point

The single most common beginner error is to produce one number — "this company will earn ₹450 crore next year, therefore the share is worth ₹820" — and then defend it. A professional estimate looks nothing like that. It looks like a distribution with explicit drivers.

Why the "disaster" row exists on every thesisPermanent capital loss and temporary price decline are entirely different risks and require entirely different responses. A 40% drawdown in a sound business with net cash is an opportunity. A 40% drawdown in a leveraged business whose lenders are getting nervous is the first instalment of a 95% loss. Section 11 of this guide is about telling the two apart before the drawdown.

What you are competing against

Before investing a single rupee of research effort, be clear about who is on the other side of your trade. In an Indian large-cap, your counterparty is frequently a well-staffed institutional desk with a market terminal, channel checks, paid expert networks, a direct line to the company's investor relations team, and twenty years in the sector. You will not out-research them on next quarter's revenue.

Where you cannot win

Short-horizon earnings prediction, speed of reaction to news, access to management, breadth of coverage, cost of execution. Accept this permanently.

Where you can win

Time horizon. A fund manager judged quarterly cannot hold a stock through three flat years. You can. This is the single largest structural edge available to an individual.

And here

Opportunity set. You can own a ₹900 crore company; a ₹50,000 crore fund cannot take a meaningful position in one without moving the price. Analyst coverage thins dramatically below ₹5,000 crore market capitalisation.

And here

No mandate. You may hold 60% cash for two years. No professional can. You are never forced to be invested.

And here

No career risk. Being unconventional and wrong ends careers. It does not end yours. This permits genuinely contrarian positions.

The catch

Every one of these edges is behavioural. They only exist if you actually use them. Many individual investors trade more frequently than professionals, which converts every structural advantage into a disadvantage.

How to read an annual report in the right order

Reading a 300-page annual report front to back is the slowest possible route. The first sixty pages are marketing. Read in this order instead.

  1. Auditor's ReportStart at the back. Look for the opinion type (unmodified, qualified, adverse or disclaimer), the Key Audit Matters — these are the things the auditor found hardest, which is a free list of where the accounting risk sits — and the CARO 2020 annexure, which asks pointed questions about defaults, undisclosed income, fund diversion and whistle-blower complaints.
  2. Notes: related-party transactionsWho is the company trading with, and are any of them owned by the promoter family? Quantify as a percentage of revenue and of purchases. A rising trend here is one of the strongest single red flags in Indian small- and mid-caps.
  3. Notes: contingent liabilities and commitmentsTax disputes, guarantees given for subsidiaries or group companies, pending litigation, capital commitments. These are real obligations that never appear on the face of the balance sheet.
  4. Cash flow statementBefore the P&L. Ten years of CFO compared against ten years of PAT answers the most important question about the accounts in one minute.
  5. Balance sheet and P&L with their notesNever read a line item without its note. "Other expenses" of ₹456 crore is meaningless; the note breaking it into power and fuel, freight, job work, CSR, professional fees and "miscellaneous" is not.
  6. Management Discussion and AnalysisMandatory under LODR Schedule V. Mediocre companies fill it with industry boilerplate copied from a consultancy report. Good ones explain variances. Read last year's MD&A and check what was promised against what happened.
  7. Directors' Report and corporate governance reportBoard composition, independent directors and their other directorships, attendance, audit committee, remuneration, and any resignation together with its stated reason.
  8. Segment reporting noteWhere revenue and — crucially — segment results and capital employed actually sit. A company with one profitable segment subsidising two loss-making ones looks entirely different once disaggregated.
  9. Chairman's letter, finallyRead it last, after you know the numbers, so that you can judge the honesty of the framing rather than be anchored by it. The test: does it discuss what went wrong, in specifics, with numbers?
Read ten years at once, not oneA single annual report tells you almost nothing. Download ten, open them side by side, and extract one line of data per year into a spreadsheet. Trends, inflections and quietly abandoned promises are only visible in the series. The mechanical act of typing ten years of numbers by hand — rather than importing them — is also the fastest way to learn what the line items actually mean.

The transcript is more useful than the audio, because you can search it. A concall has a predictable structure and the information is unevenly distributed across it.

The specific things to look for in the Q&A

  • Repeated questions. If three different analysts circle the same issue, management has not answered it. Note the issue.
  • Answer substitution. Question: "Why did gross margin fall 180 basis points?" Answer: "We are very confident about the demand environment." That is a non-answer, and it is a data point.
  • Guidance drift. Track one number — say FY2027 capex — across eight consecutive calls. "₹400 crore" becoming "₹400–450 crore" becoming "around ₹500 crore, with some spillover into FY2028" is a cost overrun being disclosed in slow motion.
  • Metric abandonment. A company that reported capacity utilisation for twelve quarters and then stopped is telling you that utilisation fell.
  • Who is on the call. If the CFO stops attending, find out why. If the same two analysts from boutique firms are the only participants, institutional interest has gone.
  • Tone towards questions about cash. Defensive responses to straightforward working-capital questions correlate with problems more reliably than almost anything else on a call.
  • Promises with dates. Write them into a file with the date. Review in four quarters. A management team's hit rate on its own stated timelines is a direct measure of both competence and candour.
A free and underused resource: your competitors' callsIf you follow a cement company, read the concalls of four other cement companies. Industry pricing, regional demand, freight costs and capacity additions get discussed from five different angles, and inconsistencies between what the companies claim about market share are very informative. Most retail investors read only the filings of the company they own — which is exactly the wrong way round.

What a DRHP gives you that nothing else does

A Draft Red Herring Prospectus is filed with SEBI before an IPO and runs to several hundred pages. Even if you never buy an IPO, DRHPs are a free industry textbook:

  • Industry section — commissioned from a research firm, with market size, growth rates, segment splits and competitive share tables that are otherwise expensive. Read the one from the newest entrant in a sector you follow.
  • Risk factors — legally required to be comprehensive, so they are unusually candid about customer concentration, regulatory exposure, litigation and dependence on specific plants or people.
  • Restated financials — three years, consistently presented, with the restatement adjustments shown. The adjustments themselves often reveal pre-IPO accounting that was being tidied up.
  • Promoter and group entity history — including past ventures, defaults and litigation that you will not easily find elsewhere.
  • Peer comparison table — the company's own choice of comparables, which tells you how it wants to be valued. Scrutinise the omissions.

Treat the valuation justification section with scepticism: it is written by merchant bankers whose fee depends on the issue being fully subscribed.

Set up a monitoring routine before you need itFor each company you follow: subscribe to the exchange announcement alerts, diarise the results date, and keep one running text file per company with dated entries. Every time you learn something — a guidance number, an analyst's concern, a competitor's capacity announcement — add a dated line. After two years this file is worth more than any report you could buy, because it is a record of what you believed and when, which is the only way to find out whether your judgement is improving.

The Profit & Loss Statement, Line by Line

The P&L answers one question: over the past twelve months, did the company sell things for more than it cost to make and sell them? It is the most-read and least-reliable of the three statements, because almost every line involves a judgement call by management, and judgement calls are where manipulation lives. Read it with its notes, and read ten years of it at once.

Meet Bharat Widgets LtdEverything that follows uses one invented company, Bharat Widgets Ltd (BWL), a mid-sized engineering components manufacturer with 12 crore shares outstanding at a face value of ₹10. Its full accounts are carried consistently through the P&L, balance sheet, cash flow, ratio, and DCF sections, so that every number ties back. BWL does not exist. What this table says

  • Gross margin is stable to slowly rising across a decade. This is the headline finding. It survived FY2020. A company that holds gross margin through a demand shock has pricing power that customers accept.
  • EBITDA margin rose faster than gross margin (+4.2 points versus +2.9 points). The extra 1.3 points is operating leverage: revenue grew 137% while employee and other costs grew less than proportionally.
  • EBIT margin rose faster still (+4.1 points), which means depreciation grew more slowly than revenue — the existing asset base was sweated harder. This is good until it is not: at some point the plant is full and a large capex cycle begins. Check capacity utilisation disclosures.

The two-line test that catches most problemsPut revenue growth and gross-margin change on the same chart for ten years. Three patterns matter. Revenue up, gross margin up — genuine competitive strength. Revenue up, gross margin down sharply — growth is being purchased with discounts and is likely worthless. Add a third line for receivable days and you have caught most revenue-quality problems before opening a single note.

Quarterly results: what is and is not in them

Quarterly filings under LODR Reg. 33 are subject to a limited review by the auditor, not a full audit. A limited review provides materially less assurance: it consists principally of enquiry and analytical procedures, not substantive testing. Q4 numbers are normally derived as the full audited year minus the first nine months, which means the annual audit adjustments all land in Q4. Consequences you should internalise:

  • Q4 margins are frequently distorted by true-ups. Compare Q4 against Q4, never against Q3.
  • A large, unexplained gap between the sum of four quarterly PATs and the audited annual PAT means the auditor adjusted something. Find out what.
  • Quarterly numbers are standalone or consolidated depending on what the company elects to publish; many publish both, and screeners mix them up.
  • Segment results are disclosed quarterly and are often more informative than the headline number.
  • Seasonality is real and sector-specific: Q3 for consumer durables and festive-season retail, Q4 for capital goods and infrastructure order closure, Q1 for agricultural inputs ahead of the monsoon. Always compare year-on-year, not quarter-on-quarter.

Reading the balance sheet as a system

Individual line items matter less than the relationships between them. Four checks, in order.

  1. Does the funding match the assets?Long-lived assets should be funded by equity and long-term debt. A company funding a three-year plant construction with 90-day working capital lines is running a refinancing risk that has nothing to do with its profitability. Compare (equity + non-current liabilities) against non-current assets. BWL: 1,500 + 600 = 2,100 against non-current assets of 1,650 — comfortably funded, with ₹450 crore left over to cover net working capital.
  2. Is growth in assets matched by growth in revenue?Total assets grew 8.7% (2,512 to 2,730) while revenue grew 13.2%. Asset turnover is therefore improving. The opposite pattern — assets growing much faster than revenue for several years — means capital is being deployed into something that does not generate sales, and you must find out what.
  3. Does equity growth reconcile with retained profit?Equity rose ₹180 crore (1,320 to 1,500). PAT was ₹240 crore, dividends ₹60 crore, OCI −₹2 crore. 240 − 60 − 2 = ₹178 crore, against ₹180 crore of actual increase; the small residual is the share-based payment reserve. It reconciles. When it does not reconcile, something has been charged directly to reserves, bypassing the P&L, and you need to find it in the statement of changes in equity.
  4. What happens in a bad year?Apply a stress: revenue down 20%, EBITDA margin down 500 basis points. BWL's EBITDA would fall from ₹504 crore to roughly ₹1,920 × 16% = ₹307 crore. Against finance costs of ₹58 crore, interest coverage falls from 6.4× to about 3.0× on an EBIT basis — uncomfortable but survivable. Repeat the same stress on a company with ₹2,000 crore of debt and the answer is a covenant breach. Do this calculation before you buy, not after the bad year starts.

Schedule III disclosures most investors never read

Amendments to Schedule III made a set of disclosures mandatory for Indian companies that are extremely useful and almost universally ignored. Look for them in the notes:

  • Trade receivable ageing — six buckets from "less than six months" to "more than three years", split between undisputed and disputed. The disputed-over-three-years bucket is effectively a loss that has not been taken.
  • Trade payable ageing — same structure. Long-overdue payables indicate liquidity stress.
  • CWIP ageing plus a schedule of projects whose completion is overdue or has exceeded the original cost. This is a direct, named disclosure of capex discipline.
  • Intangible assets under development ageing — same, for capitalised development spend.
  • Ratios — companies must now disclose current ratio, debt-equity, debt service coverage, return on equity, inventory turnover, receivables turnover, payables turnover, net capital turnover, net profit ratio, return on capital employed and return on investment, with an explanation for any change exceeding 25%. The explanations are often more revealing than the ratios.
  • Title deeds of immovable property not held in the company's name — a governance signal.
  • Loans or advances to promoters, directors, KMP and related parties — disclosed separately with amounts and percentages.
  • Wilful defaulter status, struck-off companies transacted with, charges not registered, scheme-of-arrangement compliance, and utilisation of borrowed funds for onward lending to third parties. Each of these exists because it was abused.
Book value is an accounting number, not an economic oneBWL's book value per share of ₹125 is the historical cost of its assets less depreciation and liabilities. It systematically understates land bought decades ago and brands built over generations, and systematically overstates obsolete plant and goodwill from bad acquisitions. For a manufacturer it is a rough anchor. For an asset-light services or consumer company it is close to meaningless — a software firm's real assets walk out of the building every evening. Price-to-book is a useful ratio for banks and a misleading one almost everywhere else, for reasons set out in section 8.

The Cash Flow Statement

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. The cash flow statement reconciles the accounting profit in the P&L to the actual movement of money in the bank, and in doing so it exposes nearly every form of earnings management. If you only had time to read one statement, read this one. If you only had time to compute one ratio, compute cumulative CFO divided by cumulative PAT over five to ten years.

Why CFO is harder to fake than PAT

This is the central point of the entire section, and it is worth being precise about the mechanism rather than merely asserting it.

The two loopholes in CFO, and how to close themCFO can be inflated by (a) capitalising costs, which pushes the outflow from CFO into CFI, and (b) squeezing working capital at year-end through payable stretching, aggressive collection or bill discounting. Both are closed by using free cash flow — CFO minus capex — and by checking that capex is proportionate to the capacity actually added. A company whose CFO is excellent but whose capex is three times depreciation with no new capacity to show for it has simply moved the problem one line down the statement.

Apply this test to every company you look atAdd up CFO for the last ten reported years. Add up PAT for the same ten years. Why the cash flow statement is only audited annually

Under SEBI LODR, Indian listed companies disclose a cash flow statement half-yearly, not quarterly. This means that for two quarters of every year you are reading a P&L with no cash flow statement attached to validate it. Practical consequences:

  • A company can report three strong quarters and reveal the cash position only at the half-year and full-year points. Build the habit of suspending judgement on a strong quarterly result until the next cash flow disclosure.
  • In the absence of a quarterly cash flow statement, use the quarterly balance sheet proxies that are available: the half-yearly balance sheet, and any disclosure of debt levels. A rising debt balance alongside rising profit is the quarterly equivalent of a bad CFO.
  • The half-yearly balance sheet requirement under LODR Reg. 33 was a significant improvement precisely because it gave investors a working-capital read twice a year instead of once.

Ratio Analysis in Full

A ratio is a compression device: it turns two numbers into one, so that companies of different sizes can be compared and so that a trend becomes visible. Every ratio also destroys information, and most have at least one sector where they are actively misleading. Learn the formula, the interpretation, the sector caveat, and what a deteriorating trend means — in that order.

Four rules before any ratioOne. A ratio in isolation means nothing; a ten-year trend and a peer comparison mean something. Two. Where a ratio divides a flow (from the P&L) by a stock (from the balance sheet), use the average of opening and closing balance sheet figures, not the closing figure — otherwise a company that raised equity in March will show an artificially depressed ROE. Three. Compute it yourself from the annual report; screener definitions vary and are frequently wrong. Four. Banks, NBFCs, insurers and holding companies need entirely different ratios — see section 12.

Growth Analysis

Growth is not automatically good. Growth funded at a return below the cost of capital destroys value faster the quicker it happens. The questions that matter are: where did the growth come from, what did it cost in capital, is it repeatable, and what return is the incremental capital earning? A revenue CAGR chart answers none of these.

  Revenue CAGR 10.1%  ->  PAT CAGR 16.0%
  The 5.9-point gap is MARGIN EXPANSION, which is
  finite. Margins cannot expand forever. Over the
  next decade, PAT growth will converge towards
  revenue growth unless margins keep rising.

  EPS CAGR 15.4% < PAT CAGR 16.0% because the share
  count rose from 11.4 to 12.0 crore (ESOP exercise).
  ALWAYS CHECK THIS GAP. A company growing PAT at 20%
  while issuing 8% more shares a year is growing your
  per-share claim at about 11%.

THE BASE-EFFECT TRAP
  FY2020 revenue 1,368 (a weak year)
  FY2021 revenue 1,490  ->  +8.9%
  FY2022 revenue 1,742  ->  +16.9%

  A headline of "17% growth" in FY2022 is partly a
  recovery from a depressed base. The honest measure
  is the 2-year CAGR off FY2020, or better, the CAGR
  off the pre-disruption FY2019 peak of 1,402:
    (1,742/1,402)^(1/3) - 1  =  7.5% per year
  That is the real underlying rate, and it is less
  than half the headline.

  RULE: in any year following an abnormal base, quote
  a multi-year CAGR alongside the year-on-year figure.
  Companies quote whichever flatters them.

Operating leverage: the mathematics

TEXT
OPERATING LEVERAGE - WHY MARGINS MOVE MORE THAN
REVENUE

A company's costs split into variable (move with
volume) and fixed (do not). BWL's rough split:

  Variable costs (materials, freight,
    power, packing)                  1,310   54.6%
  Fixed costs (employee, rent,
    admin, selling, insurance)         586   24.4%
  ------------------------------------------
  Total operating cost (ex-D&A)      1,896   79.0%
  EBITDA                               504   21.0%

DEGREE OF OPERATING LEVERAGE (DOL)
  DOL = contribution / EBITDA
      = (revenue - variable costs) / EBITDA
      = (2,400 - 1,310) / 504
      = 1,090 / 504
      = 2.16x

  INTERPRETATION: a 1% change in revenue produces a
  2.16% change in EBITDA, in either direction.

TEST IT - REVENUE UP 10%
  Revenue                2,400 -> 2,640   (+10%)
  Variable costs         1,310 -> 1,441   (+10%)
  Fixed costs              586 ->   586   (flat)
  EBITDA                   504 ->   613   (+21.6%)
  EBITDA margin          21.0% -> 23.2%
  Check: 10% x 2.16 = 21.6%.  Confirmed.

TEST IT - REVENUE DOWN 10%
  Revenue                2,400 -> 2,160
  Variable costs         1,310 -> 1,179
  Fixed costs              586 ->   586
  EBITDA                   504 ->   395   (-21.6%)
  EBITDA margin          21.0% -> 18.3%

TEST IT - REVENUE DOWN 25% (a real recession)
  Revenue                       -> 1,800
  Variable costs                ->   983
  Fixed costs                   ->   586
  EBITDA                        ->   231   (-54%)
  EBITDA margin                 -> 12.8%
  EBIT = 231 - 132 depreciation =    99
  Interest coverage = 99 / 58   =  1.7x
  -> Covenant territory. This is the stress number
     you should know BEFORE you buy.

COMBINING OPERATING AND FINANCIAL LEVERAGE
  Degree of financial leverage = EBIT / (EBIT - interest)
                               = 372 / (372 - 58) = 1.18x
  Total leverage = 2.16 x 1.18 = 2.55x
  A 10% revenue fall produces a 25.5% fall in PBT.

  For a company with DOL of 4 and DFL of 2.5, total
  leverage is 10x: a 10% revenue fall wipes out PBT
  entirely. Airlines, steel, shipping and hotels sit
  in this region. This is the arithmetic behind why
  cyclicals are dangerous, and it has nothing to do
  with the quality of management.
Operating leverage cuts both ways and is not a quality signalHigh operating leverage makes good years spectacular and bad years catastrophic. It is a structural property of the cost base, not evidence of a good business. When you read that a company's margins "expanded strongly on operating leverage", the correct response is to compute what happens when revenue falls by the amount it fell in the last downturn. For BWL, FY2020 saw revenue fall 2.4% and EBITDA margin fall 180 basis points — consistent with a DOL of roughly 2 at that time. The arithmetic is honest even when the narrative is not.

Incremental ROCE: the most under-used growth metric

TEXT
INCREMENTAL RETURN ON CAPITAL EMPLOYED

The question: what return did the company earn on the
capital it added over the period? This is far more
informative than the average ROCE, because the average
is dominated by legacy assets that cannot be changed.

  Incremental ROCE
    = (EBIT_end - EBIT_start) / (CE_end - CE_start)

BWL, FY2021 to FY2025
  EBIT FY2021                 =   192
  EBIT FY2025                 =   372
  Change in EBIT              =   180

  Capital employed FY2021     = 1,586
  Capital employed FY2025     = 2,100
  Change in capital employed  =   514

  Incremental ROCE = 180 / 514  =  35.0%  (pre-tax)
  Post-tax at 25.17%            =  26.2%

  Average ROCE over the period  =  ~17%

  READING: the capital BWL deployed over the last
  four years earned roughly twice the return of its
  legacy capital base. This is why ROCE has been
  rising, and it is a strong argument for the company
  continuing to reinvest rather than pay out.

CONTRAST - a value-destroying grower
  EBIT change over 4 years           +  96
  Capital employed change            + 980
  Incremental ROCE = 96/980   =  9.8% pre-tax
                              =  7.3% post-tax
  Against a WACC of 12%, every rupee of growth capex
  destroys about 4.7 paise a year, in perpetuity. The
  revenue chart still goes up and to the right.

THE SUSTAINABLE GROWTH RATE
  g = ROE x retention ratio
    = ROE x (1 - dividend payout)

  BWL: ROE 17.0%, dividend 60 on PAT 240
       payout = 25%, retention = 75%
  g = 0.170 x 0.75  =  12.75%

  INTERPRETATION: BWL can grow earnings at about
  12.75% a year indefinitely WITHOUT raising external
  capital or increasing leverage. Its actual PAT
  growth has been ~16%, which is only possible
  because margins were expanding. Once margins
  plateau, 12.75% is the ceiling.

  This single calculation is the sanity check on
  every growth forecast you will ever read. If an
  analyst projects 25% earnings growth for a company
  with 17% ROE and a 25% payout, they are implicitly
  assuming either margin expansion, an equity raise,
  rising leverage, or an acquisition. Make them say
  which.

Valuation Part 1 — Relative

Relative valuation asks what similar businesses trade at and applies that multiple to the one in front of you. It is fast, intuitive, universally used, and quietly circular — it tells you what a company is worth if the market is correctly valuing its peers. It cannot tell you whether an entire sector is mispriced. Used with that limitation understood, it is the most practical valuation tool available.

Why a low P/E is usually low for a reason

TEXT
DECOMPOSING THE P/E

For a stable business, the theoretical P/E is:

   P/E  =  payout ratio x (1 + g)  /  (r - g)

   where g = sustainable growth, r = cost of equity

BWL: payout 25%, g 12.75%, r 13.0%
   P/E = 0.25 x 1.1275 / (0.130 - 0.1275)
       = 0.2819 / 0.0025
       = 112x  ???

This absurd answer is instructive. When g approaches
r, the formula explodes. It is telling you something
true: a company that can genuinely reinvest at 17%
ROE forever, while the market demands 13%, is worth
an unbounded amount. The formula fails because the
assumption fails - NOTHING grows at 12.75% forever.

Use a sustainable long-run g instead, say 6%:
   P/E = 0.25 x 1.06 / (0.13 - 0.06)
       = 0.265 / 0.07  =  3.8x

Also wrong, because a 25% payout is inconsistent with
only 6% growth - a company growing at 6% with 17% ROE
only needs to retain 35% (6/17), so it should pay out
65%:
   P/E = 0.65 x 1.06 / (0.13 - 0.06)
       = 0.689 / 0.07  =  9.8x

THE LESSON: payout, growth and ROE are NOT independent.
  g = ROE x (1 - payout)
Any P/E model where these three are set independently
is internally inconsistent. This is the most common
error in amateur valuation work.

WHAT ACTUALLY DRIVES A LOW P/E
  Low ROE          -> more capital retained per unit
                      of growth -> less cash to
                      shareholders -> lower multiple
  High risk (r)    -> higher discount -> lower multiple
  Low growth (g)   -> lower multiple
  Cyclical peak E  -> the E is temporarily inflated,
                      so the P/E is temporarily low
  Governance doubt -> the market applies a discount to
                      reported earnings it does not
                      fully believe
  Low liquidity    -> small-cap illiquidity discount
  Earnings quality -> CFO/PAT below 1 for years

Before concluding a stock is cheap, work through this
list and find out which of the seven applies. In the
overwhelming majority of cases, at least one does.

A reality check on multiples: what the market is implicitly assuming

TEXT
WHAT A P/E OF 24x ACTUALLY IMPLIES

Simplest version: the earnings yield
   1 / 24.2  =  4.13%

If BWL never grew, never reinvested, and paid out
100% of earnings, you would earn 4.13% a year
forever. Against a 10-year G-Sec at roughly 6.8%
(late 2026, illustrative - verify at rbi.org.in),
you would be paying for the privilege of taking
equity risk.

So the 24.2x multiple is a statement that earnings
will grow. How much?

Required: equity return of 13% (cost of equity).
Current earnings yield: 4.13%.
Gap that growth must close: 8.87% a year.

Rough test using the Gordon relationship:
   r  =  earnings yield x payout  +  g
   0.13 = 0.0413 x 0.25 + g
   0.13 = 0.0103 + g
   g  =  11.97%

The market is implicitly assuming BWL grows earnings
at about 12% a year INDEFINITELY. Against a
sustainable growth rate of 12.75% computed in section
7, that is internally consistent - but only if ROE
stays at 17% and the payout stays at 25% forever.

THE QUESTION YOU NOW HAVE TO ANSWER IS SPECIFIC:
"Can this business sustain a 17% return on equity for
the next fifteen to twenty years?" That is a question
about the moat, the industry structure, and the
competence of management. It is NOT a question about
the P/E ratio.

This is the correct use of relative valuation: not to
produce a target price, but to translate the market
price into a statement about the business, which you
can then agree or disagree with on the evidence.
The multiple is not the thesis"It trades at 14× while the sector trades at 22×" is an observation, not an argument. The argument has to be a reason why the gap should close: an earnings inflection that is visible and funded, a governance concern that has been resolved, a cyclical trough that is passing, a misunderstood segment that is about to be disclosed separately. Without a named catalyst or a named misperception, a discount to peers is simply the market's correct assessment that the business is worse — and it can persist for a decade.

Valuation Part 2 — Intrinsic and the DCF

A discounted cash flow model is the only valuation method derived from first principles rather than from what other people are paying. It is also extraordinarily sensitive to assumptions you cannot know. Both statements are true and must be held simultaneously. Build the DCF, then treat the output as a range, then use the reverse DCF — which is the genuinely useful tool.

The mechanics

TEXT
DCF STRUCTURE

  Enterprise value
    = PV of explicit-period free cash flow to the firm
    + PV of terminal value

  Equity value = EV - net debt (+ surplus investments)
  Per share    = equity value / diluted shares

FCFF, from the operating result:
    EBIT
  - tax on EBIT (EBIT x effective rate)
  = NOPAT
  + depreciation and amortisation   (non-cash)
  - capital expenditure
  - increase in net working capital
  = FCFF

Discount FCFF at WACC, because FCFF is the cash
available to BOTH lenders and shareholders. Never
discount FCFF at the cost of equity; never discount
FCFE at WACC. Mixing them is the single most common
structural error in amateur models.

Qualitative Analysis: Moats, Industry and Management

The numbers tell you what happened. Qualitative analysis tells you whether it will keep happening. A high return on capital is an invitation to competitors; the only reason it persists is that something stops them. Identifying that something — or establishing that there is nothing — is the part of analysis that cannot be screened for and therefore the part where an individual can still add value.

TAM, runway, and why most TAM numbers are useless

Total addressable market figures in investor presentations are almost always sourced from a commissioned consultancy report and are almost always too large, because they count the entire category rather than the segment the company can realistically serve at its price point. Three disciplines fix this.

  1. Build TAM bottom-up, not top-downTop-down: "the Indian widget market is ₹80,000 crore". Bottom-up: number of plausible customers × units each buys per year × realistic price. The two answers typically differ by a factor of three to ten, and the bottom-up one is usually right.
  2. Compute the implied share, then sanity-check itIf a ₹2,400 crore company is to grow at 16% for ten years, it reaches ₹10,600 crore. Against a credible serviceable market of ₹30,000 crore growing at 8%, that is a move from roughly 8% share to 16% share. Ask who loses that share, and whether they will let it happen quietly.
  3. Convert runway into yearsThe useful question is not "how big is the market" but "at the current growth rate, how many years before the market constrains the company?" If the answer is under five years, the terminal assumptions in your valuation are doing all the work and the growth story is already mostly priced.

Accounting Red Flags and Forensic Basics

Forensic analysis is not about proving fraud. It is about identifying companies where the published numbers are internally inconsistent, and then declining to invest rather than investigating further. You are not an auditor, you have no subpoena power, and the asymmetry is in your favour: there are thousands of listed companies and you only need a handful. The correct response to an unresolved red flag is to move on.

The Beneish M-score and Altman Z-score: useful, limitedTwo published statistical screens exist. The Beneish M-score combines eight accounting ratios into a single earnings-manipulation probability; the Altman Z-score combines five into a bankruptcy-risk measure. Both were calibrated on US data from earlier decades and neither transfers cleanly to Indian accounting or to Indian capital structures — and the Z-score in particular is meaningless for financial companies. Use them as a ranking device across a screened list to decide where to look first, never as a verdict. The three cross-checks above, done by hand on the actual filings, are more reliable than either score.

Sector-Specific Analysis

Almost everything in sections 3 to 9 assumes a company that buys inputs, makes something, and sells it. For several large parts of the Indian market that model does not apply at all, and applying the standard ratios produces confident nonsense. Banks have no revenue line in the ordinary sense; their inventory is money and their biggest cost is a judgement about future losses. This section gives the correct framework for each major sector.

Macro and Top-Down Inputs

Macro forecasting is close to impossible and most investors would do better ignoring it entirely. What is useful is understanding the transmission mechanisms: how a change in interest rates, inflation, the currency or policy actually reaches a particular company's cash flows. You will not predict the repo rate. You can know, in advance, what a 200 basis point rate rise would do to the company you own.

Technical Analysis Part 1 — The Honest Framing

Technical analysis is the study of price and volume history in order to say something about future price. It is taught badly, sold aggressively, and defended tribally. It also contains a small, real, academically documented core surrounded by a very large body of material that does not survive honest testing. This section separates the two, because you cannot use a tool sensibly until you know which parts of it work.

A specific warning about Indian retail derivativesSEBI has published studies on individual traders in the equity derivatives segment showing that a large majority lose money, with aggregate retail losses running into many thousands of crores per year, and that losses grow with trading frequency. Short-horizon technical trading in futures and options is the single most reliable way for an individual to destroy capital in Indian markets. Check the latest SEBI study at sebi.gov.in before you form a view that you will be the exception. Nothing in this section is encouragement to trade.

The same logic cuts against chart-based methods: patterns that are visible to everyone on a free charting platform are the most likely to have been arbitraged away, because they require no effort to find.

Why most technical analysis fails: four structural reasons

  1. Overfitting and the multiple-comparisons problemThere are thousands of possible indicators, each with adjustable parameters, tested across thousands of instruments and timeframes. With that many combinations, some will look spectacular on historical data purely by chance. If you test 1,000 strategies at a 5% significance level, you expect 50 to appear "significant" when none of them work. Published trading systems are selected precisely because they looked good in the past, which guarantees selection bias.
  2. Survivorship and hindsight in the examplesTeaching material shows the charts where the pattern worked. The same pattern on the same stock three months earlier, where it failed, is not shown. Nobody keeps a log of every head-and-shoulders that did not complete.
  3. Subjectivity defeats testing"Draw a trendline connecting the significant lows" is not a rule. Which lows are significant? Over what window? Where two analysts draw different lines, the method has no falsifiable content, and whatever happens can be explained afterwards.
  4. Costs, taxes and slippage destroy the edgeA strategy generating a 12% gross annual return with 300% turnover faces brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST, stamp duty, bid-ask spread and short-term capital gains tax at 20% on equity (as at FY 2025-26 — verify at incometax.gov.in). The net result is frequently negative. Any backtest that does not model full Indian transaction costs and taxes is fiction.

The defensible uses of technical tools

Risk management

Defining in advance a price at which you will accept you were wrong, and sizing the position so that being wrong costs a known, survivable amount. This is the strongest case for using charts at all, and it is about discipline rather than prediction.

Position sizing

Volatility-adjusted sizing — smaller positions in more volatile instruments so that each contributes similar risk. ATR is a legitimate input here. See the worked example in section 15.

Execution timing

Once the fundamental decision to buy is made, spreading entry over weeks and avoiding buying into a vertical move is sensible. This is a marginal improvement to a decision already taken, not a decision in itself.

Liquidity and crowding assessment

Average traded volume and delivery percentage tell you whether you can actually enter and exit a small-cap at a reasonable price. Entirely practical, no prediction required.

Sentiment context

Extreme readings — a stock up 300% in six months on ten times normal volume — are a reason to re-examine valuation assumptions, not a signal in themselves.

A check on your own narrative

If you believe a company is improving and the price has fallen steadily for two years on rising volume, somebody disagrees with you and may know something. That is a prompt to re-read the filings, not to capitulate.

The strongest honest conclusionFor an individual investor with a multi-year horizon, technical analysis is optional, and the opportunity cost of learning it thoroughly is high. The same hours spent reading annual reports will produce more. If you do use it, confine it to risk management and execution, keep the toolkit small, write your rules down before you trade so that they can be evaluated, and keep a log of every signal including the ones that failed. If you will not keep the log, you will never know whether it is helping, and almost everyone who does not keep the log believes it is.

Support, resistance, trendlines and channels

  • Support is a price region where buying has previously been sufficient to stop a decline; resistance is the mirror image. The mechanism is partly order clustering at round numbers and prior extremes, and partly the behaviour of participants who bought at a level and want to exit at break-even.
  • They are zones, not lines. Treating ₹480 as support when the stock has turned at ₹471, ₹484 and ₹476 is false precision.
  • Broken support often acts as resistance afterwards, and vice versa. Commonly observed, modestly evidenced.
  • Trendlines connect successive higher lows in an uptrend or lower highs in a downtrend. The problem is that which lows you connect is a choice, and the line can usually be drawn several ways. A trendline on a log scale over many years is more defensible than one on a linear scale over months.
  • Channels add a parallel line on the opposite side. Visually appealing; the same subjectivity problem.
  • The only robust use: a clearly defined level below which your reason for owning the stock would be called into question, used to place a stop. That converts an ambiguous drawing into a concrete risk decision.

Most people who believe technical analysis works for them have never kept this record, and memory is systematically biased towards the trades that worked. The log is the whole experiment. Putting It Together: The Investment Thesis

Analysis that is not written down is not analysis; it is a feeling with spreadsheets attached. The discipline of compressing everything into one page forces you to identify what you are actually betting on, and the dated record is the only mechanism by which you will ever learn whether your judgement is any good.

Pre-mortem and falsification

Two disciplines do more to improve outcomes than any amount of additional model-building, and both take fifteen minutes.

The pre-mortem

Before buying, assume it is five years later and the position has lost 60%. Write the story of how. This defeats the natural tendency to build a case for a conclusion you have already reached, because it requires you to construct the opposing argument. If you cannot write a plausible failure story, you do not understand the business well enough to own it.

Falsification criteria

Specific, observable, dated conditions which, if met, mean the thesis is broken. "The stock falls 30%" is not a falsification criterion — price is not evidence about the business. "Gross margin falls below 50% for two consecutive quarters" is, because it is the measurable manifestation of the thing you claimed was durable.

A screen is a hypothesis expressed as filters. It narrows thousands of listed companies to a few dozen worth reading about. It is a starting point and never an answer, because every screen operates on standardised data that is sometimes wrong, and every popular screen is run by thousands of people simultaneously.

Frequently Asked Questions

Questions a beginner analysing Indian stocks actually asks, answered without hedging. None of this is a recommendation; all rates and rules are as at FY 2025-26 / late 2026 and must be verified at the official portals named throughout.

How many years of financials do I really need to look at?
Ten, as a hard minimum, and more if the company is cyclical. Five years will usually not include a downturn, and a business that has never been tested has not told you anything about its durability. The specific thing you are looking for in the long series is behaviour in the worst year: what happened to volumes, margins, receivables, debt and the dividend. That single year tells you more about the business than the nine good ones combined.
Can I rely on screener websites instead of reading annual reports?
For generating a reading list, yes. For making a decision, no. Screeners standardise Indian line items into their own schema and routinely mis-handle exceptional items, other income, consolidated-versus-standalone, lease liabilities and companies with recent demergers or acquisitions. More importantly, no screener has a field for related-party transactions, pledge trends, auditor resignations, contingent liabilities or the CWIP ageing schedule — and those are the items most likely to cause you a permanent loss. Use the screener for step one, the annual report for every step after.
Should I use standalone or consolidated numbers?
Consolidated, always, as your primary view — it describes the whole economic entity you own a share of. Use standalone only as a diagnostic to isolate how the parent's own operations are doing versus the subsidiaries. A company whose standalone accounts look healthy while consolidated accounts show losses has problems in subsidiaries, which is a pattern worth investigating rather than ignoring. Note that many screeners default to standalone.
What is the single most useful ratio if I only have time for one?
Cumulative cash flow from operations divided by cumulative profit after tax, over ten years. It should be comfortably above 1.0, because depreciation is added back. If it is below 1.0 over a decade, reported profits are not becoming cash, and nothing else in the analysis matters until you understand why. It takes about four minutes per company and it removes a large share of the worst outcomes from your opportunity set. If you have time for a second, make it post-tax ROIC versus WACC.
Is a low P/E a buying signal?
Almost never on its own. Work through the seven structural causes of a low multiple in section 8: low return on equity, high risk, low growth, cyclically peak earnings, governance doubt, illiquidity, and poor earnings quality. In the overwhelming majority of cases at least one applies, and the market is pricing it correctly. For cyclical businesses the relationship actually inverts — a steel or cement company at 6× trailing earnings is usually at the top of its cycle, and a low P/E there is a warning rather than an invitation.
How accurate is a DCF really?
As a point estimate, close to useless — the worked example in section 9 produces a range of ₹204 to ₹372 across nothing more than plausible variations in the discount rate and terminal growth, before touching any operating assumption. Its genuine value is in two places. First, it forces every assumption into the open where it can be argued about. Second, run in reverse, it converts the market price into a specific testable statement about growth and margins, which is a question you can actually research. Build the DCF for the discipline; use the reverse DCF for the decision.
What discount rate should I use for an Indian company?
For a typical listed non-financial company, the plausible band is roughly 11–14%, built from a 10-year G-Sec yield (verify the current level at rbi.org.in) plus an equity risk premium for India that reasonable practitioners put somewhere between about 5.5% and 8%, adjusted for the company's beta and capital structure. Add a further premium for small size, illiquidity or governance concerns if applicable. The exact number is less important than testing the valuation across the band and reporting the range. Anyone quoting a precise WACC to two decimal places is conveying false confidence.
Does technical analysis work?
A narrow part of it does. Time-series and cross-sectional momentum are robustly documented across many markets and decades, and volatility clustering is a real statistical property that supports position sizing with measures like ATR. Most of the rest — named candlestick patterns, head and shoulders, oscillator reversal signals, Elliott Wave, Gann — does not survive systematic testing, largely because it is too subjective to test and the teaching material shows only the cases that worked. For an investor with a multi-year horizon, the defensible uses are risk management, position sizing and execution timing. If you use it, keep a log including the failed signals, because memory is systematically biased towards the ones that worked.
Why are bank ratios so different?
Because a bank's raw material is money and its biggest cost is a judgement about future loan losses. Deposits are not distress debt — leverage of 8–12× is the business model, so debt-to-equity is meaningless. Interest income is operating revenue, so EBITDA has no meaning. The maturity mismatch between short deposits and long loans is the business, so the current ratio is meaningless. Use instead: net interest margin, CASA ratio, GNPA and NNPA, provision coverage, credit cost, capital adequacy and CET-1, cost-to-income, return on assets, and price to adjusted book value. Section 12 has the full set.
What are the fastest red flags to check before spending real time on a company?
Six checks, roughly ten minutes total. (1) Cumulative CFO versus cumulative PAT over ten years. (2) The promoter pledge column in the latest shareholding pattern. (3) The auditor's report for a qualification, an emphasis of matter on going concern, or a recent resignation. (4) The related-party transactions note, as a percentage of revenue and purchases, trended. (5) Receivable and inventory days over ten years against revenue growth. (6) The contingent liabilities note against equity, looking specifically for guarantees given for promoter group entities. Any one of these failing is sufficient reason to stop and move to the next idea.
How do I value a loss-making company?
Very carefully, and usually you should not try. The honest options are: a DCF that explicitly models the path to profitability with the funding requirement included, which is extremely assumption-dependent; EV/Sales benchmarked against what mature peers achieve, which requires you to assume a terminal margin; unit economics built bottom-up (contribution per customer, customer acquisition cost, payback period, retention), which is the most informative when the data is disclosed; or replacement cost and asset value for a cyclical at the trough. In every case, model the cash runway and the dilution required to reach breakeven, because a company that must raise equity at a depressed price transfers a large share of the eventual upside away from existing holders.
How much of my portfolio should be in direct stocks?
This is a question about your time, not your intelligence. A first pass on one company takes 26–42 hours, and an annual update takes about five. Fifteen companies is therefore a part-time job indefinitely. For most working professionals the honest answer is to hold the core of the equity allocation in diversified or index funds and cap the direct-stock sleeve at something like 10–25% of equity — enough to learn properly and to care about the outcome, small enough that your blind spots cannot damage the financial plan. The mutual funds guide covers the core allocation in detail, and the foundations guide covers the question of how much to put into equity at all.
What should I do when a stock I own falls 40%?
First, separate the two cases, because they require opposite responses. If the business thesis is intact and nothing on your falsification list has triggered, a 40% fall is a lower price for the same cash flows and is an opportunity — provided the position size still fits your limits. If a falsification criterion has triggered, the fall is information and you should act on the criterion, not on the price. The disciplined procedure is to re-read your original note before looking at any news, decide whether anything on the list has changed, and only then look at the price. Never average down on a position where leverage or governance is part of the concern, because those are the situations where a 40% loss becomes a 95% loss.
Are IPOs worth analysing?
The DRHP is one of the most information-dense documents available and is worth reading even if you never subscribe — particularly for the industry section and the risk factors, and particularly when the issuer is a competitor of a company you already follow. As an investment, bear in mind the structural asymmetry: the issue is timed and priced by selling shareholders and merchant bankers with far better information than you, and typically in a favourable market. There is also no long public track record to analyse, which removes most of the tools in this guide. None of that makes IPOs uninvestable; it means the burden of proof is higher, not lower, than for a company with ten years of audited filings.
How do I know when my analysis is good enough to act on?
Three tests. One: can you explain the business, its moat and its main risk to someone unfamiliar with it in three minutes, without notes? Two: can you write the pre-mortem — a specific, plausible story of how this loses 60% — without reaching? Three: have you written down falsification criteria that are observable and dated, so that in two years you will be able to tell whether you were right for the right reasons? If any of the three fails, you have a feeling rather than a thesis. The written note is not administrative overhead; it is the only instrument that will ever tell you whether your judgement is improving.

The Complete Analysis Checklist and a Six-Week Programme

Everything in this guide, compressed into a working checklist, followed by a structured programme for building the skill on one real company. Work through the checklist in order; most companies will fail at section A or B, which is the point — the checklist exists to save you time, not to be completed.

A. Information gathered (5 items)

  • Ten years of annual reports downloaded, consolidated
  • Last four concall transcripts read, with guidance numbers extracted and dated
  • Latest investor presentation read, operating metrics noted, abandoned metrics noted
  • Latest credit rating rationale read
  • Last eight quarterly shareholding patterns checked for promoter holding and pledge trend

  • Promoter pledge is zero, or small, declining and explained

  • Contingent liabilities are modest relative to equity, with no large group guarantees
  • Receivable days and inventory days are not compounding faster than revenue
  • No exchange surveillance action (ASM, GSM) in force

C. Profit and loss (7 items)

  • Ten-year revenue, gross, EBITDA, EBIT and PAT margin table built by hand
  • Gross margin trend examined against revenue growth (the two-line test)
  • Other income quantified as a percentage of PBT for each of ten years
  • Exceptional items counted over ten years and normalised earnings computed
  • Effective tax rate compared with the statutory rate; the reconciliation note read
  • Diluted share count tracked over ten years; the dilution rate computed
  • "Other expenses" note read and decomposed; "miscellaneous" checked for size and growth

D. Balance sheet (8 items)

  • Net debt computed including lease liabilities; maturity ladder built from the borrowings note
  • Goodwill and intangibles as a percentage of equity; impairment assumptions read
  • Internally generated intangibles and "intangibles under development" examined
  • CWIP ageing schedule and the overdue/over-budget project disclosure read
  • Trade receivable ageing read; provision as a percentage of receivables trended
  • Net operating working capital and incremental NWC per rupee of incremental revenue computed
  • Funding match checked: equity plus non-current liabilities against non-current assets
  • Equity movement reconciled to PAT less dividends less buybacks less OCI

E. Cash flow (5 items)

  • Ten-year CFO, capex, FCF table built; the three-section signature pattern identified
  • CFO/EBITDA and CFO/PAT computed for each of ten years
  • Capex to depreciation ratio checked against actual capacity additions
  • Maintenance versus growth capex estimated; owner earnings computed
  • Dividend tested against FCFE for affordability

F. Ratios and returns (7 items)

  • ROE, ROCE and post-tax ROIC computed on average balances, with definitions written down
  • Post-tax ROIC compared against estimated WACC; the spread stated
  • Five-step DuPont decomposition over five years; the source of any ROE change identified
  • Cash conversion cycle computed and trended; compared against direct peers only
  • Net debt/EBITDA, interest coverage and debt service coverage computed
  • Incremental ROCE over five years computed
  • Sustainable growth rate (ROE × retention) computed and compared with actual growth

G. Growth quality (4 items)

  • Organic growth separated from acquired growth using the business combinations note
  • Volume growth separated from price and mix, using disclosed operating metrics
  • Degree of operating leverage computed; a 20–25% revenue decline stress-tested through to interest coverage
  • Any past acquisition assessed for return on the price paid, and for subsequent impairment

H. Qualitative (6 items)

  • The moat named specifically, with the financial signature that evidences it
  • Ten-year ROCE variance examined — persistence, not level, is the moat test
  • Five forces assessed, with customer and supplier concentration quantified
  • TAM built bottom-up; implied market share at the forecast growth rate computed and sanity-checked
  • Ten-year capital allocation scorecard built: sources, uses, and the five questions scored
  • Board, auditor, remuneration and promoter conduct history reviewed

I. Valuation (6 items)

  • Peer table built with every multiple computed identically by you
  • Trailing and forward P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT and FCF yield computed
  • Mid-cycle earnings used instead of trailing earnings if the business is cyclical
  • DCF built with every assumption written down before the output was computed
  • Sensitivity table produced across WACC and terminal growth; both terminal-value methods reconciled
  • Reverse DCF run; the implied growth and margin assumptions stated in one sentence and judged

J. Decision and discipline (5 items)

  • One-page note written and dated
  • Pre-mortem written: a specific story of a 60% loss
  • Falsification criteria listed, observable and measurable
  • Position size set from conviction, liquidity and volatility — not from enthusiasm
  • Review triggers diarised: results dates and the specific announcements that would matter

A six-week practice programme

  1. Week 1 — Choose and understandPick one mid-sized, non-financial, profitable Indian company whose product you can describe in a sentence. Avoid banks, NBFCs, holding companies and anything loss-making for your first attempt. Read the two most recent annual reports in the order given in section 2. Write a 300-word description of what the business does and how it makes money. Do not look at the share price all week.
  2. Week 2 — Build the data sheetDownload ten years of annual reports. Type — do not import — the raw P&L, balance sheet and cash flow lines into a spreadsheet. Compute every derived ratio yourself. Where your number disagrees with a screener's, find out which is right and why. This week is tedious and it is where the skill actually forms.
  3. Week 3 — Interrogate the qualityRun every item in checklist sections B, C, D and E. Read the related-party note, the contingent liabilities note, the CWIP and receivable ageing schedules, and the auditor's Key Audit Matters for each of the last five years. Write a one-page memo listing every unresolved question.
  4. Week 4 — Industry and moatRead the last four concalls of your company and of three competitors. Find independent industry volume data from an association or regulator. Build the peer comparison table. Name the moat, state the financial evidence for it, and write the three specific ways it could erode.
  5. Week 5 — ValuationBuild the peer multiples table, then the DCF with every assumption written down first, then the sensitivity table, then the three scenarios. Finally run the reverse DCF and write one sentence describing what the current market price requires the company to achieve. Compare that sentence against what you learned in weeks 2 to 4.
  6. Week 6 — Write it up, and do nothingProduce the one-page note from section 16, including the pre-mortem and the falsification criteria. Then do not buy anything. File the note. Diarise a review for each of the next four quarterly results. The purpose of the first company is to build the process, not to take a position — and a note written without the pressure of an open position is the most honest one you will ever write. Repeat the whole cycle on a second company; by the third, it takes a quarter of the time.

And if after a year of this work you find you do not enjoy it and do not have the hours, that is a legitimate and common conclusion: the funds route is not a consolation prize, it is the correct answer for most people.
