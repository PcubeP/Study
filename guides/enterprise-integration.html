The Integration Reality of Enterprise Customers

The demo took four weeks. The deployment takes nine months. Nothing about the model, the algorithm or the user interface accounts for that gap — it is almost entirely plumbing. Getting data out of an ERP that was configured in 2011 by a consultancy that no longer exists, through a proxy that strips headers, into a schema that three departments disagree about, on a schedule that fits a change window that happens twice a year. That work is the job. A Forward Deployed Engineer who is good at integration is worth several who are good at everything else.

What an enterprise landscape actually looks like

The architecture diagram the customer shows you in the kickoff has eight boxes. The real system has sixty, and the important ones are not on the diagram because the people who drew it did not know about the Access database in Finance or the nightly SFTP drop that keeps the warehouse running. Expect to find:

Systems of record

SAP ECC or S/4HANA, Oracle EBS, Microsoft Dynamics, Workday, a custom mainframe application. Slow to change, owned by a team that does not report to your sponsor, protected by a change board.

Systems of engagement

Salesforce, ServiceNow, Microsoft 365, a customer portal, a mobile app. Faster-moving, API-friendly, and usually where your sponsor sits.

Middleware

MuleSoft, Boomi, SAP CPI, Azure Logic Apps, an IBM Integration Bus from 2009, or an in-house Java ESB that one person understands. You will be told to go through it.

The data platform

A data warehouse (Snowflake, BigQuery, Synapse, Teradata), a lake, and an overnight ETL that everyone depends on and nobody owns end to end.

The shadow estate

Excel files on SharePoint that are load-bearing, an Access database, a Python script on someone's desktop, a Power Automate flow built by a business analyst. These are often the actual integration points.

The security layer

An API gateway, a WAF, a forward proxy, a TLS-inspecting middlebox, an identity provider, a PAM vault, a DLP agent. Each is owned by a different team with a different queue.

The questions to ask in the first week

  1. What is the system of record for this entity?Customer, product, employee, invoice, case — there must be exactly one authoritative source per entity, and if there are two, you have found the project's hardest problem on day one. Ask who arbitrates when they disagree.
  2. How do I get data out, and who owns that interface?Not "does it have an API" — specifically: which API, which version, which environment, who grants access, what is the lead time for a service account, and when was it last used successfully by anyone.
  3. What is the volume and the shape?Rows per day, peak hour, average and maximum record size, how far back the history goes, how many of them change per day. A design for 10,000 records a day is a different design from one for 10 million.
  4. How fresh does the data need to be?Real time, minutes, hourly, nightly? The honest answer is usually much looser than the first answer, and the difference between "real time" and "within fifteen minutes" is often the difference between a streaming architecture and a cron job.
  5. What happens when it fails?Who notices, who fixes it, is a partial result acceptable, can we replay, is double-processing dangerous? Answer this before you design anything.
  6. What is the change process?How long from "we need a firewall rule" to "the firewall rule exists"? How many change windows per quarter? Who signs off? This determines your project plan more than any technical factor.
  7. Who has done this before here?There is almost always an existing integration with the same system. Find it, read it, and copy its auth pattern. It has already survived the security review.
Find the person, not the documentationEvery enterprise has one person per system who actually knows how it works — usually not the architect, usually someone with "senior analyst" in their title who has been there eleven years. Find them in week one, buy them a coffee, and ask them what breaks. Thirty minutes with that person is worth three weeks of reading Confluence pages last updated in 2019. In SAP landscapes this person is in the Basis team; in Salesforce it is the admin; in the mainframe world they are usually about to retire, which is its own kind of deadline.
The integration tax nobody budgets forEvery integration point adds: a credential that expires, a schema that drifts, a rate limit you will hit, a maintenance window you do not control, a network path that can break, and a team you must coordinate with. Five integration points is not five times the work of one — it is more, because the failure modes combine. When scoping, count integration points, not features, and push hard to reduce the count. The best integration is the one you talked the customer out of needing.

The Four Integration Styles

Hohpe and Woolf identified four fundamental ways to connect applications, and twenty-plus years later the taxonomy is unchanged because it is grounded in physics rather than fashion. Every integration you build is one of these four, or a combination. Knowing the trade-offs lets you argue for the right one instead of defaulting to whatever you used last time.

The four integration styles. Most real enterprise flows combine two or three.

File transfer

One application writes a file; another reads it. It is the oldest style, it is universally supported, and it remains overwhelmingly the most common way enterprises move data between organisations. Every bank, insurer, retailer and manufacturer you will work with has dozens of nightly SFTP jobs running right now. Do not be snobbish about it: file transfer has genuine virtues. It requires no shared runtime, no compatible library versions, no synchronised uptime, and it is trivially auditable — the file is right there, you can open it, you can reprocess it, and the security team understands it.

# A file-drop consumer that survives contact with reality.
# Every one of these guards exists because of a real production incident.
import csv, hashlib, os, shutil, time
from pathlib import Path

INBOX, WORK, ARCHIVE, ERROR = map(Path, ("/data/inbox", "/data/work",
                                         "/data/archive", "/data/error"))
EXPECTED_HEADER = ["invoice_id", "vendor_id", "amount", "currency",
                   "invoice_date", "due_date", "cost_centre"]

def stable(path: Path, quiet_seconds: float = 5.0) -> bool:
    """Guard 1: do not read a file that is still being written."""
    s1 = path.stat()
    time.sleep(quiet_seconds)
    s2 = path.stat()
    return s1.st_size == s2.st_size and s1.st_mtime == s2.st_mtime

def process_drop(path: Path):
    # Guard 2: sentinel file. The producer writes DATA.csv then DATA.csv.done
    sentinel = path.with_suffix(path.suffix + ".done")
    if not sentinel.exists():
        return                                   # not finished yet

    if not stable(path):
        log.warning("file.still_growing", file=path.name)
        return

    # Guard 3: claim the file by MOVING it. Two workers cannot both take it.
    work = WORK / path.name
    try:
        path.rename(work)                        # atomic within a filesystem
        sentinel.unlink(missing_ok=True)
    except FileNotFoundError:
        return                                   # another worker won the race

    digest = hashlib.sha256(work.read_bytes()).hexdigest()
    batch_id = f"{work.stem}:{digest[:16]}"

    # Guard 4: idempotency. A rerun of yesterday's job must not double-post.
    if db.batch_already_processed(batch_id):
        log.warning("file.duplicate_batch", batch_id=batch_id, file=work.name)
        shutil.move(work, ARCHIVE / f"{work.name}.dup")
        return

    # Guard 5: control file. Count and checksum must match before ANY row lands.
    ctrl = INBOX / f"{work.stem}.ctrl"
    expected_rows = expected_sum = None
    if ctrl.exists():
        meta = dict(line.strip().split("=", 1) for line in
                    ctrl.read_text().splitlines() if "=" in line)
        expected_rows = int(meta.get("RECORD_COUNT", -1))
        expected_sum  = meta.get("SHA256")
        if expected_sum and expected_sum.lower() != digest:
            fail(work, batch_id, "checksum_mismatch",
                 expected=expected_sum, actual=digest)
            return

    rows, errors, total_amount = [], [], 0
    with work.open(newline="", encoding="utf-8-sig") as fh:   # BOM: Excel's gift
        reader = csv.DictReader(fh)
        # Guard 6: schema. A silently added column is the classic 3am incident.
        if reader.fieldnames != EXPECTED_HEADER:
            fail(work, batch_id, "header_mismatch",
                 expected=EXPECTED_HEADER, actual=reader.fieldnames)
            return
        for lineno, row in enumerate(reader, start=2):
            try:
                rows.append(validate_invoice(row))
                total_amount += int(round(float(row["amount"]) * 100))
            except Exception as e:
                errors.append({"line": lineno, "error": str(e),
                               "invoice_id": row.get("invoice_id")})

    if expected_rows is not None and len(rows) + len(errors) != expected_rows:
        fail(work, batch_id, "row_count_mismatch",
             expected=expected_rows, actual=len(rows) + len(errors))
        return

    # Guard 7: all-or-nothing threshold. 3 bad rows in 10,000 is normal;
    # 4,000 bad rows means the upstream changed and we must NOT load any.
    if errors and len(errors) / max(len(rows) + len(errors), 1) > 0.05:
        fail(work, batch_id, "too_many_invalid_rows",
             invalid=len(errors), total=len(rows) + len(errors),
             sample=errors[:5])
        return

    # Guard 8: one transaction per file, with the batch recorded inside it.
    with db.transaction():
        db.bulk_upsert_invoices(rows)
        db.record_batch(batch_id, file=work.name, rows=len(rows),
                        rejected=len(errors), amount_minor=total_amount,
                        sha256=digest)

    if errors:
        write_reject_file(ERROR / f"{work.stem}.rejects.csv", errors)
    shutil.move(work, ARCHIVE / f"{time.strftime('%Y/%m/%d')}/{work.name}")
    log.info("file.processed", batch_id=batch_id, rows=len(rows),
             rejected=len(errors), amount_minor=total_amount)
The eight ways a file drop fails in productionPartial read of a file still being written. A rerun that double-posts every invoice. A column silently added or reordered by the upstream team. UTF-8 BOM, CRLF line endings, or a Windows-1252 encoding that makes every accented name a question mark. A field containing an unescaped delimiter — the classic being a company name with a comma. Numbers in scientific notation because the file passed through Excel. An empty file arriving because the upstream job failed but still wrote its output. And the file that simply never arrives, which produces no error at all unless you alert on its absence. Build a guard for each one, and alert on expected file did not arrive by 06:00 — missing data is the failure nobody detects.

Shared database

Two applications read and write the same tables. It is seductive — no serialisation, no network hop, transactional consistency for free — and it is almost always a trap. The schema becomes a shared public API that nobody can version, so no team can change a column without coordinating with every other team, and within two years nobody dares change anything. You will meet this pattern constantly in older estates; your job is usually to avoid extending it.

When a shared database is acceptableRead-only access to a replica, for reporting or for a read model you rebuild from scratch, with a documented understanding that the schema may change and you will break. Or a genuinely shared reference dataset with a stable, versioned schema and a single writer. Everything else — especially two applications writing to the same table — accumulates integrity problems that surface as data corruption two years after you have left.

Remote procedure call

One application calls a function in another and waits for the answer. REST, SOAP, gRPC, GraphQL, SAP RFC — all RPC in different clothes. The mental model is simple, which is its strength and its danger: it makes a network call look like a local call, hiding the fact that it can be slow, can fail partially, can time out after doing the work, and requires both systems to be available simultaneously. That last property, temporal coupling, is the one that hurts in enterprise environments where the other system has a maintenance window you do not control.

Messaging

One application puts a message on a channel; another takes it off, possibly much later. This decouples the two in time (the consumer can be down), in space (the producer need not know who consumes), and in throughput (the queue absorbs bursts). The costs are real: you need a broker, the semantics are eventual rather than immediate, duplicate delivery is a fact of life you must design for, and debugging a flow you cannot follow synchronously is harder. In exchange you get a system where one component's outage degrades rather than destroys.

Synchronous versus Asynchronous

The single most consequential design decision in any integration is whether the caller waits. Get it wrong in the synchronous direction and you build a system that collapses when a dependency slows down. Get it wrong in the asynchronous direction and you build complexity nobody needed for a call that takes eighty milliseconds. There is a decision procedure, and it takes about two minutes.

The decision procedure

  1. Does a human need the answer right now to continue?If yes, synchronous — but only for the part they need. Validate synchronously, process asynchronously.
  2. How long does the work take, honestly, at p99?Under about one second, synchronous is fine. Over about five seconds, a synchronous HTTP call is a liability: gateways time out, browsers give up, connections are held. Over thirty seconds, synchronous is indefensible.
  3. Can the work be retried safely?If retries are safe and cheap, asynchronous becomes much more attractive, because the queue gives you retry semantics for free.
  4. What is the volume profile?Smooth and modest, synchronous is fine. Bursty — 50,000 records at 02:00 on the first of the month — and you need a queue to flatten it, because your downstream is sized for the average and the burst will break it.
  5. What is the availability of the downstream?If it has a four-hour maintenance window every Sunday, a synchronous design means four hours of failures. A queue means four hours of accumulation and a catch-up.
  6. Does the customer's network allow it?You may not be allowed to receive inbound HTTP at all, which removes several options immediately.
The pattern that resolves most argumentsAccept synchronously, process asynchronously, report status by polling or callback. POST /jobs validates the request in 50ms, writes it to a queue, returns 202 Accepted with a Location: /jobs/{id} header. The client polls that URL or receives a webhook. The user gets immediate confirmation that their request was received and valid, the heavy work happens in a system you can scale and retry independently, and no gateway timeout can ever affect it. This design handles both "I need to know it worked" and "this takes four minutes" simultaneously, and it is what I propose by default.

The four interaction patterns

Request / reply

Caller waits for a specific answer. Use for queries and for commands whose outcome the caller needs. Over messaging, implement with a correlation ID and a reply-to queue. Always set a timeout and decide what the caller does when it expires — "assume failure" and "assume success" are both wrong without idempotency.

Fire and forget

Caller sends and continues. Use for notifications, audit events, metrics. The caller gets no confirmation that the work succeeded, so it must not matter — or the channel must be durable enough that the message cannot be lost in transit.

Publish / subscribe

One publisher, N subscribers, publisher does not know who they are. Use for domain events — "InvoiceApproved", "EmployeeHired" — where multiple systems need to react and the set of reactors changes over time. This is how you add a consumer without touching the producer, which in an enterprise is worth a great deal politically.

Point to point

One producer, one logical consumer (possibly many instances, but each message goes to exactly one). Use for work queues where a task must be done once. Competing consumers give you horizontal scaling for free.

Events versus commands — a distinction worth enforcingA command tells a specific system to do something: ProcessInvoice, imperative, one handler, the sender expects it to happen. An event states that something already happened: InvoiceApproved, past tense, zero or more handlers, the publisher does not care who reacts. Mixing them produces systems where a "publisher" secretly depends on one specific subscriber behaving in one specific way, which is a distributed monolith with extra latency. Name your topics in past tense for events and verb-first for commands, and the design discipline follows from the naming.

The Messaging Patterns Toolkit

Hohpe and Woolf catalogued sixty-five integration patterns in 2003. About a dozen do ninety per cent of the work, and they are as relevant in a Kafka-and-Kubernetes estate as they were in an MQ-and-mainframe one. Learning them gives you a vocabulary: when you say "we need a claim check here" to an enterprise architect, you have just saved twenty minutes of explanation and demonstrated that you have done this before.

Claim check, worked

# Problem: a 40 MB scanned PDF must flow through a pipeline whose broker has a
# 256 KB message limit (SQS 256KB, Service Bus 256KB standard / 100MB premium,
# Kafka default 1MB). Do not raise the limit. Use a claim check.

import boto3, json, uuid
from datetime import datetime, timedelta, timezone

s3 = boto3.client("s3")
BUCKET = "acme-integration-payloads"

def publish_large(doc_bytes: bytes, meta: dict) -> dict:
    key = f"claims/{datetime.now(timezone.utc):%Y/%m/%d}/{uuid.uuid4()}.pdf"
    s3.put_object(
        Bucket=BUCKET, Key=key, Body=doc_bytes,
        ContentType=meta["mime"],
        ServerSideEncryption="aws:kms",
        SSEKMSKeyId=KMS_KEY_ARN,
        # Lifecycle must OUTLIVE the queue's maximum retention + DLQ retention.
        # Queue 14d + DLQ 14d -> set this to at least 35 days.
        Metadata={"tenant": meta["tenant"], "sha256": meta["sha256"]},
    )
    message = {                                  # small, routable, inspectable
        "schema": "doc.received.v2",
        "claim_check": {"bucket": BUCKET, "key": key,
                        "bytes": len(doc_bytes), "sha256": meta["sha256"]},
        "doc_id": meta["doc_id"],
        "tenant_id": meta["tenant"],
        "mime": meta["mime"],
        "pages": meta["pages"],
        "received_at": datetime.now(timezone.utc).isoformat(),
    }
    queue.send(json.dumps(message),
               attributes={"schema": "doc.received.v2",
                           "trace_id": current_trace_id()})
    return message

def consume(message: dict) -> bytes:
    cc = message["claim_check"]
    try:
        obj = s3.get_object(Bucket=cc["bucket"], Key=cc["key"])
    except s3.exceptions.NoSuchKey:
        # The classic claim-check failure: the blob expired before the message
        # was consumed, usually after a long DLQ sojourn. Make it explicit.
        raise ClaimCheckExpired(
            f"payload {cc['key']} no longer exists; message age "
            f"{message_age(message)}; check the bucket lifecycle policy")
    body = obj["Body"].read()
    # Verify integrity — the message and the blob can drift
    if hashlib.sha256(body).hexdigest() != cc["sha256"]:
        raise PayloadCorrupt(f"sha256 mismatch for {cc['key']}")
    return body

Saga, worked

PYTHON
# A saga for "approve and pay an invoice" across four systems that have no
# shared transaction. Each step has a compensating action. State is persisted
# after every step, because the orchestrator itself can crash.

from enum import Enum

class Step(str, Enum):
    VALIDATE       = "validate"
    RESERVE_BUDGET = "reserve_budget"      # compensate: release_budget
    POST_TO_ERP    = "post_to_erp"         # compensate: reverse_posting
    INITIATE_PAY   = "initiate_payment"    # compensate: cancel_payment (TIME-LIMITED)
    NOTIFY         = "notify"              # compensate: none possible

SAGA = [
    (Step.VALIDATE,       validate_invoice,  None),
    (Step.RESERVE_BUDGET, reserve_budget,    release_budget),
    (Step.POST_TO_ERP,    post_to_erp,       reverse_posting),
    (Step.INITIATE_PAY,   initiate_payment,  cancel_payment),
    (Step.NOTIFY,         notify_requester,  None),
]

def run_saga(saga_id: str, ctx: dict):
    state = store.load(saga_id) or {"completed": [], "ctx": ctx}
    try:
        for step, action, _ in SAGA:
            if step in state["completed"]:
                continue                        # resume after a crash
            # Every action must be idempotent and keyed by (saga_id, step)
            result = action(saga_id, state["ctx"])
            state["ctx"].update(result or {})
            state["completed"].append(step)
            store.save(saga_id, state)          # persist BEFORE the next step
            log.info("saga.step.ok", saga_id=saga_id, step=step)
        store.mark_complete(saga_id)
    except Exception as e:
        log.error("saga.step.failed", saga_id=saga_id,
                  step=step, error=str(e))
        compensate(saga_id, state, failed_at=step)
        raise

def compensate(saga_id: str, state: dict, failed_at: Step):
    """Undo completed steps in REVERSE order. Compensation must be idempotent
    and must itself be retried — a failed compensation is an escalation."""
    for step, _, undo in reversed(SAGA):
        if step not in state["completed"] or undo is None:
            continue
        for attempt in range(1, 6):
            try:
                undo(saga_id, state["ctx"])
                log.warning("saga.compensated", saga_id=saga_id, step=step)
                break
            except Exception as ce:
                log.error("saga.compensation.failed", saga_id=saga_id,
                          step=step, attempt=attempt, error=str(ce))
                if attempt == 5:
                    # This is the case that needs a human, immediately.
                    alert_oncall("SAGA_COMPENSATION_FAILED",
                                 saga_id=saga_id, step=step,
                                 detail="Manual intervention required: "
                                        "financial state may be inconsistent")
                    store.mark_needs_intervention(saga_id, step)
                    return
                time.sleep(2 ** attempt)
    store.mark_compensated(saga_id)
Compensation is not rollbackA database rollback leaves no trace. A compensation is a new business transaction that reverses the effect of a previous one, and it is visible: the ERP shows a posting and a reversal, the customer's bank shows a payment and a refund, the recipient already received the email. Three consequences. One, compensations must be acceptable to the business — get the finance team to agree that a reversal document is fine before you design around it. Two, some steps cannot be compensated at all (an email sent, a physical item shipped), so order your saga to put irreversible steps last. Three, compensation can fail, and when it does you have an inconsistent state across systems that only a human can fix — so that path must page someone and must be in the runbook.

THE SAP CSRF DANCE — required for every POST/PUT/DELETE, trips up everyone
  1. GET any entity with header  x-csrf-token: Fetch
  2. Read the response header    x-csrf-token: <value>  AND keep the cookies
  3. Send the write request with x-csrf-token: <value>  AND the same cookies
  4. On 403 with x-csrf-token: Required -> the token expired, repeat from 1

import httpx
from urllib.parse import urljoin

class ODataClient:
    """Handles CSRF, session cookies, paging and v2/v4 shape differences."""

    def __init__(self, base: str, auth, version: str = "v2", verify=None):
        self.base = base.rstrip("/") + "/"
        self.version = version
        self.client = httpx.Client(
            auth=auth, verify=verify,                 # verify = path to their CA
            timeout=httpx.Timeout(30.0, connect=10.0),
            headers={"Accept": "application/json"},
            follow_redirects=False,                   # SAP redirects can drop auth
        )
        self._csrf: str | None = None

    def _fetch_csrf(self, entity: str):
        r = self.client.get(urljoin(self.base, entity),
                            params={"$top": 1},
                            headers={"x-csrf-token": "Fetch"})
        r.raise_for_status()
        self._csrf = r.headers.get("x-csrf-token")
        if not self._csrf:
            raise RuntimeError("Gateway did not return a CSRF token. The service "
                               "may not be activated in /IWFND/MAINT_SERVICE.")

    def get_all(self, entity: str, **params):
        """Follow nextLink. NEVER loop on $skip without a stable $orderby."""
        params.setdefault("$format", "json")
        url, first = urljoin(self.base, entity), True
        pages = 0
        while url:
            r = self.client.get(url, params=params if first else None)
            self._raise_for_odata_error(r)
            body = r.json()
            if self.version == "v2":
                d = body["d"]
                items = d.get("results", [d])
                url = d.get("__next")
            else:
                items = body.get("value", [])
                url = body.get("@odata.nextLink")
            yield from items
            first = False
            pages += 1
            if pages > 10_000:                     # guard against an infinite loop
                raise RuntimeError("pagination exceeded 10,000 pages; "
                                   "the service is probably returning the same "
                                   "nextLink repeatedly")

    def create(self, entity: str, payload: dict, retry_csrf: bool = True):
        if not self._csrf:
            self._fetch_csrf(entity)
        r = self.client.post(urljoin(self.base, entity), json=payload,
                             headers={"x-csrf-token": self._csrf,
                                      "Content-Type": "application/json"})
        if r.status_code == 403 and \
           r.headers.get("x-csrf-token", "").lower() == "required" and retry_csrf:
            self._csrf = None
            return self.create(entity, payload, retry_csrf=False)
        self._raise_for_odata_error(r)
        return r.json()

    @staticmethod
    def _raise_for_odata_error(r: httpx.Response):
        if r.is_success:
            return
        # SAP returns rich errors. Surface them — "400 Bad Request" alone is useless.
        try:
            err = r.json()["error"]
            msg = err.get("message")
            msg = msg.get("value") if isinstance(msg, dict) else msg
            code = err.get("code")
            details = err.get("innererror", {}).get("errordetails", [])
            raise ODataError(f"{r.status_code} {code}: {msg}; "
                             f"details={[d.get('message') for d in details][:3]}")
        except (ValueError, KeyError):
            raise ODataError(f"{r.status_code}: {r.text[:500]}")
$skip-based pagination is unsafe on live dataIf you page with $top=1000&$skip=0, then $skip=1000, and rows are inserted or deleted between the two calls, you will skip records and duplicate others — silently. The reconciliation job catches it three weeks later and nobody can explain it. Always either follow the server's nextLink/skiptoken (which uses a stable cursor), or page on a monotonic key: $filter=Id gt '<lastId>'&$orderby=Id&$top=1000. The second approach also makes your job resumable after a crash, which matters for an eight-hour initial load.

    ("customer_name",  13, 35, "A"),

def parse_signed_overpunch(raw: str) -> Decimal:
    """Mainframe zoned-decimal: the sign is encoded in the LAST character.
    '1234J' = -12341 ... this is not a joke and you will meet it."""
    POS = {"{": "0", "A": "1", "B": "2", "C": "3", "D": "4",
           "E": "5", "F": "6", "G": "7", "H": "8", "I": "9"}
    NEG = {"}": "0", "J": "1", "K": "2", "L": "3", "M": "4",
           "N": "5", "O": "6", "P": "7", "Q": "8", "R": "9"}
    body, last = raw[:-1], raw[-1]
    if last in POS:  return Decimal(body + POS[last])
    if last in NEG:  return Decimal("-" + body + NEG[last])
    return Decimal(raw)

def parse_line(line: str) -> dict:
    out = {}
    for name, start, length, kind in LAYOUT:
        raw = line[start - 1: start - 1 + length]
        if   kind == "A":  out[name] = raw.strip()
        elif kind == "N":  out[name] = int(raw or 0)
        elif kind == "D":  out[name] = (datetime.strptime(raw, "%Y%m%d").date()
                                        if raw.strip() and raw != "00000000" else None)
        elif kind.startswith("S"):
            dp = int(kind[1:])
            out[name] = parse_signed_overpunch(raw.strip()) / (10 ** dp)
    return out

Webhooks

A webhook is an HTTP callback: when something happens in their system, they POST to your URL. It is the cheapest way to get near-real-time events out of a SaaS platform, and it is also the integration most likely to lose data quietly, because the sender's idea of "delivered" is a 200 response and nothing else. You will both consume webhooks and build them, and the engineering is asymmetric.

Consuming webhooks safely

  1. Verify the signature before anything else.Your endpoint is on the public internet, or at least reachable by whatever can reach your gateway. Anyone can POST to it. Use HMAC verification with a constant-time comparison, against the raw request body, before parsing.
  2. Check the timestamp.A valid signature on a replayed payload from last Tuesday is still valid. Reject anything outside a five-minute window and keep a short-lived cache of seen event IDs.
  3. Return 200 fast and do the work later.Most providers time out at 3–10 seconds and will retry or disable your endpoint if you are slow. Validate, persist to a queue, return. Never do business logic inside the request.
  4. Be idempotent.Every provider will deliver the same event twice eventually — at-least-once is the industry norm. Deduplicate on the provider's event ID.
  5. Do not trust the payload's contents.Many providers deliberately send thin payloads. Even when the payload is fat, treat it as a hint and fetch the current state from the API, because webhooks arrive out of order and a stale update can overwrite a newer one.
  6. Handle out-of-order delivery.Compare a version or updated_at field and discard anything older than what you already have.
  7. Have a reconciliation path.Webhooks will be missed — the provider had an outage, your endpoint was down past their retry limit, a firewall rule changed. A periodic poll of "everything changed since T" is not optional; it is the safety net.
PYTHON
import hmac, hashlib, time, json
from fastapi import APIRouter, Request, Response, HTTPException

router = APIRouter()
TOLERANCE_S = 300

@router.post("/webhooks/crm")
async def receive(request: Request):
    raw = await request.body()          # RAW bytes. Never re-serialise the parsed
                                        # JSON before verifying — key order changes
                                        # and the signature will never match.
    sig_header = request.headers.get("X-Signature", "")
    ts_header  = request.headers.get("X-Timestamp", "")

    # --- 1. Replay window -------------------------------------------------
    try:
        ts = int(ts_header)
    except ValueError:
        raise HTTPException(400, "missing or invalid timestamp")
    if abs(time.time() - ts) > TOLERANCE_S:
        WEBHOOK_REJECTED.labels(reason="stale").inc()
        raise HTTPException(400, "timestamp outside tolerance")

    # --- 2. Signature over timestamp + body (prevents detaching the body) --
    signed_payload = f"{ts}.".encode() + raw
    expected = hmac.new(WEBHOOK_SECRET, signed_payload, hashlib.sha256).hexdigest()
    provided = sig_header.removeprefix("sha256=")
    if not hmac.compare_digest(expected, provided):      # constant time
        WEBHOOK_REJECTED.labels(reason="bad_signature").inc()
        log.warning("webhook.bad_signature",
                    remote=request.client.host, bytes=len(raw))
        raise HTTPException(401, "invalid signature")

    # --- 3. Parse only AFTER verification ---------------------------------
    try:
        event = json.loads(raw)
    except json.JSONDecodeError:
        raise HTTPException(400, "invalid JSON")

    event_id = event.get("id")
    if not event_id:
        raise HTTPException(400, "missing event id")

    # --- 4. Idempotency. SETNX with a TTL longer than the provider's
    #        maximum retry window (Stripe: 3 days; most: 24h).
    if not await redis.set(f"wh:crm:{event_id}", "1", nx=True, ex=4 * 86400):
        WEBHOOK_DUPLICATE.inc()
        return Response(status_code=200)      # 200, not 409 — it IS handled

    # --- 5. Persist and return. Work happens in a consumer.
    await outbox.insert(topic="crm.event.received", key=event_id, payload=event)
    WEBHOOK_ACCEPTED.labels(type=event.get("type", "unknown")).inc()
    return Response(status_code=200)          # under 100ms, every time
Signature schemes differ; read their docs, do not guessStripe signs timestamp.body with HMAC-SHA256 and sends t=…,v1=… in one header. GitHub signs the raw body and sends sha256=… in X-Hub-Signature-256. Slack signs v0:timestamp:body. Shopify base64-encodes the HMAC rather than hex-encoding it. Twilio signs the URL plus sorted POST parameters. Microsoft Graph uses a validation token handshake on subscription creation and a clientState value thereafter. Getting this wrong fails closed, which is at least safe — but you will spend an afternoon on it, so budget for that and write an integration test with a captured real payload.

        return True

        # Permanent: retrying will never help. 410 Gone means unsubscribe.
        await subs.record_permanent_failure(sub.id, r.status_code)
        if r.status_code == 410:
            await subs.deactivate(sub.id, reason="endpoint_gone")
        return False
    return await _schedule_retry(sub, event, attempt, reason=str(r.status_code))

async def _schedule_retry(sub, event, attempt, reason):
    if attempt + 1 >= len(RETRY_SCHEDULE_S):
        await dlq.insert(subscription_id=sub.id, event=event, reason=reason)
        await subs.increment_failures(sub.id)
        # Auto-disable after sustained failure, and TELL the customer — an
        # endpoint that has been silently disabled for a month is a disaster.
        if sub.consecutive_failures >= 50:
            await subs.deactivate(sub.id, reason="too_many_failures")
            await notify_customer_admin(sub, "Your webhook endpoint has been "
                                             "disabled after 50 consecutive "
                                             "failures. Events are retained "
                                             "for 7 days and can be replayed.")
        return False
    delay = RETRY_SCHEDULE_S[attempt + 1] * random.uniform(0.8, 1.2)   # jitter
    await scheduler.enqueue_at(time.time() + delay, deliver, sub, event, attempt + 1)
    return False

Message Brokers and Event Streaming

You will not usually choose the broker. The customer has one — often three, inherited from different eras — and your job is to use it correctly. But you must understand the differences, because the delivery semantics, ordering guarantees and failure modes vary enormously, and designing for Kafka semantics on top of a queue (or vice versa) produces subtle data loss.

Kafka concepts that determine your design

TEXT
TOPIC -> PARTITIONS -> OFFSETS

  topic "invoice.approved.v1", 6 partitions
    P0: [0][1][2][3][4]...        consumer-group "erp-sync"   offset 3
    P1: [0][1][2]...              consumer-group "analytics"  offset 0
    ...

  PARTITION COUNT is the unit of parallelism. A consumer group can have at
  most as many ACTIVE consumers as there are partitions. 6 partitions means
  a maximum of 6 parallel consumers; the 7th sits idle. You can increase
  partitions later but NOT decrease, and increasing breaks key-ordering for
  existing keys because the hash-to-partition mapping changes.

  KEY determines the partition: partition = hash(key) % num_partitions.
    key = invoice_id   -> all events for one invoice are ordered, spread evenly
    key = tenant_id    -> ordered per tenant, but one huge tenant creates a
                          HOT PARTITION and that consumer falls behind alone
    key = null         -> round robin, no ordering guarantee at all

  ORDERING is guaranteed ONLY within a partition. There is no global order.
  If your design needs global ordering you need one partition, which means
  one consumer, which means no horizontal scaling. Usually the right answer
  is to find a key that gives you the ordering you actually need.

  CONSUMER GROUPS are independent. Adding a new group does not affect existing
  ones and starts from the configured offset. This is the property that makes
  Kafka politically useful in an enterprise: you can add a consumer without
  asking the producing team for anything.

  REBALANCING happens when a consumer joins, leaves or times out. During a
  rebalance, consumption STOPS for the group. Long processing times trigger
  max.poll.interval.ms and cause a rebalance storm:
      consumer takes 6 min to process a batch
      -> exceeds max.poll.interval.ms (default 5 min)
      -> broker declares it dead, rebalances
      -> another consumer gets the same messages, also takes 6 min
      -> permanent rebalancing, zero progress, growing lag
  FIX: smaller max.poll.records, longer max.poll.interval.ms, or hand work to
  a thread pool and pause()/resume() the partition.

Exactly-once is mostly a marketing termKafka's exactly-once semantics are real but narrow: they apply to a read-process-write cycle entirely within Kafka, using transactions, with isolation.level=read_committed. The moment your consumer writes to an external system — a database, SAP, an HTTP API — you are back to at-least-once, because there is no distributed transaction across Kafka and SAP. The correct engineering position is: assume at-least-once delivery and make every consumer idempotent. That is achievable, testable and explainable to a customer. Promising exactly-once is a promise you cannot keep, and the day a network blip causes a duplicate posting in their general ledger is the day that promise is examined.

The transactional outbox

The dual-write problem: you need to update your database and publish an event. If you write the database then publish, a crash in between loses the event. If you publish then write, a crash loses the database change while the world has been told it happened. There is no ordering that fixes it, because the two systems have no shared transaction. The outbox pattern solves it by making the publish part of the database transaction.

-- 1. The outbox table lives in the SAME database as your business data
CREATE TABLE outbox (
  id             BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
  aggregate_type TEXT        NOT NULL,     -- 'invoice'
  aggregate_id   TEXT        NOT NULL,     -- '4411-2026'

  last_error     TEXT
);

#    (a) polling — simple, portable, works anywhere, ~1s latency

def relay_tick(batch: int = 500):
    with db.transaction():
        rows = db.query("""
            SELECT id, event_type, aggregate_id, payload, headers
            FROM outbox
            WHERE published_at IS NULL
            ORDER BY id                       -- preserves per-aggregate order
            LIMIT %s
            FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED            -- lets N relay instances run safely
        """, (batch,))

        for r in rows:
            try:
                producer.produce(
                    topic=r["event_type"],
                    key=r["aggregate_id"].encode(),
                    value=json.dumps(r["payload"]).encode(),
                    headers=[(k, str(v).encode()) for k, v in r["headers"].items()]
                            + [("outbox_id", str(r["id"]).encode())],
                )
            except BufferError:
                producer.flush(5)
                raise                          # retry the whole batch next tick

        producer.flush(30)                     # block until brokers acked ALL
        db.execute("UPDATE outbox SET published_at = now() WHERE id = ANY(%s)",
                   ([r["id"] for r in rows],))

# NOTE: this is at-least-once. If the process dies after flush() but before the
# UPDATE, the batch is republished. That is correct and expected — consumers
# are idempotent. The outbox guarantees NO LOST EVENTS, not no duplicates.

# 4. Housekeeping — the step everyone forgets until the table is 400 GB

Consumer lag is the health metric for any streaming integrationLag is the difference between the latest offset in a partition and the consumer group's committed offset — how far behind you are, in messages. Expose it (kafka_consumergroup_lag from kafka_exporter, or Burrow), graph it per partition, and alert on sustained growth rather than absolute value, because the absolute number is meaningless without the processing rate. Also alert on lag in seconds rather than messages where you can — "we are 40 minutes behind" is a sentence the business understands and "we are 180,000 messages behind" is not. A single hot partition with growing lag while the others are at zero is the classic symptom of a bad partition key.

Idempotency has a scope and a window — state both"This endpoint is idempotent" is incomplete. Idempotent for how long? Scoped to which tenant? Keyed on what? A payment API that dedupes for 24 hours is not idempotent against a replay you run after a three-day outage. Write it into the integration specification: "Idempotency keys are honoured for 7 days, scoped per tenant, and a repeated key with a different payload returns 409." That last clause matters — silently returning the original result for a different payload hides a client bug.

Dead letter queues and the human process

A DLQ is where messages go when they cannot be processed. The technical part is easy. The part that fails in practice is that nobody owns the DLQ, nobody looks at it, and six months later it contains 40,000 messages representing real business transactions that never happened.

  • Every DLQ has a named owner and an alert on depth greater than zero, or on age of the oldest message.
  • Messages in the DLQ carry the failure reason, the original topic/queue, the attempt count, the timestamp and the trace ID — in headers, so you do not have to parse the body to triage.
  • There is a documented process: who triages, within what SLA, what the categories are, and how to replay.
  • There is a replay tool, tested, that can re-inject a selected subset after a fix — not a manual copy-paste.
  • Replaying is idempotent, so re-processing a message that partially succeeded is safe.
  • DLQ messages are retained long enough to be useful (14+ days) and are included in the reconciliation report.
  • The DLQ itself has a DLQ policy, or at least an alert, so a failing replay does not loop forever.

Reconciliation — the control that catches what monitoring misses

Every integration that moves business data needs a reconciliation job. Monitoring tells you that the pipeline is running; reconciliation tells you that the data is correct. They are different questions and the second one is what the customer's finance team cares about.

# Daily reconciliation. Run it, publish the result, and alert on variance.
# This single job has found more real problems than every dashboard I have built.

def reconcile(day: date) -> dict:
    src = source_system.aggregate(day)      # count + sum from the SOURCE
    tgt = target_system.aggregate(day)      # count + sum from the TARGET
    dlq_count = dlq_depth_for(day)
    rejected  = reject_file_count(day)

    report = {
        "date": day.isoformat(),
        "source_count":  src.count,
        "target_count":  tgt.count,
        "count_variance": src.count - tgt.count,
        "source_amount_minor": src.amount,
        "target_amount_minor": tgt.amount,
        "amount_variance":     src.amount - tgt.amount,
        "in_dlq":    dlq_count,
        "rejected":  rejected,
        # The accounting identity that must hold:
        "unexplained": src.count - tgt.count - dlq_count - rejected,
    }

    if report["unexplained"] != 0:
        # Records vanished without appearing anywhere. This is the alert that
        # matters; it means silent data loss.
        alert("RECONCILIATION_UNEXPLAINED_VARIANCE", severity="critical", **report)
        # Produce the actual list so a human can act, not just a count
        missing = set(src.ids) - set(tgt.ids) - set(dlq_ids(day)) - set(reject_ids(day))
        write_report(f"/reports/recon/{day}/missing_ids.csv", sorted(missing))
        report["missing_sample"] = sorted(missing)[:50]
    elif report["amount_variance"] != 0:
        # Counts match but money does not: a transformation bug — rounding,
        # currency, sign, or a unit mismatch (pounds vs pence).
        alert("RECONCILIATION_AMOUNT_VARIANCE", severity="critical", **report)

    RECON_VARIANCE.labels(integration="invoice-sync").set(report["unexplained"])
    publish_report(report)
    return report

    + add a new enum value IF consumers ignore unknown values (design for this)
    + widen a type (int32 -> int64, string length)
    + add a new event type on a new topic

  BREAKING — requires a new version
    - remove a field            - rename a field
    - change a type             - make an optional field required
    - change the meaning of a field while keeping its name   <-- the worst one,
      because nothing detects it: "amount" silently becomes gross instead of net

  VERSIONING STRATEGY THAT WORKS IN ENTERPRISES
    topic/endpoint name carries the major version:  invoice.approved.v1
    payload carries the exact schema id:            "schema":"invoice.approved.v1.3"
    v1 and v2 run IN PARALLEL during migration — publish to both, let consumers
    migrate on their own change calendar, retire v1 only when all consumers
    have confirmed. In an enterprise this takes 6-18 months. Plan for it.

  USE A SCHEMA REGISTRY if the customer has one (Confluent, Apicurio, AWS Glue).
  Set compatibility to BACKWARD or FULL and let the registry REJECT a breaking
  change at publish time rather than at 3am in a consumer you have never heard of.

2. RFC and BAPI

Remote Function Call is SAP's native RPC. BAPIs are the subset of function modules blessed as a stable business API — BAPI_SALESORDER_CREATEFROMDAT2, BAPI_CUSTOMER_GETLIST. You need the SAP NetWeaver RFC SDK (a licensed download from SAP, which you must get the customer to provide) and network access to the gateway ports.

# pyrfc requires the NetWeaver RFC SDK on the machine. Build it into your image.

                                  for m in errors))

    #    Forgetting this is THE classic SAP integration bug: everything looks
    #    successful, the order number is returned, and nothing is saved.
    conn.call("BAPI_TRANSACTION_COMMIT", WAIT="X")   # WAIT='X' = synchronous

except CommunicationError as e:

    log.error("sap.comm_error", message=e.message)
    raise
finally:
    conn.close()

4. CDS views and direct HANA access

Core Data Services views are SQL-like semantic views over SAP tables, and in S/4HANA they are the intended read model. Some are exposed as OData automatically (annotated @OData.publish: true); others can be read over SQL if you are granted HANA access. Direct table reads (SE16N-style, or JDBC to tables like VBAK, BSEG, MARA) are technically possible and almost always the wrong answer — you bypass all business logic, the table structures are not a stable contract, and you will be blamed for every subsequent data problem.

5. SAP Integration Suite (CPI)

SAP's own iPaaS, on BTP. If the customer has it, they will very likely require you to route through it rather than talking to SAP directly, because it is where their integration governance, monitoring and security policies live. Learn the vocabulary: integration flow (iFlow), adapters (HTTPS, OData, SFTP, SOAP, IDoc, JMS), message mapping, content modifier, groovy script, value mapping.

Using a DIALOG user. Insist on a SYSTEM or COMMUNICATION user. Reading tables directly instead of using an API.     if select: params["$select"] = select

# DELTA QUERY — the right way to sync. First call returns everything plus a
# deltaLink; subsequent calls with that link return ONLY changes. This is the
# difference between a 6-hour full scan and a 20-second incremental.
def delta_sync(drive_id: str, state_key: str):
    link = state.get(state_key) or \
        f"https://graph.microsoft.com/v1.0/drives/{drive_id}/root/delta"
    token = cred.get_token("https://graph.microsoft.com/.default").token
    with httpx.Client(timeout=60) as c:
        while True:
            r = c.get(link, headers={"Authorization": f"Bearer {token}"})
            r.raise_for_status()
            body = r.json()
            for item in body.get("value", []):
                if "deleted" in item:
                    handle_delete(item["id"])
                else:
                    handle_upsert(item)

Workday

Auth is OAuth 2.0 or (still) WS-Security on SOAP. The APIs are SOAP-first (the Human Resources web service WSDL is enormous), with REST and RaaS available. RaaS — Reports as a Service — is the pragmatic path: a Workday analyst builds a custom report, enables web service access, and you consume it as JSON, CSV or XML over a simple URL. Microsoft Dynamics 365

Dataverse Web API (OData v4) plus Entra ID auth. The trap is the difference between logical names and display names, option-set integer values that differ per environment, and the $batch endpoint being the only sane way to do bulk operations (up to 1,000 changes per batch, in a changeset that is transactional). Plugins and workflows fire on your writes, so a bulk import can trigger thousands of background jobs and bring the environment down — always ask whether to bypass custom logic with the MSCRM.BypassCustomPluginExecution header, and get permission before you use it.

The universal SaaS checklistFor every platform, answer these before writing code: What is the auth mechanism and what expires, when? What is the rate limit, is it shared with other integrations, and how do I observe my consumption? What is the bulk path for the first full load? What is the incremental path (delta token, change stream, modified-since filter)? How do deletes propagate — do I get a deletion event, or do I have to detect absence? What is the sandbox, and how closely does it match production? What is the field-level permission model, and will it silently filter my results? Which fields are PII and what is the retention policy? Legacy and the Long Tail

The systems that matter most are frequently the oldest. The warehouse runs on an AS/400 application written in RPG; the general ledger posts through a nightly batch on a mainframe; the pricing team maintains a spreadsheet that six departments depend on. This is not a failure of the customer's architecture — it is thirty years of accumulated business logic that works. Your job is to integrate with it respectfully, not to propose replacing it.

Alert on the file that did NOT arriveEvery monitoring system detects errors. Almost none detect absence. If the customer's nightly extract fails upstream, no file appears, no error is raised, and your pipeline reports perfect health while the business quietly runs on yesterday's data. Implement a "deadline monitor": for each expected feed, record the expected arrival window and alert when nothing has arrived by the deadline. Export it as a metric — feed_last_received_timestamp_seconds{feed="invoices"} — and alert on time() - feed_last_received_timestamp_seconds > expected_interval * 1.5. This one alert catches more real incidents than any other in a file-based integration.

Excel as an interface

It will happen. A business team maintains a spreadsheet that is genuinely the system of record for something — pricing tiers, cost centre mappings, approval thresholds — and your integration must consume it. Resist for about ten minutes, then build it properly, because the alternative is that they email it to someone who types it in.

def read_mapping_sheet(path: str) -> dict:
    """Consume a business-maintained workbook defensively."""
    wb = openpyxl.load_workbook(path, data_only=True, read_only=True)
    # data_only=True returns the CACHED value of formulas. If the file was
    # written by a tool rather than Excel, that cache is EMPTY and every
    # formula cell returns None. Detect it and fail with a clear message.
    if "CostCentreMap" not in wb.sheetnames:
        raise ValueError(f"Sheet 'CostCentreMap' missing. Found: {wb.sheetnames}")
    ws = wb["CostCentreMap"]

    header = [str(c.value).strip() if c.value else "" for c in next(ws.rows)]
    required = {"SourceCode", "TargetCostCentre", "ValidFrom"}
    missing = required - set(header)
    if missing:
        raise ValueError(f"Columns missing: {sorted(missing)}. Present: {header}")

    idx = {name: i for i, name in enumerate(header)}
    mapping, problems = {}, []
    for rowno, row in enumerate(ws.iter_rows(min_row=2, values_only=True), start=2):
        if all(v is None for v in row):
            continue                                  # blank spacer row
        src = row[idx["SourceCode"]]
        if src is None:
            continue
        src = str(src).strip()
        # Excel helpfully turns "00123" into the number 123. Restore it.
        if src.isdigit() and len(src) < 5:
            src = src.zfill(5)
        tgt = row[idx["TargetCostCentre"]]
        if tgt is None:
            problems.append(f"row {rowno}: SourceCode {src} has no target")
            continue
        if src in mapping:
            problems.append(f"row {rowno}: duplicate SourceCode {src}")
        mapping[src] = str(tgt).strip()

    if problems:
        # Email the business owner the EXACT rows to fix. Do not silently drop.
        notify_owner("CostCentreMap validation", problems)
    if len(problems) > len(mapping) * 0.1:
        raise ValueError(f"{len(problems)} problems in {len(mapping)} rows — "
                         f"refusing to load. See the emailed report.")
    return mapping

Databases as Integration Points

"Just give us read access to the database" is the request that sounds reasonable and is usually a trap. Sometimes it is the right answer. Knowing which is which, and knowing the modern alternative — log-based change data capture — separates an integration engineer from someone who writes SELECT statements.

Why direct database access is usually wrong

You bypass the business logic

An application's tables are its internal state, not its API. The status column means something only in combination with three other tables and a rule implemented in code. Reading it directly means reimplementing that logic and getting it subtly wrong.

The schema is not a contract

The owning team will rename a column in a routine release, because from their perspective it is private. They are right. Your integration breaks and the postmortem concludes it was your fault for depending on it.

You create load you do not control

A badly written join against a production OLTP database at 09:00 on a Monday is an outage with your name on it. DBAs know this, which is why they resist.

Polling does not scale and misses deletes

WHERE updated_at > :last requires a reliable updated_at (often missing or set by triggers that do not fire on bulk updates), misses hard deletes entirely, and either polls too often (load) or too rarely (latency).

When it is acceptableA read-only replica, with a dedicated login, a statement timeout, a resource governor or workload group, agreed query patterns, and written acknowledgement that the schema may change. Or a purpose-built view or materialised view that the owning team maintains as a deliberate, versioned interface — which is really an API that happens to speak SQL, and that is fine.

Change data capture with Debezium

CDC reads the database's own transaction log — PostgreSQL WAL, MySQL binlog, SQL Server CDC tables, Oracle LogMiner or XStream — and emits a stream of row-level change events. It captures inserts, updates and deletes, in commit order, with before and after images, at very low cost to the source database because reading the log is what replicas do anyway.

The replication slot is a loaded gunIn PostgreSQL, a logical replication slot makes the database retain WAL segments until the consumer confirms it has read them. If your Debezium connector stops — a crash, a Kafka outage, an expired credential, a long weekend — WAL accumulates and the source database's disk fills. When it fills, the customer's production ERP stops accepting writes. This is a genuine, documented way to take down a system you were only supposed to read from. Mitigations, all of them, not one: monitor pg_replication_slots.confirmed_flush_lsn lag and alert hard at a few gigabytes; set max_slot_wal_keep_size (PostgreSQL 13+) so the database drops the slot rather than dying; configure a heartbeat query so low-traffic tables still advance the slot; and have a documented runbook for dropping the slot and re-snapshotting. Tell the DBA about all of this before you create the slot, in writing. They will respect you for it.

CDC gives you rows, not eventsA change event says "the status column changed from approved to paid". Build versus buy versus "live with theirs"

  1. If the customer has a platform and a team, use it for flows that fit it.Simple mapping, routing and scheduled transfers belong there. You inherit their monitoring, their credential vault, their change process and — crucially — their operational ownership after you leave.
  2. Write code for anything with real logic.Complex business rules, anything iterative, anything that needs proper testing, anything performance-sensitive. Expressing a twelve-branch algorithm in a visual designer produces something nobody can review or maintain.
  3. Never split one flow across both.Half in Boomi and half in your service is the worst outcome: two debugging surfaces, two deployment processes, two teams, and an error-handling seam where messages vanish. Pick the boundary at a clean interface — their platform calls your HTTP endpoint, or publishes to a queue you consume.
  4. Expose a clean interface either way.Whether you build or they build, the contract between the two sides is a documented API or message schema with a version, not a shared database table or an undocumented file format.
  5. Count the cost of their platform honestly.Per-connection, per-message or per-flow licensing is real money, and some platforms charge per endpoint. If your design creates 40 new connections, find out what that costs before you present it.
How to win the "use our ESB" conversationDo not argue about technology. Ask three questions: what is the lead time for a change in the platform, who operates it after go-live, and what happens when a message fails at 2am? If the answers are "six weeks", "the integration CoE" and "they triage it next business day", and your use case needs faster iteration or real-time error handling, you have a factual basis for an exception — and you can offer a compromise: your service handles the real-time path, their platform handles the batch and governance path, with a documented interface between them. That proposal gets approved far more often than "we would prefer to write our own".

Secrets

  • Secrets live in the customer's vault — Azure Key Vault, HashiCorp Vault, AWS Secrets Manager, CyberArk — not in environment variables baked into an image, not in a config file, never in git.
  • Prefer workload identity (managed identity, IRSA, workload identity federation) so that there is no secret at all. This is the answer that makes security reviewers happy and it is increasingly available.
  • Every credential has a recorded expiry date and an owner, and there is a calendar reminder 30 days before. Expired credentials are a top-three cause of integration outages, and they always expire on a holiday.
  • Rotation must be possible without downtime: support two valid credentials simultaneously during the overlap, or re-read the secret on a schedule rather than only at startup.
  • Expose credential expiry as a metric: integration_credential_expiry_timestamp_seconds{system="sap"}, and alert at 30, 14 and 7 days.
  • Secrets never appear in logs, traces, error messages, support bundles or exception payloads. Test this explicitly by logging an object that contains one.
  • Scope every credential to the minimum: specific objects, specific operations, read-only where possible, IP-restricted where supported.

Network controls you will negotiate

IP allow-listing

They will want a fixed set of source IPs. In cloud, that means a NAT gateway with static IPs or a dedicated egress. Agree the list, document it, and commit to notice before changes — then actually give notice, because a surprise IP change is an outage.

Forward proxy

All outbound through proxy.corp:8080, often with authentication and TLS inspection. Your HTTP client, your SDK, your container runtime and your language runtime all need to be told, and they all use different variables. Test the full chain.

mTLS

Both sides present certificates. Needs a PKI decision: their internal CA, a public CA, or self-signed with pinning. Ask who issues, what the lifetime is, and what the renewal process is — then put the expiry in your metrics.

Private connectivity

Private Link, VPC peering, ExpressRoute, VPN. Slower to set up (weeks) but often the only acceptable option for data-sensitive flows. Get the CIDR ranges early to avoid overlap.

TLS inspection

A middlebox terminates and re-signs TLS. Your code sees their CA, not the real one. Certificate pinning breaks. The fix is to trust their CA explicitly — never to disable verification.

DLP and egress filtering

Data loss prevention agents may block payloads containing patterns that look like card numbers or national IDs — including your legitimate business data. Find out early; the error is usually an opaque connection reset.

Data governance

  • Classify every field you move: public, internal, confidential, restricted/PII/PHI. Put it in a table in the integration specification. Reviewers approve documents, not intentions.
  • Minimise: transfer only the fields the target genuinely needs. "We take the whole record because it is easier" is both a security finding and an unnecessary coupling.
  • Know the lawful basis and the residency rules. Moving EU employee data to a US-hosted service is a DPA question, not an engineering decision, and it must be raised in week one or it blocks go-live.
  • Audit trail: who accessed what, when, from where — including your integration's own reads. Many regulated customers require this and it is far easier to build in than to retrofit.
  • Retention and erasure: if a data subject requests erasure, can the customer find and delete their records in every store your integration created, including queues, DLQs, archives and logs? Design for this; it will be asked.
  • Pseudonymise where you can: a salted hash of an employee ID still supports joins and deduplication without carrying an identifier.
  • Encrypt in transit (TLS 1.2+ everywhere, no exceptions) and at rest (the platform's KMS, with the customer's keys where they require it).

# A client-side limiter plus adaptive concurrency. Use BOTH: the limiter
# enforces the published rate, the adaptive controller discovers the real one.
import asyncio, time, random

class TokenBucket:
    def __init__(self, rate_per_sec: float, burst: int):
        self.rate, self.capacity = rate_per_sec, burst
        self.tokens = float(burst)
        self.updated = time.monotonic()
        self._lock = asyncio.Lock()

    async def acquire(self, n: int = 1):
        while True:
            async with self._lock:
                now = time.monotonic()
                self.tokens = min(self.capacity,
                                  self.tokens + (now - self.updated) * self.rate)
                self.updated = now
                if self.tokens >= n:
                    self.tokens -= n
                    return
                deficit = (n - self.tokens) / self.rate
            await asyncio.sleep(deficit + random.uniform(0, 0.05))

class AdaptiveConcurrency:
    """AIMD — additive increase, multiplicative decrease. The same algorithm
    TCP uses for congestion control, applied to an undocumented API limit."""
    def __init__(self, start=4, minimum=1, maximum=64):
        self.limit = start
        self.min, self.max = minimum, maximum
        self._sem = asyncio.Semaphore(start)
        self._since_change = 0

    def on_success(self):
        self._since_change += 1
        if self._since_change >= 20 and self.limit < self.max:
            self.limit += 1                       # additive increase
            self._sem.release()
            self._since_change = 0
            CONCURRENCY_LIMIT.set(self.limit)

    def on_throttle(self):
        new = max(self.min, self.limit // 2)      # multiplicative decrease
        for _ in range(self.limit - new):
            asyncio.create_task(self._sem.acquire())
        self.limit = new
        self._since_change = 0
        CONCURRENCY_LIMIT.set(self.limit)
        log.warning("concurrency.reduced", new_limit=new)

        # Read the limit headers — most APIs tell you exactly where you stand
        remaining = r.headers.get("X-RateLimit-Remaining") \
                 or r.headers.get("RateLimit-Remaining")
        if remaining is not None:
            API_QUOTA_REMAINING.labels(api="crm").set(int(remaining))
        if r.status_code == 429:
            concurrency.on_throttle()
            wait = float(r.headers.get("Retry-After", 30))
            THROTTLED.labels(api="crm").inc()
            await asyncio.sleep(wait + random.uniform(0, 2))
            raise Throttled(wait)
        concurrency.on_success()
        return r

Designing within the budget

  1. Measure the budget first.Call the limits endpoint, read the headers, ask the platform owner. Write the number into the design document. "Salesforce org limit 45,000/day, current baseline consumption from other integrations 28,000/day, our budget 8,000/day with 20% headroom."
  2. Use the bulk path for bulk work.One Bulk API job for a million records versus a million REST calls is the difference between 1 API call and 1,000,000. This single decision usually determines whether the project is feasible.
  3. Go incremental as fast as possible.Delta queries, modified-since filters, change streams. A full scan should happen once, at go-live, and then never again.
  4. Cache reference data aggressively.Country lists, product masters, cost centre mappings, user directories. These change daily at most. Caching them for an hour removes most of your call volume.
  5. Batch and coalesce.Composite requests, $batch, multi-record payloads, and debouncing — if the same record changes five times in a minute, sync it once.
  6. Schedule around their peak.Run bulk work in their quiet window, and ask what that is. Then check that their quiet window is not also when their backup, their batch and three other integrations run.
  7. Degrade, do not die.When you hit the limit, queue the work and slow down. Do not fail the user's request if the data can be eventually consistent.
  8. Instrument consumption as a percentage of budgetand alert at 70%, before you are the reason someone else's integration fails.
Be a good neighbour, visiblyTell the platform owner your expected call volume before you deploy, show them the dashboard of your actual consumption after you deploy, and reduce it when you can. In every enterprise, API quota is a shared commons, and the team that treats it carelessly becomes the team that gets throttled, audited and eventually removed. Two emails and one dashboard panel buy you an enormous amount of goodwill with the people who control whether your integration keeps running after you leave.

Testing, Cutover and Operations

Integration testing is harder than unit testing because the interesting behaviour lives in systems you do not control, cannot easily reset, and must not break. Cutover is harder still, because it happens once, under time pressure, in a window that was negotiated three months ago and cannot slip. Both are solvable with preparation, and preparation is the entire skill.

Data migration and cutover

Parallel run with a flag-based switch. The comparison report is what converts opinion into a go/no-go decision.
  1. Profile the source data before you design the migration.Row counts, distinct values, null rates, duplicates, orphans, encoding anomalies, dates in the future, negative amounts. You will find that 4% of records violate a constraint everyone assured you was enforced. Find it in week two, not in the cutover window.
  2. Define the migration as idempotent and resumable.It will fail halfway at least once. Keyed upserts, a checkpoint table, and the ability to restart from the last committed batch.
  3. Rehearse at full scale, twice.A migration that takes 90 minutes in test with 10% of the data takes fifteen hours with all of it, and your window is four. Measure, then optimise, then measure again. The second rehearsal is where you discover the problems the first rehearsal's fixes introduced.
  4. Run in parallel.New integration writes to a shadow target while the old process remains authoritative. Compare outputs daily. Every variance must be explained — not waved away — before go-live.
  5. Agree go/no-go criteria in writing, in advance."Variance under 0.01%, zero unexplained records, full load completes in under 3 hours, all reconciliation checks green." Decided when everyone is calm, not at 02:00 during the window.
  6. Switch with a feature flag, not a deployment.A flag flips in seconds and flips back in seconds. A deployment takes twenty minutes and a rollback takes longer under pressure.
  7. Freeze changes around the window.No other releases, no infrastructure changes, no certificate rotations. Get this agreed with the customer's change board as part of the window booking.
  8. Hypercare.Two weeks of daily reconciliation, a daily stand-up with the business owner, and the rollback still available. This is also when you train their support team, because the questions are real.

Interview Questions

Why is so much of a deployment taken up by integration work, and how do you plan for it?
Because the valuable part of any enterprise system is the data that already exists, and that data lives in systems that were built over decades by different teams with different assumptions. Getting it out means navigating an API that may not be activated, a service account that takes three weeks to provision, a firewall rule owned by a team with its own queue, a schema that nobody fully documents, and a change process measured in quarters. None of that is visible in a demo, which is why demos take four weeks and deployments take nine months. I plan for it by counting integration points rather than features during scoping, because each point brings a credential that expires, a schema that drifts, a rate limit, a maintenance window and a team to coordinate with — and the effort is superlinear in the number of points, not linear. Then in week one I establish, in writing, the answers to six questions per integration: what is the system of record, how do I get data out and who owns that interface, what is the volume and shape, how fresh does it need to be, what happens when it fails, and what is the lead time for a change. Those answers shape the architecture far more than any technology preference, and getting them early is the single biggest determinant of whether the project lands on time.
Walk me through the four integration styles and when you would choose each.
File transfer: one system writes a file, another reads it. It has no shared runtime, works through any firewall, is trivially auditable and replayable, and it remains the most common inter-company integration in the world. I choose it when the counterparty is another organisation, when latency of minutes to hours is acceptable, or when it is simply what the customer already does well. Shared database: two applications use the same tables. Consistent and fast, but it is total coupling — the schema becomes a public API nobody can version, and within two years nobody dares change a column. I avoid it except for read-only access to a replica or a view that the owning team maintains deliberately as an interface. RPC — REST, SOAP, gRPC, SAP RFC: one system calls another and waits. Simple mental model, immediate result, good for queries. The hidden cost is temporal coupling: both systems must be up simultaneously, so a four-hour maintenance window downstream becomes four hours of failures. And it makes a network call look like a local one, which hides partial failure and timeout ambiguity. Messaging: a broker decouples producer and consumer in time, space and throughput. I default to this for anything that can be asynchronous, because one component's outage degrades the system rather than destroying it, and the queue absorbs bursts. The costs are real — you need a broker, duplicates are a fact of life, and debugging requires distributed tracing — but they are costs I know how to pay. Most real flows combine two or three styles, and the skill is choosing the boundary between them deliberately.
What is idempotency and how do you implement it?
An operation is idempotent if performing it more than once has the same effect as performing it once. It matters because essentially every realistic delivery mechanism is at-least-once: brokers redeliver on timeout, clients retry on an ambiguous response, operators replay a dead letter queue, and a file job gets rerun. Without idempotency, each of those produces a duplicate business transaction. There are four implementations I use in order of preference. First, natural idempotence: structure the write as an upsert on a business key, so re-running it converges to the same state. Second, a dedupe store: record the message ID in Redis or a table with SETNX and a TTL, and skip anything already seen — necessary for operations that are not naturally idempotent, like sending an email or incrementing a balance. Third, idempotency keys on outbound calls, where the provider dedupes server-side — and critically, the key must be derived from business identifiers so that it is stable across retries of the same logical operation, not generated fresh at call time, which is a mistake I have seen more than once. Fourth, conditional writes with optimistic concurrency, which is the safest for state machines because it also protects against out-of-order updates. Two details people miss: the dedupe TTL must exceed the maximum possible redelivery window, including DLQ retention and manual replay, so seven days is a sensible floor rather than one hour; and idempotency has a scope that must be documented — per tenant, for how long, keyed on what, and what happens if the same key arrives with a different payload.
Explain the transactional outbox pattern and the problem it solves.
It solves the dual-write problem. When a business operation needs to update your database and publish an event, there is no ordering that is safe, because the two systems have no shared transaction. If you commit the database then publish, a crash in between means the state changed but nobody was told. If you publish then commit, a crash means the world believes something happened that did not. The outbox pattern makes the publication part of the database transaction: in the same transaction that updates the invoice, you insert a row into an outbox table containing the event. The transaction either commits both or neither, so the event can never be lost. A separate relay process then reads unpublished outbox rows and publishes them to the broker, marking them published afterwards. The relay can be a simple poller using SELECT ... FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED so multiple instances can run safely, or it can be Debezium reading the database's write-ahead log, which removes the polling latency. The important property to be honest about is that this gives you at-least-once, not exactly-once: if the relay crashes after the broker acknowledges but before it marks the row published, the event is published twice. That is correct and expected — the outbox guarantees no lost events, and consumer idempotency handles the duplicates. The other thing people forget is housekeeping: the outbox table grows forever unless you delete published rows on a schedule, in batches, and I have seen one reach four hundred gigabytes.
A customer says their API has a rate limit you will exceed. What do you do?
First I measure precisely what the budget is and what is already consuming it, because in an enterprise the limit is usually shared across every integration in the company and I need to know my share, not the total. For Salesforce that means calling the limits endpoint and reading the Sforce-Limit-Info header; for Graph it means understanding per-workload throttling; for an internal system it often means asking the platform owner, because it is undocumented. Then I attack the volume rather than the limit. The biggest lever is almost always using the bulk path instead of per-record calls: one Bulk API 2.0 job for a million records versus a million REST calls is a six-order-of-magnitude difference and usually decides whether the project is feasible. Next is going incremental — delta queries, change streams, modified-since filters — so a full scan happens once at go-live and never again. Then caching reference data that changes daily at most, batching with composite or $batch endpoints, and debouncing so a record that changes five times in a minute syncs once. On the client side I implement a token-bucket limiter matched to their published rate, plus adaptive concurrency using additive-increase multiplicative-decrease to discover the real limit where it is undocumented, and I always honour Retry-After because enterprise APIs mean it and ignoring it escalates throttling. Finally I instrument consumption as a percentage of budget, alert at seventy per cent, and proactively show the platform owner my dashboard. That last part is political rather than technical, but quota is a shared commons and the team that treats it carelessly is the team that gets throttled, audited and eventually removed.
How would you approach integrating with a customer's SAP system?
I would start with vocabulary and people rather than code, because in SAP the gatekeepers are the Basis team and they can tell within ninety seconds whether you know what you are asking for. I would establish which system and client we are talking to, whether it is ECC or S/4HANA, and whether SAP Gateway is installed and which OData services are activated — because an OData path is dramatically easier than RFC. Then I would ask for a technical user of type SYSTEM or COMMUNICATION, never DIALOG, because a dialog user consumes a named licence, is subject to password expiry and interactive login policies, and will lock your integration out at 2am on day ninety. I would ask what authorisation roles it gets and request an authorisation trace during testing, because "it returns no rows" in SAP is far more often a permissions problem than a code problem. Technically there are five ways in: OData via Gateway, which is my first choice; RFC and BAPI calls, which need the NetWeaver RFC SDK, gateway ports and often SNC encryption; IDocs, which are asynchronous, status-tracked and the most reliable mechanism, especially for high-volume flows; CDS views and direct HANA reads; and SAP Integration Suite if they have it, in which case they will probably require me to route through it. Three mistakes I specifically avoid: forgetting BAPI_TRANSACTION_COMMIT, which makes everything report success while saving nothing; reading tables directly instead of using an API, which bypasses business logic and breaks on the next support pack; and assuming HTTP 200 means success, when BAPIs return errors inside the RETURN table of a successful response.
How do you design a webhook consumer that does not lose data?
Six things, in order. Verify the signature first, using HMAC over the raw request body with a constant-time comparison, before parsing anything — the endpoint is reachable by anyone who can reach your gateway, and re-serialising parsed JSON before verifying will break the signature because key order changes. Check the timestamp and reject anything outside a few minutes, because a valid signature on a replayed payload from last week is still valid. Return 200 fast and do the work asynchronously: most providers time out at three to ten seconds and will disable a slow endpoint, so I validate, write to a queue or outbox, and return in under a hundred milliseconds. Deduplicate on the provider's event ID with a TTL longer than their maximum retry window, because every provider delivers duplicates eventually. Treat the payload as a hint rather than truth — webhooks arrive out of order, so I compare a version or updated-at field and discard anything older than what I already have, and for critical data I fetch current state from the API rather than trusting the body. And finally, build a reconciliation path: a periodic poll of everything changed since a timestamp, because webhooks will be missed when the provider has an outage, when my endpoint is down past their retry limit, or when a firewall rule changes. That poll is not optional; it is the safety net that turns "we lost four hours of events and nobody noticed" into "we caught up automatically".
What is the difference between a queue and a log, and how do you choose?
In a queue — RabbitMQ, SQS, Service Bus, IBM MQ — a message is consumed and removed. The broker tracks per-message state: unacknowledged, acknowledged, dead-lettered. You get rich routing, priorities, TTLs, scheduled delivery and built-in dead-letter semantics, and competing consumers give you horizontal scaling. What you do not get is replay: once consumed, the message is gone. In a log — Kafka, Pulsar, Kinesis, Event Hubs — messages are appended to an immutable, ordered, partitioned sequence, and consumers track their own offsets. Reading does not remove anything, so any number of independent consumer groups can read the same data, and replay is simply resetting an offset. What you do not get out of the box is per-message retry and dead-lettering; you build those. I choose with three questions. Will more than one independent system need this data, now or in the future? If yes, a log, because adding a consumer group requires nothing from the producing team, which in an enterprise is a significant political advantage. Will I need to reprocess history after fixing a bug or to rebuild a downstream store? If yes, a log. Does each item need to be worked on exactly once by one worker, with per-message retry, delay and dead-lettering? If yes, a queue. In practice the customer already owns one or both and the real decision is using whichever they have correctly — the dangerous failure is designing for log semantics on top of a queue, or assuming queue-style per-message retry in Kafka, where a poison message blocks an entire partition unless you explicitly route it to a DLQ topic.
What do you think about exactly-once delivery?
It is mostly a marketing term, and being clear about that is a professional obligation. Kafka's exactly-once semantics are real but narrow: they cover a read-process-write cycle entirely within Kafka, using transactions and read-committed isolation. The moment a consumer writes to an external system — a database, SAP, an HTTP API — you are back to at-least-once, because there is no distributed transaction spanning Kafka and SAP, and two-phase commit across heterogeneous enterprise systems is not something anyone will let you deploy. So the correct engineering position is: assume at-least-once delivery and make every consumer idempotent. That combination is sometimes called effectively-once, and it is achievable, testable and explainable. The reason to be firm about this with customers is that promising exactly-once is a promise you cannot keep, and the promise gets examined on the day a network blip causes a duplicate posting in their general ledger. I would rather have the harder conversation in the design review, where I can show the dedupe mechanism and the reconciliation report, than in an incident review three months later.
A customer wants to give you direct read access to their production database. How do you respond?
Carefully, because it sounds helpful and is usually a trap. An application's tables are its internal state, not its API: the status column means something only in combination with three other tables and logic implemented in code, so reading directly means reimplementing that logic and getting it subtly wrong. The schema is not a contract — the owning team will rename a column in a routine release because from their perspective it is private, and they are right — so my integration breaks and the postmortem concludes it was my fault. And polling on updated_at misses hard deletes entirely and depends on a timestamp that is often unreliable, especially after bulk updates that bypass triggers. So I would ask three questions: is there an API, even an imperfect one; can the owning team expose a view or materialised view as a deliberate, versioned interface; and can we use log-based change data capture against a replica instead? CDC with Debezium reads the transaction log, captures inserts, updates and deletes in commit order with before and after images, and costs the source database almost nothing. But I would raise the replication-slot risk explicitly and in writing with the DBA, because in PostgreSQL a stalled consumer causes WAL to accumulate until the source database's disk fills and production stops accepting writes — which is a real way to take down a system you were only supposed to read from. The mitigations are monitoring slot lag, setting max_slot_wal_keep_size, configuring a heartbeat query, and having a documented re-snapshot runbook. If direct access really is the only option, I would insist on a read-only replica, a dedicated login, a statement timeout, agreed query patterns, and written acknowledgement that the schema may change without notice.
How do you plan a cutover for an integration that replaces an existing process?
The organising principle is that I must be able to roll back, and if I cannot roll back I do not have a plan, I have a hope. Concretely: I profile the source data early, because I will find that several per cent of records violate a constraint everyone assured me was enforced, and I want that in week two rather than in the window. I make the migration idempotent and resumable with checkpointing, because it will fail halfway at least once. I rehearse at full scale twice — a load that takes ninety minutes with ten per cent of the data takes fifteen hours with all of it, and the second rehearsal is where I find the problems the first rehearsal's fixes introduced. I run the new integration in parallel, writing to a shadow target while the old process stays authoritative, and I produce a daily comparison report where every variance must be explained rather than waved away. I agree go/no-go criteria in writing in advance, when everyone is calm, not at two in the morning. I switch with a feature flag rather than a deployment, because a flag flips back in seconds. I get a change freeze around the window agreed with their change board. And I write the runbook with an owner and a duration on every line, work backwards from the rollback deadline to find the real point of no return, and put that in bold. Afterwards, two weeks of hypercare with daily reconciliation, the rollback still available, and that is when I train their support team, because the questions they ask are real ones.
What goes into an integration specification, and which part matters most?
Purpose and scope in business language, with an explicit out-of-scope list that prevents more arguments than the in-scope list. Systems and ownership, naming the actual owner and contact for each. The data contract: every field, its type, its source, whether it is required, and its data classification — which is the section the data protection officer reads. The flow, with a sequence diagram. The interaction model: direction, synchronous or asynchronous, frequency, and the one-time initial load separately from steady state. Non-functional requirements with actual numbers: latency target, throughput sustained and burst, availability with named exclusions, retention, RPO and RTO. Security: the identity mechanism, transport encryption, secret storage, network path, audit. Operations: dashboards, alert routing, runbooks, reconciliation, support tiers and escalation. Risks and assumptions with mitigations. And an open-questions section, which I keep deliberately, because an honest list of what is not yet decided builds far more trust than a document pretending everything is settled. The part that matters most is the error-handling matrix: a table enumerating every failure condition, how it is detected, what action is taken, whether it retries, what severity of alert it raises, and who owns it. Architects and operations managers have all been burned by integrations that work beautifully in the happy path and fail mysteriously otherwise, and that table is what demonstrates you have thought about the three years after go-live rather than just the launch. In my experience it is the section read most closely and the one that most changes how seriously the design is taken.

Integration Design Checklist and Lab Plan

A checklist to run before any integration goes live, and a seven-part lab that builds a realistic enterprise integration end to end on a laptop. The checklist is the deliverable; the lab is how it becomes instinct.

Design checklist

Contract and data

  • System of record identified for every entity; arbitration rule agreed where two systems claim it.
  • Field-level mapping documented, including type, source, required flag and data classification.
  • Natural/business key identified and confirmed stable. It is the basis of your idempotency.
  • Volume documented: average, peak, growth, record size, history depth.
  • Deletes: how are they represented, and does the target need them?
  • Money as integer minor units with an ISO-4217 currency field. No floats. No assumed two decimal places.
  • Timestamps are timezone-aware UTC with the business event time distinct from the message time.
  • Schema versioned, with an explicit compatibility policy and a parallel-run plan for the next version.
  • Known data quality problems profiled and documented before design, not discovered at cutover.

Reliability

  • Delivery semantics stated explicitly; consumers idempotent; dedupe scope and TTL documented.
  • Retry policy: what is retryable, how many attempts, exponential backoff with full jitter, a global retry budget.
  • Retry-After honoured everywhere.
  • Timeouts explicit at every layer, forming a budget where inner timeouts are shorter than outer ones.
  • Circuit breaker on every external dependency, tripping on latency as well as errors.
  • Dead letter channel with an owner, an alert, a triage process and a tested replay tool.
  • Transactional outbox or equivalent wherever a state change and a published event must agree.
  • Reconciliation job comparing source and target counts and amounts, with an unexplained-variance alert.
  • Deadline monitoring: alert when an expected file or batch does not arrive.
  • Claim check for payloads above the broker limit, with blob lifetime exceeding queue plus DLQ retention.

Security and governance

  • Dedicated service identity, least privilege, owned by a team, documented in the customer's register.
  • No integration runs as a named human, ever.
  • Secrets in the customer's vault; workload identity preferred; nothing in images, config or git.
  • Credential expiry exposed as a metric with alerts at 30, 14 and 7 days, sized to the renewal lead time.
  • TLS verification never disabled; internal CAs trusted explicitly; proxy variables set for every runtime.
  • Data classification table completed; minimisation applied; restricted fields explicitly excluded.
  • Residency, lawful basis and sub-processor questions raised in week one, not at go-live.
  • Audit trail for reads and writes; erasure path understood across queues, DLQs, archives and logs.

Operations and handover

  • Dashboard per integration: throughput, success rate, per-hop latency, DLQ depth, oldest message, lag, credential expiry.
  • Alerts routed to the customer's queue from day one, each with a runbook link and a business-impact description.
  • Correlation ID propagated end to end and visible in every system's logs.
  • Daily reconciliation report delivered automatically to the business owner.
  • Credential register and dependency map handed over, including upstream maintenance windows and contacts.
  • Rate-limit consumption instrumented as a percentage of agreed budget, alerting at 70%.
  • Handover acceptance test completed: their team diagnoses an injected fault using only your artefacts.

Lab plan — build a realistic enterprise integration

  1. Lab 1 — The file drop that survives reality (3 hours)Build a consumer for a CSV drop with every guard: sentinel file, stability check, atomic claim by rename, batch-level idempotency keyed on a content hash, control-file count and checksum validation, header schema assertion, a reject threshold, and a single transaction per file. Then attack it: feed it a file still being written, the same file twice, a file with a column inserted in the middle, a UTF-8 BOM, CRLF endings, a field containing an unescaped comma, a zero-byte file, and no file at all. Success criterion: every one of those produces a clear, actionable log line and the correct outcome, and the missing-file case raises an alert.
  2. Lab 2 — Queue versus log, hands on (4 hours)Run RabbitMQ and Kafka in Docker. Publish the same event stream to both. In RabbitMQ, implement a dead letter exchange, demonstrate redelivery on nack, and implement a manual retry with a delay queue. In Kafka, implement manual offset commits after processing, route a poison message to a DLQ topic, and demonstrate that failing to do so blocks the partition. Then add a second consumer group to Kafka and show it reads all history independently, and try the equivalent in RabbitMQ to feel the difference. Finally, deliberately key your Kafka producer by tenant ID with one dominant tenant and observe the hot partition in the per-partition lag graph. Success criterion: you can explain from your own graphs why the partition key choice matters.
  3. Lab 3 — Idempotency and the outbox (3 hours)Build a service with a POST endpoint that writes a business record and publishes an event, using the transactional outbox pattern with a polling relay using SELECT FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED. Then break it on purpose: kill the relay between the broker flush and the published-at update, and confirm the event is republished and that your idempotent consumer handles it. Kill the process between the business write and the publish, and confirm nothing is lost. Send the same request twice with the same Idempotency-Key and confirm one job is created. Send the same key with a different payload and confirm a 409. Success criterion: you can state precisely which guarantee each mechanism provides and demonstrate each one.
  4. Lab 4 — Webhooks in both directions (3 hours)Build a consumer with HMAC verification over the raw body, a timestamp replay window, constant-time comparison, dedupe on event ID, and a sub-100ms response that enqueues the work. Write tests that send a tampered body, a stale timestamp, a replayed event and an out-of-order update, and assert the correct rejection or suppression for each. Then build the producer side: signed delivery, an exponential retry schedule with jitter, permanent-versus-transient status classification, a delivery log, auto-disable after sustained failure with customer notification, and a replay endpoint. Success criterion: you can take your own consumer down for twenty minutes and lose nothing once it returns.
  5. Lab 5 — CDC and the hot path (4 hours)Stand up PostgreSQL, Kafka Connect and Debezium. Capture changes from an invoices table. Observe the initial snapshot, then inserts, updates and deletes. Translate the raw row-change events into named business events in a single translation step and publish to a clean topic. Then cause the failure that matters: stop the connector, generate load, and watch pg_replication_slots lag and WAL size grow. Set max_slot_wal_keep_size and confirm the database protects itself. Write the runbook for dropping and recreating the slot. Success criterion: you can explain the replication-slot risk to a DBA in two minutes and show them your monitoring for it.
  6. Lab 6 — Living within a rate limit (3 hours)Build a mock upstream with WireMock that enforces a token-bucket limit of eight requests per second with a burst of twenty, returns 429 with Retry-After when exceeded, and randomly injects 503s and five-second delays. Write a client that stays within the limit using a token bucket, discovers an undocumented concurrency ceiling with additive-increase multiplicative-decrease, honours Retry-After, retries with full jitter, trips a circuit breaker on sustained failure, and exposes quota consumption as a metric. Load it with 100,000 requests. Success criterion: zero sustained 429s, a graph showing your concurrency converging on the real limit, and a completion time within twenty per cent of the theoretical minimum.
  7. Lab 7 — The full pipeline with a cutover (5 hours)Compose everything: an SFTP drop feeding a file consumer, which publishes via an outbox to Kafka, consumed by a translator that enriches from a cached reference API, routed by region, written idempotently to a target database, with a DLQ, a daily reconciliation job, correlation IDs propagated end to end and visible in every log, and a dashboard showing throughput, lag, DLQ depth and reconciliation variance. Then perform a cutover: run a legacy path and the new path in parallel against the same input, produce a daily comparison report, define go/no-go criteria, switch with a feature flag, and practise the rollback. Finally, write the integration specification for what you built, including the error-handling matrix, and hand the whole thing to a colleague. Success criterion: they can, from your specification and dashboards alone, diagnose a fault you inject and execute the documented recovery without asking you a question.
The habit that makes you the person they ask forAfter every integration incident, add a fixture file. The malformed payload, the unexpected 200-with-an-error, the HTML login page where JSON was expected, the truncated response, the duplicate that arrived nine days later. Over a couple of years that directory becomes the most valuable artefact you own: a concrete record of every way enterprise systems actually fail, encoded as executable tests. It is also the thing that lets you say, in a design review, "I have seen this fail three ways and here is how we handle each" — which is the sentence that gets your design approved and gets you asked for by name on the next engagement.
