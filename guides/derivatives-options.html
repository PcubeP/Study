Read this first: what actually happens to retail F&O traders

Most guides to futures and options open with a payoff diagram. This one opens with the outcome data, because the outcome data is the single most important fact about this market and almost nobody puts it first. If you read only one section of this guide, read this one.

What that number means and what it does not mean

A 90%-plus loss rate does not mean the market is rigged, and it does not mean the 7–9% who profit are all geniuses. It means something more boring and more useful:

Derivatives are close to zero-sum before costs

For every rupee a futures buyer gains, a futures seller loses exactly a rupee. For every rupee an option buyer gains, the writer loses it. There is no underlying economic growth being shared out the way there is with equity ownership. The pie does not grow; it only moves.

And firmly negative-sum after costs

Brokerage, STT, exchange transaction charges, SEBI turnover fees, stamp duty, GST and the bid–ask spread all leak out of the pot before anybody settles up. Taken together those costs are a meaningful share of the money that individuals lose — SEBI's own analysis attributes a large chunk of small-trader losses to transaction costs alone.

The counterparties are not amateurs

The consistent profit in Indian F&O accrues to proprietary trading firms, foreign portfolio investors running systematic books, and market makers. They have co-located servers, millisecond latency, negotiated cost structures, risk teams and inventory models. You are a retail participant with a mobile app. That is the matchup.

Leverage compresses the timeline

With 5–10x effective leverage, an edge that would take a decade to show up in cash equities shows up — in either direction — in weeks. Leverage does not create skill. It accelerates whatever you already have, including the absence of skill.

Three legitimate reasons to use derivatives

Derivatives exist for good reasons. They were invented so that a farmer could lock in a price for a crop and a miller could lock in a cost for the flour. Retail participants have three defensible uses, and none of them is "make money fast".

  1. Hedging an existing exposureYou own ₹10,00,000 (ten lakh) of equity and you are worried about a drawdown over a specific window — a results season, a budget, an election. You can buy index put options or short index futures to offset part of that risk. You pay for the protection. You expect the hedge to lose money most of the time, because insurance that pays out most of the time is mispriced.
  2. Generating income against something you already ownYou hold shares in a company you intend to keep. Writing a covered call against that holding converts some future upside into cash premium today. This is a genuine strategy with a genuine cost: you cap your upside and you still own all the downside. It is not free money and it is not "safe".
  3. Expressing a view with defined, capped riskYou believe something specific will happen in a specific window. Buying a vertical spread or a single long option caps your loss at the premium paid. You know your maximum loss before you enter. This is the only speculative use that does not have an open-ended tail.
What is not on that list"Selling options for steady monthly income" with naked short positions. "Scalping Bank Nifty weeklies." "Buying cheap out-of-the-money calls on expiry day." Martingale-style averaging down on a losing option position. Any strategy whose description includes a claimed accuracy percentage. Each of these is covered later in this guide with the arithmetic showing why it fails.

What a derivative actually is

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from something else. You are not buying the thing. You are buying a legally enforceable agreement about the thing — typically about its price at a future date. Everything else is detail.

Why leverage is the defining feature

Here is the arithmetic that makes derivatives dangerous, stated as plainly as possible. Assume a hypothetical index at 25,000 and a lot size of 75.

TEXT
NOTIONAL EXPOSURE OF ONE INDEX FUTURES CONTRACT
================================================
Index level                      25,000
Lot size                         75
Notional value   = 25,000 x 75 = Rs 18,75,000   (eighteen lakh seventy-five thousand)

Approximate initial margin (SPAN + exposure), say 12% of notional
Margin blocked   = 18,75,000 x 0.12 = Rs 2,25,000

Effective leverage = 18,75,000 / 2,25,000 = 8.3x

NOW MOVE THE INDEX BY 1%
------------------------
Index moves 25,000 -> 25,250 (+250 points, +1.0%)
P&L on one lot = 250 x 75 = Rs 18,750 profit
Return on margin = 18,750 / 2,25,000 = +8.3%

Index moves 25,000 -> 24,750 (-250 points, -1.0%)
P&L on one lot = Rs 18,750 LOSS
Return on margin = -8.3%

A 1% index move = an 8.3% move on your capital.
A 12% index move against you = your entire margin, and you owe more.

varies daily with volatility. Verify on the exchange margin calculator.

Losses are not capped on futures or short optionsIf you are long futures, short futures, or short (written) options, your loss is not limited to the money in your account. A gap-down of 8% on a leveraged long position can leave you owing your broker money. Indian brokers will square you off, but a gap happens before any square-off can execute. This is not a theoretical risk; it has happened in every market, repeatedly.

Equity is positive-sum. Derivatives are not.

This is the most important conceptual difference and it is routinely glossed over.

Buying equity

You own a share of a business. The business generates earnings, reinvests, grows, pays dividends. Over long periods the total value of all shareholders' holdings grows because the underlying businesses produce real output. Every long-term shareholder can profit simultaneously. The pie grows.

Trading derivatives

Every contract has a long and a short. Their P&L sums to exactly zero before costs. There is no earnings stream inside a futures contract. For you to make ₹50,000, somebody must lose ₹50,000. After brokerage, STT, exchange fees and spread, the two sides together lose money. The pie shrinks.

Forwards, futures and the Indian futures market

A forward is a private bilateral agreement. A future is the same economic idea, standardised, listed on an exchange and guaranteed by a clearing corporation. That standardisation and guarantee is the entire innovation, and it is what makes exchange-traded derivatives usable by someone with a demat account rather than a treasury department.

Forward versus future, side by side

DimensionForwardFuture
Where it tradesOver the counter, negotiated directly between two partiesOn an exchange (NSE, BSE) with a visible order book
TermsFully customised — any quantity, any date, any underlyingStandardised — fixed lot size, fixed expiry dates, fixed tick
Counterparty riskYou bear it. If they default, you litigate.Novated to the clearing corporation, which becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer
MarginNegotiated or absentMandatory, computed by SPAN, collected upfront, topped up intraday
Daily settlementNone — P&L settles once, at maturityMark to market every day; cash moves daily
Liquidity / exitYou must renegotiate with the same counterpartySell to anyone in the order book
Who uses itCorporates hedging currency, commodity producers, banksEveryone, including individuals
The clearing corporation is the unsung heroWhen you buy an index future, you are not relying on the stranger who sold it. The clearing corporation steps in between. It collects margin from both sides daily, marks positions to market, and maintains a settlement guarantee fund. This is why a retail participant can take the other side of a bank's position without doing credit analysis on the bank. It is also why margin rules are not negotiable — the guarantee only works if the margin is actually collected.

The expiry calendar, and why it keeps moving

For years, Indian index options had weekly expiries on multiple indices across both exchanges, producing an expiry event almost every trading day of the week. That concentration of short-dated, cheap, high-gamma contracts is exactly where retail losses were heaviest. SEBI's October 2024 framework restricted weekly expiries to one benchmark index per exchange. Exchanges have since reshuffled which weekday their expiry falls on. The practical consequences:

  • Never assume an expiry day from memory. Check the contract master.
  • A strategy that depended on four weekly expiries a week no longer exists in that form.
  • Monthly expiry is the last weekly expiry of the month for the relevant index — so monthly and weekly coincide that day, with correspondingly larger open interest and sharper moves.

Futures do not settle once at expiry. They settle every single day. Cash physically moves in and out of your trading account at the end of each session. Worked example with a hypothetical index at 25,000 and lot size 75:

1. On Day 3 and Day 4 real money LEFT your account. If your free
   cash was insufficient, you get a margin call and may be squared
   off at the worst possible moment - at the Day 4 low, locking in
   -30,000 and missing the Day 5 recovery entirely.
2. Your "paper loss" is not paper. It is cash, daily.
3. The final position P&L (+50 points x 75 = +3,750) equals the sum

   still in the trade to collect it.

and you can still be stopped out by the journey.
"I'll just hold through the drawdown" is not available to youIn cash equity you can ignore a 20% fall indefinitely. In futures you cannot, because the loss is collected from your bank balance every evening and because a margin shortfall triggers a penalty and then an auto square-off. Leverage removes the option to be patient. That is its central cost.

Futures pricing: cost of carry, basis and a hedge that actually works

A futures price is not a forecast. It is today's spot price adjusted for the cost of holding the underlying until expiry. Once you internalise that, half the mystique of the futures market evaporates and the remaining half is arithmetic.

The cost-of-carry model

TEXT
FAIR VALUE OF A FUTURES CONTRACT
=================================
F  = S x e^(r x t)        (continuous compounding form)
or approximately
F  = S x (1 + r x t) - D   (simple form, with dividends)

where
  F = fair futures price
  S = spot price of the underlying
  r = risk-free interest rate (annualised, decimal)
  t = time to expiry in years
  D = present value of dividends expected before expiry

INTUITION
---------
If you want exposure to the index in one month you have two choices:
  (a) buy the basket today and hold it for a month
  (b) buy the futures contract

With (a) you tie up capital for a month (you forgo interest r)
but you RECEIVE any dividends (D) paid in that month.
With (b) you tie up only margin.

For the two routes to cost the same - i.e. for there to be no
arbitrage - the futures price must be higher than spot by the
forgone interest and lower by the dividends received.

WORKED EXAMPLE
--------------
Spot index S                     25,000.00
Risk-free rate r                 6.50% p.a.  (illustrative)
Days to expiry                   30  ->  t = 30/365 = 0.08219 yr
Expected dividend yield over
  the period, in index points    22.00

Interest component = 25,000 x 0.065 x 0.08219
                   = 25,000 x 0.0053425
                   = 133.56 points

Fair value F = 25,000 + 133.56 - 22.00
             = 25,111.56

So with the index at 25,000, a one-month future SHOULD trade
around 25,112. If it trades at 25,250, it is rich by 138 points.
If it trades at 25,040, it is cheap by 72 points.

Rates and dividend assumptions are illustrative.
As at late 2026 - verify current rates independently.

Basis, contango and backwardation

TEXT
BASIS = FUTURES PRICE - SPOT PRICE

Futures 25,112   Spot 25,000   ->  Basis = +112  (POSITIVE)
Futures 24,940   Spot 25,000   ->  Basis =  -60  (NEGATIVE)

POSITIVE BASIS (futures above spot) = CONTANGO
  Normal for equity index futures, because the cost of carry
  (interest) usually exceeds the dividend yield.

NEGATIVE BASIS (futures below spot) = BACKWARDATION
  In equity indices this typically appears when:
   - a large dividend is due before expiry (dividends subtract)
   - there is heavy short hedging pressure (institutions selling
     futures to hedge cash holdings, pushing futures below fair)
   - borrowing the stock to arbitrage is hard or expensive
  Backwardation is a positioning signal, not a crash forecast.

CONVERGENCE
-----------
At expiry, the futures contract settles against the underlying.
Therefore basis must be ZERO at expiry, by construction.

   Days to expiry:  30    20    10     5     2     0
   Typical basis:  +112   +75   +38   +19    +8     0
                    \                              /
                     \__ decays roughly linearly __/
                        with time to expiry

This decay is DETERMINISTIC, unlike option time decay which
depends on volatility. It is why a long futures position held
to expiry in contango carries a small structural headwind, and
a short futures position carries a small structural tailwind.
Both are tiny compared to directional risk.
Basis convergence. The futures line and the spot line must meet at expiry because the contract settles against the underlying.

Calendar spreads

A calendar spread is simultaneously long one expiry and short another of the same underlying. You are trading the basis between two expiries, not direction. Because the two legs largely offset, exchanges historically granted a margin benefit on such spreads.

The expiry-day calendar-spread margin benefit was removedSEBI's 2024–25 framework removed the calendar-spread margin benefit on the day of expiry for contracts expiring that day. The rationale: on expiry day the two legs stop behaving like a hedged pair — the expiring leg's delta collapses to 0 or 1 while the far leg does not — so treating them as offsetting understated real risk. If you ran spreads into expiry relying on reduced margin, that is gone. Verify the current treatment at nseindia.com and with your broker's margin calculator.
TEXT
CALENDAR SPREAD - WHAT YOU ARE ACTUALLY TRADING
===============================================
Position: SELL near-month future @ 25,112
          BUY  far-month future  @ 25,240
          Spread = 128 points

You now have (nearly) zero directional exposure. If the index
rises 500 points, both legs move roughly 500 points and cancel.

What you profit from: the spread NARROWING (if you are short the
near and long the far, you want the far-near gap to shrink).

What actually moves the spread:
  - changes in the interest rate assumption
  - changes in expected dividends between the two expiries
  - institutional roll pressure in the last week before expiry
  - stock-borrow costs for single-stock calendars

Typical spread range: a few tens of index points.
Typical move you can capture: perhaps 10-30 points.
Transaction cost: 4 legs (2 in, 2 out) of brokerage + STT + fees.

RUN THE NUMBERS BEFORE YOU ROMANTICISE THIS. Four legs of cost
to chase a 20-point spread on a 75 lot = Rs 1,500 gross, against
perhaps Rs 300-600 of round-trip cost plus slippage on four fills.
Without the margin benefit it also ties up substantial capital.

A worked hedge: protecting a ₹10 lakh equity portfolio with index futures

TEXT
SITUATION (hypothetical)
========================
You hold a diversified Indian equity portfolio.
Portfolio value                      Rs 10,00,000  (ten lakh)
Portfolio beta vs the index          1.10
  (i.e. historically, when the index moves 1%,
   this portfolio has moved about 1.10%)
Index spot                           25,000
Index futures (1 month)              25,112
Lot size                             75
Notional per futures lot = 25,112 x 75 = Rs 18,83,400

You want to hedge for 4 weeks across a results season.
You do NOT want to sell the portfolio (capital gains tax,
re-entry risk, you still like the holdings long term).

STEP 1 - COMPUTE THE HEDGE RATIO
--------------------------------
Beta-adjusted exposure = Portfolio value x Beta
                       = 10,00,000 x 1.10
                       = Rs 11,00,000

Number of lots to SHORT = Beta-adjusted exposure / Notional per lot
                        = 11,00,000 / 18,83,400
                        = 0.584 lots

PROBLEM: you cannot short 0.584 of a lot. You can short 0 or 1.
This is the "lot size granularity" problem and it is the single
biggest practical obstacle to retail hedging in India after the
2024-25 lot-size increases.

STEP 2 - DECIDE
---------------
Option A: short 1 lot -> you are OVER-hedged
          (hedging Rs 18.83 lakh of exposure against Rs 11 lakh)
Option B: do not hedge with futures; use index options instead,
          or accept the risk, or reduce the position in cash.

Let us work Option A and see what over-hedging costs you.

READ THE TABLE HONESTLY
-----------------------
1. The hedge WORKS on the downside - a 10% crash costs you
   Rs 1.1 lakh on the portfolio but the short future makes
   Rs 1.96 lakh. You end up ahead.
2. But you are OVER-HEDGED. A 10% rally costs you Rs 69,100 net.
   You have converted a long portfolio into a net-short position.
3. The +8,400 at an unchanged index is the basis you captured by
   shorting the future above spot (112 points x 75). That is the
   one small structural tailwind of a short hedge in contango.
4. A hedge should NEUTRALISE risk, not reverse it. Over-hedging
   by 71% is not a hedge; it is a new directional bet.

MARGIN COST OF THE HEDGE
------------------------

rallies 5%, you pay out Rs 85,350 in daily MTM over those sessions
even though your portfolio is up - you need that cash available.

So hedging Rs 10 lakh of equity requires roughly Rs 2.26 lakh of
margin PLUS a cash buffer of perhaps Rs 1 lakh for MTM. That is
over 30% of the portfolio value sitting idle.

Options fundamentals

An option is a contract that gives the buyer the right but not the obligation to buy (call) or sell (put) the underlying at a fixed price (the strike) on or before a fixed date (expiry), in exchange for a payment (the premium) made to the seller, who takes on the matching obligation. Every option concept in this guide is a consequence of that one sentence.

This is quantified later with a worked blow-up scenario.

Call and put parity — the relationship that must hold

TEXT
PUT-CALL PARITY (European options, same strike and expiry)
==========================================================
  C - P  =  S - K x e^(-r x t)

  C = call premium, P = put premium
  S = spot, K = strike, r = rate, t = time in years

CHECK IT with our numbers (spot 25,000, strike 25,000,
18 days, r = 6.5%):

  t = 18/365 = 0.049315

                 = 25,000 x 0.996800
                 = 24,920.00

  Right-hand side  = 25,000 - 24,920 = 80.00
  Left-hand side   = C - P = 218.50 - 196.00 = 22.50

illustrative premiums ignore the dividend the index will pay
before expiry. Adding an expected dividend of about 57 index
points to the put side closes the gap.

WHY YOU CARE
------------
1. Parity is why a synthetic position exists: long call + short
   put at the same strike = long futures. Any time you think you
   have invented a clever structure, check whether parity says it
   is just a future in disguise with extra transaction costs.
2. Large persistent parity violations do not exist in liquid
   contracts. If your screen shows one, it is a stale quote, a
   dividend you forgot, or an illiquid strike you cannot actually
   trade at the displayed price.
3. It means calls and puts are not independent instruments. If you
   have a view on one, you implicitly have a view on the other.

Payoff diagrams and break-even arithmetic

A payoff diagram shows profit or loss on the vertical axis against the underlying price at expiry on the horizontal axis. It ignores time value entirely — it is the picture at expiry. That is a critical limitation and the source of a lot of beginner confusion, which this section addresses directly.

The diagram is the end of the film, not the filmPayoff diagrams describe the position's value at expiry only. Before expiry, the position is worth something different because of remaining time value and implied volatility. A long call can be deeply underwater on the diagram and still be worth money today. A short straddle can show a comfortable profit zone and still be bleeding badly mid-cycle because volatility rose. Never size a position using only the expiry diagram.

What drives an option premium: volatility, IV and the India VIX

Six inputs determine a European option's theoretical price. Five of them you can look up. The sixth — volatility — you cannot, and that is where essentially all the money is made and lost in options.

Volatility raises both calls and putsThis is counter-intuitive until you see why. Volatility measures the spread of possible outcomes, not their direction. A wider spread of outcomes increases the chance of finishing far in the money, and an option's downside is already capped at zero — the buyer cannot lose more by the underlying going further the wrong way. More uncertainty is therefore unambiguously good for an option buyer and bad for a seller, on both sides of the chain.

Black–Scholes, conceptually

The Black–Scholes–Merton model prices a European option by constructing a hypothetical portfolio of the underlying and cash that replicates the option's payoff, then arguing that the option must cost what that replicating portfolio costs. You do not need the derivation. You need the shape of the answer and the places it is wrong.

TEXT
BLACK-SCHOLES: WHAT IT SAYS IN WORDS
====================================
Call price = (probability-weighted value of receiving the stock
              if it finishes above the strike)
           - (discounted strike, weighted by the probability of
              finishing above the strike)

Formally:   C = S x N(d1) - K x e^(-rt) x N(d2)

   N(.)  = cumulative normal distribution function
   N(d2) = (risk-neutral) probability the option expires ITM
   N(d1) = the option's DELTA (see the Greeks section)

   d1 = [ ln(S/K) + (r + sigma^2 / 2) x t ] / (sigma x sqrt(t))
   d2 = d1 - sigma x sqrt(t)

YOU WILL NEVER COMPUTE THIS BY HAND. Your broker's platform and
every option calculator does it. What matters is the ASSUMPTIONS,
because each one is false in the Indian market:

ASSUMPTION                      REALITY
------------------------------  --------------------------------
Returns are lognormal, no jumps Indices gap on budgets, results,
                                policy news, global shocks.
                                Fat tails are the norm.
Volatility is constant          Volatility clusters: calm periods
                                and violent periods alternate.
No transaction costs            STT, brokerage, GST, stamp duty,
                                spread - all material for retail.
Continuous hedging is possible  You cannot rehedge continuously,
                                and each rehedge costs money.
You can borrow/lend at r        You cannot.

CONSEQUENCE: Black-Scholes is a QUOTING CONVENTION, not a truth.
The market does not use it to find the price. The market finds
the price, then inverts the formula to express that price as a
volatility number. That number is IMPLIED VOLATILITY.

Implied volatility: the only number that is really traded

TEXT
IMPLIED VOLATILITY (IV)
=======================
Take the market premium. Hold S, K, t, r, D fixed. Ask:
"what value of sigma makes Black-Scholes output this premium?"
That sigma is the IMPLIED volatility.

IV is NOT a prediction you should trust. It is the market's
consensus price for uncertainty, expressed in annualised
percentage terms.

TRANSLATING IV INTO AN EXPECTED MOVE
------------------------------------
Expected 1-standard-deviation move over period t:

   Move = Spot x IV x sqrt(t)

Worked, index at 25,000, IV = 14%, 18 days to expiry:

   t = 18/365 = 0.049315
   sqrt(t) = 0.22207
   Move = 25,000 x 0.14 x 0.22207
        = 777.2 index points

INTERPRETATION: with IV at 14%, the options market is pricing
roughly a 68% chance that the index finishes within
  25,000 +/- 777  =  24,223 to 25,777  in 18 days,
and roughly a 95% chance within +/- 1,554 (two SDs).

SANITY CHECK ON YOUR OWN TRADE
------------------------------
Before buying a 25,600 CE (600 points OTM) with 18 days left:
  600 / 777 = 0.77 standard deviations.
  Probability of finishing above 0.77 SD is roughly 22%.
  Probability of finishing above the BREAK-EVEN (25,600 + 34.50
  = 25,634.50, i.e. 0.82 SD) is roughly 21%.

So you have about a 1-in-5 chance of making anything at all.
The premium is not "cheap at Rs 34.50". It is priced for a
1-in-5 event. The market knows what it is selling you.

The volatility smile and skew

If Black–Scholes were correct, every strike on the same expiry would have the same IV. They do not. Plotting IV against strike produces a curve — a "smile" or, in equity indices, a downward-sloping "skew".

A typical equity-index skew. Out-of-the-money puts trade at materially higher implied volatility than out-of-the-money calls because markets fall faster than they rise and demand for downside protection is structural.

India VIX

India VIX is a volatility index computed by NSE from the order book of near-term index option contracts. It expresses, as an annualised percentage, the volatility the options market is pricing for roughly the next 30 days.

TEXT

  Monthly expected move = VIX / sqrt(12)

NEW PREMIUM, 25,200 CE, spot 25,180, 4 days left, IV 13%:
  Roughly Rs 96.00 per unit

YOUR P&L
  Entry  148.00
  Exit    96.00
  Loss   -52.00 per unit  x 75  =  -Rs 3,900   (-35%)

DECOMPOSING WHAT HAPPENED
  Gain from spot moving +180 pts (delta effect)   +Rs  71 /unit
  Loss from IV falling 22% -> 13% (vega effect)   -Rs 102 /unit
  Loss from 2 days passing (theta effect)         -Rs  21 /unit
  ------------------------------------------------------------
  Net                                             -Rs  52 /unit

YOU WERE RIGHT ABOUT DIRECTION AND LOST 35%.

This is routine. It is not bad luck. The market priced the event
INTO the premium before the event. You bought the uncertainty at
its most expensive moment and sold it at its cheapest.

THE GENERAL RULE
  Buying options immediately before a known, scheduled event is
  buying high IV. You need the move to exceed what is ALREADY
  priced in - not merely to go the right way.
Expiry-day option buyingOn expiry day, the remaining premium of an out-of-the-money option is almost entirely time value that must decay to exactly zero within hours. Theta is at its maximum and gamma makes the position violently sensitive. The product is cheap in rupees (₹5, ₹12, ₹30 per unit) which makes it feel low-risk. The probability of any given OTM expiry-day option finishing in the money is low — often in the single digits to low teens — and the loss when it does not is 100%, not 20%. This is where SEBI's data shows the heaviest concentration of small-account losses. The cheapness is the trap: it encourages repeated small bets with a deeply negative expected value, which compounds relentlessly.

The Greeks

The Greeks are partial derivatives: each one answers "if exactly one input moves by one unit and everything else is held still, how much does my option's value change?" They are the vocabulary of risk for anyone holding options, and they are the difference between knowing what you own and guessing.

Delta

TEXT
DELTA - THREE SIMULTANEOUS MEANINGS
===================================
1. RATE OF CHANGE
   "If the index moves 1 point, my option premium moves by
    DELTA points."

2. EQUIVALENT UNDERLYING POSITION ("delta equivalent")
   A 0.50 delta call on a 75 lot behaves like owning
   0.50 x 75 = 37.5 units of the index.

3. ROUGH PROBABILITY OF FINISHING IN THE MONEY
   A 0.30 delta call has roughly a 30% chance of expiring ITM.
   (This is an approximation of N(d2), not N(d1), but close
    enough for position sizing intuition.)

   Long call  delta: 0 to +1
   Short call delta: 0 to -1
   Long put   delta: 0 to -1
   Short put  delta: 0 to +1
   Long 1 futures lot: delta = +1 per unit

Gamma

TEXT
GAMMA - THE RATE OF CHANGE OF DELTA
===================================
Gamma tells you how fast your exposure changes as the market moves.
High gamma = your position's character changes rapidly.

WHERE GAMMA LIVES
  - Highest: at the money, close to expiry
  - Lowest : deep ITM, deep OTM, or far from expiry

Index 25,000, IV 14%.  Gamma per 1-point move:

Strike  Type   18 days   7 days   2 days   Expiry day (3 hrs)
------  ----  ---------  -------  -------  ------------------
24,400  CE     0.00018   0.00009  0.00001        0.00000
25,000  CE     0.00056   0.00092  0.00171        0.00480
25,600  CE     0.00021   0.00014  0.00002        0.00000

(Index-point gammas are small numbers; multiply by the move
 and the lot to get something you can feel.)

WORKED - WHY GAMMA MATTERS ON EXPIRY DAY
----------------------------------------
Position: SHORT 1 lot of the ATM 25,000 CE on expiry morning.
Premium Rs 42.00. Delta -0.50. Gamma 0.0048.

Index moves up 100 points to 25,100.
  New delta = -0.50 - (0.0048 x 100) = -0.50 - 0.48 = -0.98

In ONE HUNDRED POINTS, your position went from behaving like
half a short futures lot to behaving like a FULL short futures
lot. You did not change anything. The market changed your
position for you.

Index moves up another 100 points to 25,200.
  Delta is now pinned near -1.00. You are fully short.
  Each further point costs you 75 rupees, with no decay left
  to offset it.

P&L on that 200-point move:
  Premium went from 42.00 to roughly 205.00
  Loss = (205 - 42) x 75 = -Rs 12,225
  against a maximum possible gain of 42 x 75 = Rs 3,150.

A 0.8% index move on expiry day turned a Rs 3,150 maximum gain
into a Rs 12,225 loss. That is gamma.

THE RULE
  Long gamma  = the market's moves help you (you get longer as
                it rises, shorter as it falls). You pay theta
                for this privilege.
  Short gamma = the market's moves hurt you, increasingly, in
                both directions. You are PAID theta to accept it.

"Collect the theta, ignore the gamma" is the single most common
way Indian retail option sellers blow up on expiry day.

TEXT
THETA - TIME DECAY
==================
Theta is quoted as the premium lost per CALENDAR DAY, all else equal.
For a long option it is always negative. Always.

NOTICE THE SHAPE. The premium does not decay in a straight line.
It decays in proportion to sqrt(time). Roughly:

   Premium is proportional to sqrt(t)

   Half the time remaining  -> only 29% of the premium is gone
   (sqrt(0.5) = 0.707, so 70.7% of value remains)

   Last 7 days of a 30-day option: you lose 137 of 282 points,
   i.e. 49% of the value in the last 23% of the time.

   Last 2 days: 72.50 points, 26% of the original value in the
   last 6.7% of the time.

TWO OPPOSITE CONCLUSIONS, BOTH POPULAR, BOTH INCOMPLETE
-------------------------------------------------------
BUYER'S TRAP: "Buy long-dated, decay is slow." True, but
  long-dated options are expensive, have low gamma, and tie up
  capital. You need a bigger move to profit.

SELLER'S TRAP: "Sell short-dated, decay is fastest." Also true,
  and it is exactly where gamma is highest. The accelerating
  theta you want and the accelerating gamma that kills you are
  THE SAME PHENOMENON viewed from two angles. You cannot have
  one without the other. There is no arrangement of strikes and
  expiries that gives you fast decay with low gamma risk.

Time value decays roughly with the square root of time remaining, not linearly. The last week is brutal for buyers — and that same week is where gamma risk for sellers is highest.

Vega

TEXT
VEGA - SENSITIVITY TO IMPLIED VOLATILITY
========================================
Vega = premium change for a 1 PERCENTAGE POINT change in IV.
Long options are always long vega. Short options always short.

WHERE VEGA LIVES
  - Highest: at the money, LONG time to expiry
  - Lowest : deep ITM/OTM, or very close to expiry

Index 25,000, IV 14%. Vega per 1 point of IV:

Strike  Type   45 days   18 days   7 days   1 day
------  ----  --------  --------  -------  -------
25,000  CE      22.50     14.20      8.80     3.30
25,300  CE      19.80     10.60      5.10     0.95
25,900  CE      11.20      3.40      0.70     0.03

WORKED - A VEGA DISASTER
------------------------
Position: SHORT STRANGLE
  SELL 1 lot 25,600 CE @ 34.50
  SELL 1 lot 24,400 PE @ 46.00
  Total premium received = (34.50 + 46.00) x 75 = Rs 6,037.50
  18 days to expiry. IV = 14%.
  Combined vega (short) = -(3.40 + 4.10) = -7.50 per IV point

OVERNIGHT: a global risk event. The index opens DOWN only 0.6%
to 24,850 - a small move. But IV jumps from 14% to 24%.

Change in premium from VEGA alone:
  IV up 10 points x vega 7.50 = 75.00 points against you
  75.00 x 75 = -Rs 5,625

Change from DELTA (index down 150 points):
  Call delta +0.13 (short, so helps): +0.13 x 150 =  +19.5 pts
  Put  delta -0.14 (short, so hurts): -0.14 x -150 = -21.0 pts
  Net roughly flat.

NET: you have lost almost your entire maximum possible profit
(Rs 6,038) on a 0.6% index move, because VOLATILITY repriced.

Worse: your margin requirement just went UP, because SPAN
margin scales with volatility. You may face a margin call on a
position that is only slightly out of the money.

THE LESSON
  Short-premium positions are short volatility. You can be
  completely right about direction and lose on vega. For a
  retail account with no vega hedge, this is an unmanaged risk.

Rho

TEXT
RHO - SENSITIVITY TO INTEREST RATES
===================================
Rho = premium change for a 1 percentage point change in r.

Index 25,000, 18 days, IV 14%:
  25,000 CE rho = +0.63   (per 1% rate change)
  25,000 PE rho = -0.60

A 25 basis point policy rate change moves the ATM call premium
by about 0.63 x 0.25 = 0.16 points = Rs 12 on a 75 lot.

CONCLUSION: for short-dated Indian equity options, RHO IS NOISE.
It matters for LEAPS-style long-dated options and for interest
rate derivatives. For a weekly or monthly index option it is
below the bid-ask spread. Know what it is; do not think about it.

Gamma, theta and vega all peak at the moneyThat is not a coincidence — they are three views of the same underlying quantity: how much optionality the contract contains. At-the-money options have the most optionality and therefore the most of all three sensitivities. There is no strike that gives you high theta with low gamma, and no strike that gives you high gamma with low theta cost. Every option strategy is a trade-off within that constraint.

The one habit that separates serious option traders from gamblersBefore entering any multi-leg position, write out the net delta, gamma, theta and vega, convert each into rupees, and then write one sentence describing what you are actually betting on. If the sentence is "I am betting the index stays inside a range and volatility does not rise", you now know you have two ways to lose and one way to win. Most retail traders enter iron condors without ever converting vega into rupees.

Core option strategies, with the hidden risk stated

Thirteen structures follow. Each gets a construction, worked maximum profit and loss, break-evens, the circumstance in which it is defensible, and — the part usually omitted — the risk that is not visible on the payoff diagram. All examples use a hypothetical index at 25,000 with a lot size of 75 and 18 days to expiry, except where a single stock is required.

A strategy is not an edgeKnowing the construction of an iron condor is like knowing the rules of chess. It tells you nothing about whether you should play, against whom, or at what stake. Every structure below has a negative expected value for a participant with no edge, once costs are included. The structures are tools for shaping risk, not for creating returns.

1. Covered call

Covered call. You have sold the right-hand tail of your own stock position in exchange for a fixed cash payment. The left tail is untouched.
TEXT
COVERED CALL - WORKED
=====================
This one needs a single stock, because you must OWN the shares.
Hypothetical: a share trading at Rs 1,800, F&O lot size 500.

POSITION
  You already own 500 shares at an average cost of Rs 1,800
  Holding value = 500 x 1,800 = Rs 9,00,000
  SELL 1 lot of the 1,900 CE @ Rs 42.00
  Premium received = 42 x 500 = Rs 21,000

NUMBERS
  Break-even on the combined position = 1,800 - 42 = Rs 1,758
  Max profit = (1,900 - 1,800 + 42) x 500 = 142 x 500 = Rs 71,000
               (achieved at any price at or above 1,900)
  Max loss   = (1,800 - 42) x 500 = Rs 8,79,000  (share to zero)

expiry     (Rs)         (Rs)          (Rs)         Rs 9,00,000
--------   -----------  ------------  -----------  -----------

 1,800                  0   +21,000      +21,000      +2.3%

 2,500       +3,50,000   -2,79,000      +71,000      +7.9%

WHEN IT IS DEFENSIBLE
  - You own the shares, intend to keep owning them, and would be
    genuinely happy to sell at 1,900.
  - You expect the share to drift sideways or rise modestly.
  - You accept that a 39% rally (to 2,500) earns you the same
    Rs 71,000 as a 5.6% rally.

THE HIDDEN RISKS
  1. OPPORTUNITY LOSS IS REAL LOSS. At 2,500 you forgo
     Rs 2,79,000. "I still made money" is the wrong frame -
     you sold a Rs 2.79 lakh outcome for Rs 21,000.
  2. YOU STILL OWN THE ENTIRE DOWNSIDE. At 1,400 you are down
     Rs 1.79 lakh. The Rs 21,000 premium covered 2.3% of a
     22% fall. A covered call is NOT a hedge.
  3. PHYSICAL SETTLEMENT. Indian single-stock options settle by
     physical delivery. If the share closes above 1,900 on
     expiry, your 500 shares WILL be delivered out. You need
     them in the demat account, unencumbered, and you will face
     delivery margins in the expiry week (see the margin section).
  4. TAX EVENT. Delivery triggers a capital gains event on the
     shares at a price you did not choose.
  5. YOU CANNOT SELL THE SHARES while short the call without
     converting the position into a naked short call.

2. Protective put

TEXT
PROTECTIVE PUT - WORKED
=======================
Long the underlying + long a put. The textbook hedge.

POSITION (index version, for portfolio hedging)
  Portfolio value                  Rs 10,00,000
  Index spot                       25,000
  BUY 1 lot 24,400 PE @ 46.00  (about 2.4% OTM)
  Cost = 46.00 x 75 = Rs 3,450

  Note the notional mismatch: one 24,400 put covers
  24,400 x 75 = Rs 18.3 lakh of notional against a Rs 10 lakh
  portfolio. You are over-hedged by about 83%.
  This is the lot-size granularity problem again.

SCENARIO TABLE (assume portfolio beta 1.0 for clarity)

level     P&L         (gross, Rs)     (Rs)        (Rs)
--------  ----------  --------------  ----------  ----------

25,000           0              0      -3,450       -3,450
24,400     -24,000              0      -3,450      -27,450

READ THIS CAREFULLY
  1. Between 25,000 and 24,400 the put does NOTHING. You have a
     2.4% deductible. The worst net outcome is -Rs 27,450, at
     exactly the strike.
  2. Below 24,400 the OVER-HEDGE flips you net positive in a
     crash. That is not a hedge; that is a short position with
     extra steps. A correctly sized hedge would leave you flat.
  3. The Rs 3,450 cost is 0.35% of the portfolio for 18 days.
     Annualised, running this continuously costs roughly 7% a
     year - which will exceed the long-run equity risk premium
     you are trying to protect.

WHEN IT IS DEFENSIBLE
  - A SPECIFIC, TIME-BOXED risk you want to cross: a results
    season, a budget, a known binary event, a period when you
    need the capital intact for a real-world reason.
  - NOT as a permanent overlay. Permanent put buying is a
    reliable way to underperform.

HIDDEN RISKS
  - You pay the volatility skew: OTM puts carry the chain's
    highest IV. You are buying the most expensive insurance
    in the market, by design.
  - Basis and lot granularity mean the hedge is imperfect.
  - If the market drifts sideways you lose the premium with
    nothing to show for it - which is the hedge WORKING.

3. Collar

TEXT
COLLAR - WORKED
===============
Long underlying + long OTM put + short OTM call.
You buy the downside protection and PAY for it by selling
the upside. Often structured to be "zero cost".

POSITION (single stock, share at Rs 1,800, lot 500)
  Own 500 shares at Rs 1,800
  BUY  1,700 PE @ Rs 38.00  -> cost 38 x 500 = Rs 19,000
  SELL 1,950 CE @ Rs 34.00  -> credit 34 x 500 = Rs 17,000
  NET COST = Rs 2,000  (near zero cost)

NUMBERS
  Floor (protected below)  = 1,700
  Cap   (capped above)     = 1,950
  Net cost per share       = Rs 4.00
  Max loss = (1,800 - 1,700 + 4) x 500 = 104 x 500 = Rs 52,000
  Max gain = (1,950 - 1,800 - 4) x 500 = 146 x 500 = Rs 73,000

  - You have a target exit price anyway, and a pain threshold.

HIDDEN RISKS
  - You have sold your upside. In a sustained rally this is
    expensive and psychologically brutal.
  - Both legs are physically settled on Indian single stocks.
    Assignment on the call delivers your shares out; exercise
    of the put delivers them out too. Plan for both.
  - "Zero cost" is marketing. The cost is the forgone upside,
    which in a bull market dwarfs the premium saved.
  - Skew works against you: the put you buy has higher IV than
    the call you sell, so a strictly symmetric collar around
    spot is usually a net DEBIT, not zero.

4. Cash-secured put

TEXT
CASH-SECURED PUT - WORKED
=========================
Sell a put AND set aside the full cash to buy the underlying
if assigned. The collateral is what distinguishes this from a
naked short put.

POSITION (single stock at Rs 1,800, lot 500)
  You want to own this share, but at Rs 1,700, not Rs 1,800.
  SELL 1 lot 1,700 PE @ Rs 38.00
  Premium received = Rs 19,000
  CASH SET ASIDE = 1,700 x 500 = Rs 8,50,000

  (If you do not set this aside, you are running a leveraged
   short put, not a cash-secured put. The margin blocked might
   be only Rs 1.4 lakh - that is the point of the distinction.)

OUTCOMES
  Share > 1,700 at expiry : put expires worthless.
    You keep Rs 19,000. Return on Rs 8,50,000 = 2.24% in 18 days.
    Annualised that looks like 45%. IGNORE THAT NUMBER - you
    cannot repeat it reliably and the tail is what matters.

  Share < 1,700 at expiry : you are ASSIGNED.
    You buy 500 shares at Rs 1,700 = Rs 8,50,000 outflow.
    Effective cost = 1,700 - 38 = Rs 1,662 per share.
    Physical delivery - the shares land in your demat account.

THE TRADE-OFF, PLAINLY
  The spread loses LESS when wrong (Rs 9,169 vs Rs 16,388)
  and earns MORE for a modest move (to 25,300).
  It earns far less for a large move (Rs 13,331 vs Rs 96,113
  at 26,500).

is the better expression. If your view is "up enormously", the
spread caps exactly the outcome you were trying to capture.

WHEN IT IS DEFENSIBLE
  - You have a bounded directional view with a target price.
  - You want defined risk and a lower break-even than a naked
    long call.
  - IV is elevated: the short leg recovers some of the inflated
    premium you are paying on the long leg.

  - Liquidity: the far strike may have a wider spread; you may
    not get the mid price on both legs.
  - If the index closes between 25,000 and 25,300 at expiry,
    the long leg is ITM and the short is not. For INDEX options
    this is cash-settled and harmless. For STOCK options it
    creates a physical delivery obligation on one leg only -
    a very expensive surprise. Never hold a stock vertical
    through expiry without understanding this.

7. Long straddle

TEXT
LONG STRADDLE - WORKED
======================

THE PROBABILITY CHECK - DO THIS EVERY TIME
------------------------------------------
Expected 1-SD move at IV 14%, 18 days = 777 points (computed
earlier). Your break-evens are at +/- 414.50 points, which is
414.50 / 777 = 0.53 standard deviations.

Probability of finishing OUTSIDE +/- 0.53 SD is roughly 60%.
So this looks favourable? NO - because that 60% includes closes
only just beyond the break-even, where you make almost nothing.

The honest framing: the straddle is priced so that the EXPECTED
profit is approximately zero before costs and NEGATIVE after.
You are not getting a 60% edge. You are paying for a move the
market has already priced.

WHEN IT IS DEFENSIBLE
  - You believe realised volatility will EXCEED implied
    volatility - i.e. you think 777 points is too low an
    estimate of the coming move. That is a volatility view,
    not a direction view, and it is a hard view to hold well.
  - BEFORE an event where IV has NOT yet risen. Buying a
    straddle after IV has already inflated is the IV-crush trap.

HIDDEN RISKS
  - DOUBLE THETA. You are paying decay on two options. At 18
    days that is roughly (5.90 + 5.40) x 75 = Rs 848 per day.
    Fourteen flat days cost you Rs 11,872 - 38% of the premium
    - with the index unchanged.
  - DOUBLE VEGA, which cuts both ways. If IV falls 3 points you
    lose roughly (14.20 + 13.60) x 3 x 75 = Rs 6,255.
  - You need the move to be BIG and SOON. Big but slow is a loss.

    outside to make a worthwhile return is considerably lower.
  - Pinning: markets often close near large open-interest
    strikes on expiry. Your 600-point dead zone is where the
    index spends most of its time.

THE PITCH AND THE ARITHMETIC
----------------------------
THE PITCH: "The index stays within 1.66% about 70% of the time.
Sell the straddle every cycle and collect Rs 31,088. Twelve
cycles a year = Rs 3.7 lakh on Rs 2.6 lakh of margin."

THE ARITHMETIC: run it as a 12-cycle sequence where 10 cycles
land in the profit zone and 2 do not.

  10 winning cycles, average profit 60% of max:
      10 x 18,653 =  +Rs 1,86,530
   1 moderate loss (index moves 3%):
       1 x -43,913 =   -Rs 43,913
   1 bad cycle (index moves 7% - happens most years):
       1 x -1,93,913 = -Rs 1,93,913
  ---------------------------------------------------
  TWELVE-MONTH TOTAL                 = -Rs 51,296

  Plus 48 legs of transaction costs (12 cycles x 4 legs),
  roughly Rs 350 per leg all-in on this size = -Rs 16,800
  ---------------------------------------------------
  NET                                = -Rs 68,096

  On Rs 2.6 lakh of margin that is -26% for a year of work
  in which you were RIGHT 83% OF THE TIME.

THIS IS THE CORE OF WHY ~90% LOSE.
A high win rate with an unbounded loss is not an edge.
It is a loan from the market that gets called in.

HIDDEN RISKS BEYOND THE PAYOFF TABLE
  1. VEGA. A volatility spike loses you money with no index
     move at all. See the vega worked example earlier.
  2. MARGIN EXPANSION. When volatility rises, SPAN margin rises.
     Your position moves against you AND your margin requirement
     increases simultaneously. You can be forced to close at the
     worst price because you cannot fund the margin.
  3. GAP RISK. Overnight gaps bypass every stop loss you set.
     There is no stop that protects a short straddle from a
     7% gap open.
  4. GAMMA on expiry day. See the gamma worked example.
  5. SEBI's additional expiry-day tail-risk margin on short
     options exists precisely because of this risk profile.
     Verify current requirements at nseindia.com.
Short straddles and strangles are the single most dangerous retail structureThey are popular because they win frequently and the losses arrive rarely. That combination is maximally seductive and maximally destructive. A retail account with no vega hedge, no intraday risk system, no ability to trade through a gap, and a margin requirement that expands exactly when the position is losing, has no business holding an uncovered short straddle. If you take one instruction from this guide, take this one.

the tail. Sometimes it does. You will not know when.

THE BREAK-EVEN WIN RATE
-----------------------
Let p = probability of a full-profit outcome.
Ignore partial outcomes for simplicity.

  EV = p x 4,012.50 - (1-p) x 18,487.50 = 0
  p x 4,012.50 = 18,487.50 - p x 18,487.50
  p x 22,500 = 18,487.50
  p = 0.8217

You must win 82.2% of the time merely to break even,
BEFORE transaction costs on eight legs.

With costs of roughly Rs 1,800 per complete round trip
(8 legs on this size):
  EV = p x 4,012.50 - (1-p) x 18,487.50 - 1,800 = 0
  p x 22,500 = 20,287.50
  p = 0.9017

YOU MUST WIN MORE THAN 90% OF THE TIME.

Is a 90% win rate achievable? The structure's own probability
of full profit is roughly 86%. The market has priced the credit
so that it is NOT comfortably achievable. This is not a flaw in
the iron condor; it is the market functioning.

WHEN IT IS DEFENSIBLE
  - You have a genuine, evidenced view that implied volatility
    is too high relative to what will be realised.
  - You can manage the position actively and will close early
    rather than holding into gamma risk.
  - You size it so that several consecutive max losses do not
    matter to you.

HIDDEN RISKS
  - EIGHT legs of transaction cost. This is the dominant
    practical problem.
  - The wings are OTM and illiquid; you may not get good fills,
    especially when you need to exit urgently.
  - Managing the position (rolling a tested side) converts the
    defined risk into something else and adds more cost.
  - The temptation to "widen the wings for more credit" is the
    temptation to convert it back into a short strangle.

13. Calendar spread (horizontal)

TEXT
CALENDAR SPREAD - WORKED
========================
SELL 1 lot NEAR-expiry 25,000 CE  @ 218.50 (18 days) +Rs 16,387.50
BUY  1 lot FAR-expiry  25,000 CE  @ 352.00 (46 days) -Rs 26,400.00
NET DEBIT = 133.50 points                            = Rs 10,012.50

WHAT YOU ARE ACTUALLY TRADING
  Not direction. You are long the DIFFERENCE between the far
  option's slower decay and the near option's faster decay,
  and you are LONG VEGA on the back month.

  Net theta  : POSITIVE (near leg decays faster than far leg)
  Net vega   : POSITIVE (far leg has more vega than near leg)
  Net delta  : near zero at entry
  Net gamma  : NEGATIVE (short the high-gamma near option)

THE UNUSUAL PROPERTY
  This is the only common structure that is simultaneously
  POSITIVE THETA and POSITIVE VEGA. Most structures force you
  to choose. That is why it is used by desks ahead of an
  expected volatility expansion.

Margins in India: SPAN, exposure and the penalty regime

Margin is not a deposit and it is not a cost. It is collateral the clearing corporation holds against the possibility that you will be unable to meet your obligations. Understanding exactly how it is computed, when it changes, and what happens when you fall short is more important to your survival than any strategy in the previous section.

Margin rules in India changed repeatedly between 2020 and 2026Peak margin reporting, upfront margin collection, the pledge/re-pledge mechanism for collateral, upfront premium collection from option buyers, and expiry-day tail-risk margin have all been introduced or revised. Every number in this section is illustrative as at late 2026. Compute live requirements on the exchange margin calculator at nseindia.com and confirm with your broker, who may impose a higher requirement than the exchange minimum.

How SPAN actually works

TEXT
SPAN - STANDARD PORTFOLIO ANALYSIS OF RISK
==========================================
SPAN does NOT apply a fixed percentage. It simulates your ENTIRE
portfolio across a grid of scenarios and charges you the worst one.

THE SCENARIO GRID (conceptually, 16 scenarios)
  For each combination of:
     price move: 0, +1/3, -1/3, +2/3, -2/3, +3/3, -3/3 of the
                 "price scan range"
     volatility move: up or down by the "volatility scan range"
  ... plus two extreme-move scenarios at a fractional weight.

  Compute the portfolio's P&L in each scenario.
  SPAN margin = the LARGEST LOSS across all scenarios.

WHY THIS MATTERS TO YOU
---------------------------------------------------------------
1. HEDGED POSITIONS GET MARGIN RELIEF AUTOMATICALLY.
   An iron condor's wings cap the loss in the extreme scenarios,
   so SPAN charges far less than for a naked strangle. You do
   not have to ask for this; SPAN sees the whole portfolio.

2. MARGIN RISES WITH VOLATILITY.
   The price scan range and volatility scan range are derived
   from recent volatility. When the market gets violent, the
   scan ranges widen, and EVERY position's margin goes up -
   including positions you opened weeks ago.

3. THE SEQUENCE THAT KILLS ACCOUNTS
   Market gaps -> your short option loses money (MTM debit)
               -> volatility spikes
               -> SPAN scan range widens
               -> margin requirement INCREASES
               -> you now have less cash AND need more margin
               -> margin shortfall -> penalty -> forced square-off
               -> you realise the loss at the worst price

   Three independent things hit you simultaneously and they are
   CORRELATED BY CONSTRUCTION. This is not bad luck.

Peak margin and intraday monitoring

TEXT
PEAK MARGIN - WHAT CHANGED AND WHY IT MATTERS
=============================================
HISTORICALLY: margin was checked at END OF DAY. A trader could
run a huge intraday position on minimal margin, square off
before close, and never be measured.

NOW: the clearing corporation takes random SNAPSHOTS during the
session (multiple per day) and the HIGHEST margin requirement
observed across those snapshots is the "peak margin". You must
have had that margin available at that moment.

CONSEQUENCE FOR INTRADAY TRADERS
  The leverage available intraday is now broadly similar to the
  leverage available overnight. The old "20x intraday margin"
  offers largely disappeared.

SEBI'S 2024-25 ADDITION: INTRADAY POSITION LIMIT MONITORING
  Exchanges now monitor derivative position limits intraday
  rather than only at end of day, with a specified number of
  random snapshots. The intent is to prevent participants from
  exceeding limits intraday and unwinding before the end-of-day
  measurement.

Verify current peak-margin methodology, snapshot frequency and
position-limit monitoring rules at sebi.gov.in and nseindia.com.
As at late 2026.

Margin shortfall penalties

TEXT
MARGIN SHORTFALL PENALTY - ILLUSTRATIVE STRUCTURE
=================================================
If the margin available falls below the requirement, the
clearing corporation levies a penalty on the SHORTFALL amount,
passed through to you by your broker.

Illustrative slab structure (VERIFY CURRENT RATES):

  Shortfall size                        Penalty rate (per day)
  ------------------------------------  ----------------------
  < Rs 1 lakh AND < 10% of requirement        0.5%
  >= Rs 1 lakh OR  >= 10% of requirement      1.0%

  If the shortfall persists for more than 3 consecutive days,
  or occurs more than 5 times in a month, a higher rate
  (illustratively 5%) may apply.

WORKED
  Requirement        Rs 2,19,375
  Available          Rs 1,85,000
  Shortfall          Rs   34,375  (15.7% of requirement)
  -> 15.7% exceeds the 10% threshold -> 1.0% rate

  Plus GST on the penalty.

  higher rate: 34,375 x 0.05 x 4 = Rs 6,875 plus GST.

  You are most likely to face a shortfall exactly when the
  market has moved against you and margin requirements have
  risen. The penalty therefore arrives on your worst day,
  on top of the loss itself.

shortfall. That square-off happens at the broker's discretion,
at market, possibly at the worst price of the day, and the
broker may charge a square-off fee on top.

Physical delivery margins in expiry week

This is where beginners get genuinely hurtSingle-stock F&O in India settles by physical delivery. If you hold an in-the-money stock option or any stock futures position into expiry, you take or give actual shares. In the days before expiry the exchange levies escalating delivery margins on positions likely to go to delivery, because the settlement obligation is for the full contract value, not the margin.
TEXT
DELIVERY MARGIN RAMP - ILLUSTRATIVE
===================================
Hypothetical share at Rs 1,800, lot 500.
You are SHORT 1 lot of the 1,750 CE (in the money).
If it stays ITM, you must DELIVER 500 shares at Rs 1,750.
Settlement obligation = 1,750 x 500 = Rs 8,75,000

illustrative ramp applied to the delivery obligation:

  Session               % of delivery margin applied
  --------------------  ----------------------------
  Expiry minus 4 days              10%
  Expiry minus 3 days              25%
  Expiry minus 2 days              45%
  Expiry minus 1 day               70%
  Expiry day                      100%

  Expiry minus 1 day requirement, illustrative:
    delivery margin base (say 20% VaR-type figure on
    Rs 8,75,000) = Rs 1,75,000, x 70% = Rs 1,22,500
    ON TOP OF your normal SPAN + exposure margin.

WHAT GOES WRONG
  1. You sold a stock call for Rs 8,000 of premium.
  2. It drifts in the money.
  3. Three days before expiry your margin requirement DOUBLES
     because delivery margin kicks in.
  4. You have no spare cash. Shortfall penalty. Forced exit.
  5. Or you hold on and get assigned - and must deliver 500

     market (or facing AUCTION SETTLEMENT at a punitive price).

  a penalty. Auction prices can be materially worse than the
  market price. This is not a theoretical risk.

THE SIMPLE RULE THAT AVOIDS ALL OF THIS
  CLOSE ALL SINGLE-STOCK F&O POSITIONS BEFORE EXPIRY WEEK
  unless you specifically intend to take or give delivery,
  have the shares or the full cash available, and understand
  the margin ramp.

Collateral: cash versus pledged securities

PointDetail (illustrative, verify)
You can pledge securities as marginShares, ETFs and mutual fund units in your demat can be pledged to the broker, who re-pledges to the clearing corporation. You receive collateral value after a haircut.
HaircutThe collateral value is less than market value — e.g. a 20% haircut on a liquid share means ₹1,00,000 of shares gives ₹80,000 of margin. Haircuts are larger for volatile or less liquid securities.
Cash component requirementA minimum proportion of total margin — commonly cited as 50% — must be in cash or cash equivalents. You cannot fund an entire derivatives book with pledged shares.

The STT-on-exercise trap

This one has wiped out profitable positionsSecurities Transaction Tax on a sold option is charged on the premium. STT on an option that is exercised (i.e. expires in the money and settles) is charged on the settlement / intrinsic value, which can be orders of magnitude larger than the premium. If you let a deep in-the-money option expire rather than selling it, you can hand over a large STT bill on a position you thought was a small win.

Assignment, expiry and settlement

More retail damage is done in the last three days of a contract's life than in the preceding three weeks. This section is the operational manual for the end of the cycle.

What happens at expiry, step by step

  1. Trading stopsTrading in the expiring contract ceases at the end of the expiry-day session. There is no extended window.
  2. Settlement price is determinedFor index derivatives, typically the volume-weighted average price of the underlying index over a defined closing period of the expiry day — not the last traded price. For single stocks, the closing price of the underlying.
  3. Out-of-the-money options expire worthlessNothing happens. The premium paid is lost in full by the buyer and retained in full by the seller. No STT on exercise, no settlement.
  4. In-the-money options are automatically exercisedYou do not need to do anything. Indian exchanges automatically exercise all ITM options at expiry (subject to any applicable do-not-exercise facility). This is why "I did not want to exercise" is not a defence.
  5. Index options settle in cashThe intrinsic value is credited to the buyer and debited from the seller. STT on exercise applies to the buyer on the settlement value.
  6. Stock options settle by physical deliveryThe call buyer receives shares and pays the strike; the call seller delivers shares and receives the strike. The put buyer delivers shares and receives the strike; the put seller receives shares and pays the strike.
  7. Failure to deliver triggers auctionIf you are obliged to deliver shares you do not hold, the exchange procures them in the auction market at your cost, plus penalties.

  REQUIREMENT: 500 shares in your demat, unencumbered.
  If you do not have them: short delivery -> auction -> you pay
  the auction price plus penalty, which can far exceed Rs 1,850.

CASE 3: You are LONG a 1,900 PE (ITM by 50)
  You must DELIVER 500 shares
  You RECEIVE      1,900 x 500 = Rs 9,50,000
  REQUIREMENT: 500 shares in demat. Same auction risk as Case 2.

CASE 4: You are SHORT a 1,900 PE (ITM by 50)
  You must PAY   Rs 9,50,000
  You RECEIVE    500 shares
  REQUIREMENT: Rs 9,50,000 in cash.

THE PATTERN
  TWO of the four cases require you to produce LARGE CASH.
  TWO of the four require you to produce SHARES YOU MAY NOT OWN.
  None of the four is satisfied by the margin you posted.

A position with Rs 40,000 of margin can create a Rs 9.5 lakh
settlement obligation. That ratio is the whole hazard.

THE OPERATIONAL RULE
  Put a recurring calendar entry three trading days before every
  monthly expiry. Review every single-stock F&O position. Close
  anything you do not intend to settle physically. Do not rely
  on your broker's auto square-off, which happens at the
  broker's chosen time and price and may carry a fee.
Do-not-exercise (DNE)At various points Indian exchanges have permitted a "do not exercise" instruction for close-to-the-money options, so that a buyer whose option is barely in the money can avoid a settlement whose STT and delivery costs exceed the intrinsic value. The availability, the eligible strikes and the broker-level support for this have changed over time. Check with your broker and at nseindia.com whether DNE is currently available and for which contracts. Do not assume it exists.

Why most retail F&O traders lose: the arithmetic

The loss rate is not a mystery and it is not primarily about psychology, though psychology makes it worse. It is a stack of independently sufficient mathematical reasons, any one of which would produce a negative expectancy on its own. Together they produce a near-certainty.

The eight reasons, in order of magnitude

#ReasonEffect on expectancy
1Negative sum after costsThe game pays out less than it takes in. Average participant loses by the amount of the friction, before any skill differential.
2Adverse counterparty selectionYou are trading against the participants who price these instruments for a living. The average retail fill is worse than the average professional fill.
3Leverage mis-sizingPosition sizes are chosen by premium outlay, not by delta-equivalent exposure, producing 3–5x the intended risk.
4Selling tailsHigh-win-rate, unbounded-loss structures create a long run of small wins terminated by one catastrophic loss.
5Over-tradingCost drag scales linearly with frequency; a 40%+ annual drag is routine at retail sizes.
6Behavioural errorsRevenge trading, averaging down, cutting winners early, holding losers, position-size escalation after losses.
7Survivorship bias in the information dietYou only see the winning screenshots. Your estimate of achievable returns is calibrated on a non-random sample.
8Instrument complexityMost participants do not know their position's vega or gamma, and therefore do not know what they own.

1–2. The expectancy equation, stated properly

TEXT
THE EXPECTANCY EQUATION FOR ANY TRADING ACTIVITY
================================================
  E = (P_win x Avg_win) - (P_loss x Avg_loss) - Costs

For a PURE zero-sum game with no skill differential:
  (P_win x Avg_win) = (P_loss x Avg_loss)
  Therefore E = -Costs,  always negative.

To have E > 0 you need:
  (P_win x Avg_win) - (P_loss x Avg_loss) > Costs

i.e. your skill edge must EXCEED the friction. Not match it.
Exceed it, persistently, across hundreds of trades, net of the
fact that your counterparty is also trying to have an edge.

QUANTIFYING THE HURDLE FOR A TYPICAL RETAIL OPTION TRADER
---------------------------------------------------------
Account                         Rs 1,00,000
Average position premium        Rs 7,000
Round trips per month           20
Round-trip cost per trade       Rs 176 (computed earlier)
Monthly cost                    Rs 3,520
Annual cost                     Rs 42,240  = 42.2% of account

For E > 0 you need a gross edge of more than 42% a year on
account equity, or 2.5% per trade on premium, purely to
overcome friction.

WHAT 2.5% PER TRADE MEANS
  If your average winner is +30% of premium and your average
  loser is -40% of premium (typical for directional option
  buying), then to net +2.5% you need:

    0.30 w - 0.40 (1-w) = 0.025
    0.30 w - 0.40 + 0.40 w = 0.025
    0.70 w = 0.425
    w = 0.607

  You need a 61% WIN RATE on directional option trades, every
  month, forever, just to break even after costs.

  For context: the implied probability that an ATM option
  finishes in the money is roughly 50%. You are being asked
  to beat a coin flip by 11 percentage points, persistently,
  against professionals.

THAT IS THE ENTIRE GAME, EXPRESSED IN ONE NUMBER.

3. Position sizing: the error almost everyone makes

TEXT
SIZING BY PREMIUM vs SIZING BY EXPOSURE
=======================================
Account: Rs 2,00,000. You decide "I will risk 10% per trade,
so Rs 20,000 of premium."

TRADE: buy 25,600 CE @ 34.50 (delta 0.13), lot size 75
  Premium per lot = 34.50 x 75 = Rs 2,587.50
  Rs 20,000 / 2,587.50 = 7.7 -> you buy 7 lots
  Premium outlay = Rs 18,112.50  ("only 9% of the account")

WHAT YOU ACTUALLY OWN
  Delta-equivalent units = 7 lots x 75 x 0.13 = 68.25 units
  Notional exposure      = 68.25 x 25,000 = Rs 17,06,250

  You have Rs 17 lakh of index exposure on a Rs 2 lakh account.
  That is 8.5x leverage, achieved while telling yourself you
  risked 9%.

NOW ADD GAMMA
  Gamma per unit 0.00021. If the index rallies 400 points:
  New delta = 0.13 + (0.00021 x 400) = 0.214
  Delta-equivalent = 7 x 75 x 0.214 = 112.4 units
  Notional = Rs 28,09,000

  Your exposure grew 65% without you doing anything.

AND THE DOWNSIDE
  If the index finishes below 25,600, you lose Rs 18,112.50 -
  100% of the premium, 9% of the account - on a trade that had
  roughly a 13% probability of finishing in the money.

  Expected value, crudely:
    0.13 x (some average ITM payoff, say Rs 30,000)
      = +Rs 3,900
    0.87 x (-Rs 18,112.50)
      = -Rs 15,758
    EV = -Rs 11,858 per trade, before costs.

  Repeat that eight times and the account is gone.

THE CORRECT SIZING QUESTION
  Not "how much premium am I spending?"
  But  "how many delta-equivalent units of the index do I own,
        and what happens to my account if the index moves 3%?"

4. The maths of "sell options for steady income" — a full blow-up scenario

TEXT
A 24-CYCLE SIMULATION OF SHORT STRANGLE "INCOME"
================================================
Starting capital                 Rs 5,00,000
Strategy: sell a ~2.5% OTM strangle each cycle
Margin per strangle (illustrative) Rs 2,40,000
Position: 1 strangle per cycle (conservative - most people
          scale up after a winning run, which is the problem)
Premium collected per cycle      Rs 6,000 (net, after costs)

CYCLES 1-14: THE MARKET BEHAVES
  14 x Rs 6,000 = +Rs 84,000
  Capital now Rs 5,84,000
  Return so far: +16.8%
  Win rate so far: 100%

  WHAT HAPPENS PSYCHOLOGICALLY HERE
  - You conclude the strategy works.
  - You start posting your results.
  - You scale from 1 strangle to 3 ("I have more capital now
    and I have proven the edge").
  - Margin for 3 strangles = Rs 7,20,000 - more than you have,
    so you PLEDGE your equity portfolio to fund it.
  - Premium per cycle now Rs 18,000.

CYCLES 15-20: STILL FINE
  6 x Rs 18,000 = +Rs 1,08,000
  Capital now Rs 6,92,000
  Return: +38.4% over 20 cycles. Win rate 100%.
  You are now certain. You tell friends.

CYCLE 21: A 6% GAP DOWN OVERNIGHT
  Index gaps from 25,000 to 23,500.
  Your short 24,400 PE (sold at 46.00) is now worth
  roughly 950.00 intrinsic plus elevated time value, say 1,080.

  Loss per put = (1,080 - 46) x 75 = Rs 77,550
  You are short 3 of them       = Rs 2,32,650

  Your short calls expire worthless: +3 x 34.50 x 75 = +Rs 7,763
  NET LOSS ON THE CYCLE         = -Rs 2,24,887

  AND SIMULTANEOUSLY:
  - IV spikes from 14% to 30%, so even if you want to close,
    the exit price is far worse than intrinsic alone.
  - SPAN margin requirement roughly doubles.
  - Your PLEDGED EQUITY COLLATERAL has also fallen 6%, and the
    haircut may have widened, so your available margin falls.
  - You face a margin shortfall, a penalty, and a forced
    square-off at the worst available price.

AFTER CYCLE 21
  Capital: 6,92,000 - 2,24,887 = Rs 4,67,113
  You are now BELOW your Rs 5,00,000 starting capital after
  20 consecutive wins and one loss.

  Win rate: 20/21 = 95.2%
  Return: -6.6%

CYCLE 22: THE AFTERMATH
  Markets remain volatile. You either:
  (a) stop, having learned an expensive lesson, or
  (b) "make it back" by increasing size further - which is how
      the -6.6% becomes -60%.

Option (b) is what the SEBI data is measuring in aggregate.

THE GENERAL RESULT
  With a strategy whose max gain is G and whose tail loss is
  L, and whose tail probability is q, the strategy is
  profitable only if:

      (1-q) x G  >  q x L

  With G = Rs 18,000, L = Rs 2,25,000, that requires:
      (1-q) x 18,000 > q x 225,000
      18,000 - 18,000q > 225,000q
      18,000 > 243,000q
      q < 0.074

  The tail event must occur less than 7.4% of the time - i.e.
  less often than roughly once in 14 cycles. A 6% adverse gap
  in an Indian index over a monthly cycle is not a once-in-14
  event in a long sample. It is closer to once in 10-20, and
  the events cluster.

Figures illustrative throughout. The SHAPE of this result is
robust and is the arithmetic behind SEBI's loss statistics.
The central deception of high-win-rate strategiesA 95% win rate feels like skill. It is a property of the structure, not of the trader. Anyone selling far-out-of-the-money options achieves a high win rate automatically. The win rate tells you nothing about expectancy because it says nothing about the size of the loss. When someone shows you a track record, ask for the maximum drawdown and the largest single loss, not the win rate.

5–6. Over-trading, revenge trading and averaging down

TEXT
THE MARTINGALE / AVERAGING-DOWN TRAP ON OPTIONS
===============================================
"The option fell from 100 to 50 - I will double up and my
average becomes 75, so I only need it back to 75."

WHY THIS IS CATASTROPHIC ON OPTIONS SPECIFICALLY
  1. An option has an EXPIRY. A share can recover over years;
     an option has days. Time is running out while you average.
  2. The fall from 100 to 50 was caused by some combination of
     delta (wrong direction), theta (time passing - which
     continues) and vega (IV falling - often permanently after
     an event). Two of those three are IRREVERSIBLE.
  3. Your position size has doubled while your probability of
     success has fallen.

WORKED SEQUENCE
  Buy 1 lot @ 100.00    outlay Rs  7,500   total Rs  7,500
  Falls to 50: buy 2    outlay Rs  7,500   total Rs 15,000
                        (3 lots, avg 66.67)
  Falls to 25: buy 4    outlay Rs  7,500   total Rs 22,500
                        (7 lots, avg 42.86)
  Falls to 10: buy 8    outlay Rs  6,000   total Rs 28,500
                        (15 lots, avg 25.33)
  Expires at 0:         LOSS Rs 28,500

  You started risking Rs 7,500 and lost Rs 28,500 - 3.8x your
  intended risk - on a single idea that was wrong from the start.

  To have broken even on the final position, the option needed
  to reach 25.33 from 10.00 - a 153% rise - with less time left
  than when you first bought it at 100.

THE STRUCTURAL POINT
  Averaging down converts a bounded loss into a much larger
  bounded loss and converts a small mistake into an account
  event. On options, where the instrument expires, it has no
  defensible version.

COMPARE: averaging down on a diversified index fund during a
drawdown is a reasonable long-horizon behaviour. The two
activities share a name and nothing else.
BehaviourWhat triggers itThe cost, quantified

7. Survivorship bias in your information diet

What you see is not a random sampleIf 100 people trade a weekly expiry and 7 make money, you will see screenshots from the 7. The 93 post nothing. Social platforms amplify extreme positive outcomes because those generate engagement. Your intuition about "what is achievable" is being calibrated on the visible tail of a distribution whose body is invisible to you. This is not a minor distortion — it is the single largest source of unrealistic expectations in retail trading.
  • Screenshots are trivially fabricated, and even genuine ones show one position, not an account.
  • A genuine ₹3 lakh profit screenshot is compatible with a ₹9 lakh year-to-date loss.
  • Nobody posts the margin call. Nobody posts the pledged portfolio that got liquidated.
  • Paid "mentors" earn from subscriptions, not from trading. Their incentive is your enrolment, not your P&L.
  • The only track record worth anything is a full, audited, continuous equity curve with drawdowns — which essentially nobody in retail social media provides.

8. Not knowing what you own

A simple diagnosticOpen your current F&O positions. Without looking anything up, write down: net delta in index units, net theta in rupees per day, net vega in rupees per IV point, and the rupee loss if the index gaps 4% against you overnight. If you cannot produce those four numbers in under a minute, you do not know what you own. That is not a moral failing — it is a reason to stop trading the instrument until you can.

The SEBI regulatory response, 2024–25

In October 2024 SEBI announced a package of measures to "strengthen the equity index derivatives framework for increased investor protection and market stability", phased in from November 2024 through 2025. It is the most significant intervention in Indian retail derivatives in a decade and it exists because of the loss data in section one.

Verify the current frameworkThe measures below are described as announced and as subsequently implemented, as at late 2026. Several were phased, some thresholds have been revised after implementation, and further consultation papers have been issued. The authoritative source is sebi.gov.in (press releases, circulars and consultation papers) and the exchange circulars at nseindia.com. Do not rely on this summary for a trading decision.

2Removal of calendar-spread margin benefit on expiry dayPositions expiring on a given day no longer receive offsetting margin treatment against other expiries on that dayThe basis risk between an expiring and a non-expiring leg is real and was being under-margined
3Intraday monitoring of position limitsExchanges monitor client-level position limits intraday via random snapshots, not just at end of dayPrevent participants exceeding limits intraday and unwinding before measurement

What each measure actually does to a retail position

TEXT
THE MEASURES, TRANSLATED INTO EFFECTS ON YOUR ACCOUNT
=====================================================
1. UPFRONT PREMIUM COLLECTION
   BEFORE: some brokers allowed buying options against
           unsettled funds or notional limits intraday.
   AFTER : you need the cash in the account. Full stop.
   EFFECT: removes a source of uncontrolled intraday leverage.
           Mildly inconvenient; unambiguously protective.

2. CALENDAR-SPREAD MARGIN BENEFIT REMOVED ON EXPIRY DAY
   BEFORE: a near/far spread got substantial margin relief
           even on expiry day.
   AFTER : full margin on both legs that day.
   EFFECT: capital required for spread strategies held into
           expiry roughly doubles. Many such strategies became
           uneconomic for small accounts.

3. INTRADAY POSITION LIMIT MONITORING
   BEFORE: limits measured end of day; intraday excesses
           invisible.
   AFTER : random intraday snapshots; breaches penalised.
   EFFECT: mostly affects larger participants; for retail it
           closes a loophole you probably were not using.

4. MINIMUM CONTRACT SIZE RAISED
   BEFORE: a single index option lot might have had a notional
           of roughly Rs 5 lakh, with premiums of a few
           thousand rupees.
   AFTER : minimum notional raised substantially; one lot now
           represents a much larger exposure.
   EFFECT: this is the BIGGEST change for small accounts.
           - A single lot now costs more in premium
           - Position sizing in anything under 1 lot is
             impossible
           - Hedging a small portfolio became harder, not
             easier (the granularity problem)
           - Small accounts were, in effect, priced out
   HONEST ASSESSMENT: protective in intent, but it also made
           legitimate small-scale hedging harder. SEBI's
           position is that if you cannot afford one contract,
           you cannot afford the risk.

5. WEEKLY EXPIRY RATIONALISATION
   BEFORE: multiple indices with weekly expiries across two
           exchanges produced an expiry nearly every weekday.
   AFTER : one benchmark index per exchange has weekly options.
   EFFECT: the "every day is expiry day" scalping regime ended.
           Volumes in ultra-short-dated options fell materially.
           This is the measure most directly aimed at the
           behaviour the loss data identified.

6. ADDITIONAL EXPIRY-DAY TAIL-RISK MARGIN
   BEFORE: standard SPAN margin on expiry day.
   AFTER : extra margin on SHORT option positions that day.
   EFFECT: expiry-day option selling requires materially more
           capital. Directly targets the gamma-risk problem
           described earlier in this guide.

Did it work?

The honest answer is partially, and the evidence is still being gathered.

What appears to have changed

Index option volumes in the very short-dated segment fell after the weekly-expiry rationalisation. The number of unique individual participants in index options declined from the peak. Average contract size rose, mechanically, from the minimum-size increase.

What did not change

SEBI's own 2025 follow-up study covering FY 2024–25 found roughly 91% of individual index-option traders still losing money. The loss rate barely moved. Fewer participants, similar proportion losing.

Why the loss rate is sticky

The measures reduced access and leverage. They did not change the zero-sum structure, the cost stack, or the skill gap between retail and professional participants. A smaller number of people losing at the same rate is a partial success at best.

The displacement question

Some activity may have migrated to other products, other segments, or to unregulated venues (dabba trading — illegal off-exchange betting on prices, which carries total counterparty risk and no legal recourse). Regulatory tightening in a legal market does not reduce the appetite that drove the activity.

What further change might look like

  • Further increases in minimum contract size, phased.
  • Product suitability assessments or categorisation of derivative products by complexity.
  • Revisions to expiry-day margining and position limits.
  • Stricter rules on the marketing of derivatives, finfluencer regulation, and the advertising of returns.
  • Possible changes to the taxation of derivative transactions (STT rates on F&O have been revised before).
  • Continued publication of participant-level outcome studies, which is itself a policy instrument — disclosure as deterrent.
Risk disclosures on broker platformsSEBI has required brokers and certain intermediaries to display prominent disclosures about the proportion of individual traders losing money in equity derivatives. If your broker shows you such a notice, it is not boilerplate — it is the regulator making you read the base rate before you trade. Verify the current disclosure requirement at sebi.gov.in.

Use case 3: a put spread instead of a stop loss

TEXT
THE PROBLEM WITH STOP LOSSES IN INDIAN EQUITIES
===============================================
You hold a position and place a stop loss 8% below.
  - An overnight gap can open 12% below your stop. The stop
    becomes a market order at the gap price. You lose 12%,
    not 8%.
  - An intraday spike can trigger your stop and then reverse,
    taking you out of a position you still wanted.
  - Circuit limits can prevent execution entirely.

A PUT (OR PUT SPREAD) HAS NONE OF THESE PROBLEMS
  - It pays out regardless of the gap. A 12% gap down means
    the put is 12% in the money. No execution risk.
  - It cannot be "triggered" by an intraday wick.
  - Your maximum loss is known in advance, exactly.
  - BUT you pay a premium, and a stop loss is free.

WORKED COMPARISON (index version, Rs 10 lakh exposure)
------------------------------------------------------
Index 25,000. Suppose you hold index exposure and want to
limit the damage from a fall below 24,000.

METHOD A: STOP LOSS AT 24,000
  Cost upfront:        Rs 0
  Loss if it drifts to 24,000:   -Rs 40,000 (-4%)
  Loss if it GAPS to 23,200:     -Rs 72,000 (-7.2%)
  Loss if it gaps to 22,000:   -Rs 1,20,000 (-12%)
  Risk of being stopped out on a wick and then rallying: HIGH

METHOD B: BUY THE 24,000 PE @ 118.00
  Cost upfront:        Rs 8,910 (0.89%)
  Loss at 24,000:      -Rs 40,000 - 8,910 = -Rs 48,910
  Loss at 23,200:      -Rs 80,000 + 60,000 - 8,910 = -Rs 28,910
  Loss at 22,000:    -Rs 1,20,000 + 1,50,000 - 8,910 = +Rs 21,090
  Risk of being stopped out on a wick: ZERO

METHOD C: BUY THE 24,000/22,800 PUT SPREAD @ 76.00
  Cost upfront:        Rs 5,800 (0.58%)
  Loss at 24,000:      -Rs 45,800
  Loss at 23,200:      -Rs 80,000 + 60,000 - 5,800 = -Rs 25,800
  Loss at 22,000:    -Rs 1,20,000 + 84,300 - 5,800 = -Rs 41,500

THE TRADE-OFF, STATED PLAINLY
  A stop loss costs nothing and fails exactly when you need it
  (a gap). A put costs 0.89% and works exactly when you need it.
  You are paying 0.89% to remove gap risk and wick risk.

  Whether that is worth it depends on how likely a gap is and
  how much a gap would hurt you. For a leveraged position or a
  position you cannot afford to lose, it usually is. For a
  small long-term holding you were going to keep anyway, it
  usually is not - the right answer there is to not use a stop
  loss at all.

If you still want to trade F&O: the honest prerequisites

You have read the evidence and you still want to proceed. That is your decision to make. What follows is the minimum standard a serious person would hold themselves to — not a path to profit, but a set of conditions below which the activity is indefensible.

Before anything elseIf you do not have an emergency fund, health insurance, term life cover (if anyone depends on you), zero high-interest debt, and a funded long-term investment plan, you should not be trading derivatives. Derivatives are the last thing you add to a financial life, not the first. See the investing foundations guide.

The ten prerequisites

  1. Risk capital you can lose entirelyNot savings. Not borrowed money. Not money with a purpose. A sum whose complete loss changes nothing material in your life. For most salaried software engineers in India that is a small number, and it should be.
  2. A fully funded long-term portfolio firstIndex funds, EPF/NPS, emergency fund — all in place and on autopilot. Derivatives come out of the discretionary surplus after that, never instead of it.
  3. Time you actually haveIntraday derivatives trading is incompatible with a full-time engineering job. Not "difficult" — incompatible. If your plan requires watching a chart during standups, the plan is already broken.
  4. A tested edge, written downA specific, falsifiable hypothesis about why you expect positive expectancy, tested over a meaningful sample. "I read charts well" is not an edge. See the trading strategy guide for how to specify and test one.
  5. Defined risk on every single positionMaximum loss computed and acceptable before entry. In practice this means long options and debit/credit spreads with bought wings. No naked short options, ever.
  6. No naked short options. Ever.This is not a preference. For a retail account with no vega hedge, no intraday risk system and no ability to trade through a gap, an uncovered short option is an unmanaged tail exposure. The high win rate is the bait.
  7. Position sizing by exposure, not by premiumCompute delta-equivalent notional. Cap total portfolio delta exposure as a percentage of net worth, not of the trading account.
  8. A written trading planEntry criteria, exit criteria, stop rule, size rule, maximum positions, maximum daily loss, what you will do on a gap. Written before the session, not during it.
  9. Six months of paper trading, minimumWith real position sizes, real cost modelling, a real journal, and real honesty about fills you would not have got. If you cannot produce a positive expectancy on paper over six months and 100+ trades, you do not have an edge — and paper results are systematically better than live results.
  10. A pre-committed stop conditionA drawdown level at which you stop entirely and go back to investing. Write it down now, while you are calm. −25% of the trading capital is a reasonable place to put it. You will want to move it. That is exactly why you write it down in advance.
Paper trading overstates your resultsPaper trading has no slippage, perfect fills at mid, no emotional pressure, no margin calls, and a retrospective view of what "you would have done". Expect your live results to be materially worse than your paper results. If your paper expectancy is marginally positive, your live expectancy is almost certainly negative.

The things that disqualify you, today

  • You are trading with borrowed money, a personal loan, or a credit card.
  • You have pledged your long-term equity portfolio to fund derivatives margin.
  • You cannot state your current position's net delta, theta and vega in rupees.
  • You have ever increased position size specifically to recover a loss.
  • You are following tips, a Telegram group, or a paid signal service.
  • You are trading primarily on expiry day.
  • You hold uncovered short options.
  • Your trading affects your sleep, your work, or your relationships.
  • You have not computed your round-trip transaction cost as a percentage of capital at risk.
  • You do not have a written plan you could show to someone else.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it really true that about 9 out of 10 F&O traders lose money?
That is what SEBI's studies of actual participant data report. The September 2024 study covering FY22–FY24 found roughly 93% of individual F&O traders made losses, with aggregate individual net losses in the region of ₹1.8 lakh crore over the period. A 2025 follow-up covering FY25 found roughly 91% of individual index-option traders losing. Treat the precise figures as approximate and verify the latest at sebi.gov.in — SEBI revises the methodology and the period. The structural conclusion is stable across every version: derivatives are a negative-sum game after costs and most individuals lose.
If the odds are that bad, why does anyone trade F&O?
Several reasons. Hedgers and institutions use it for its intended purpose — transferring risk — and accept a negative expected P&L in exchange for reduced variance. Professional firms have genuine structural edges (market making, latency, scale, cost structure) and profit consistently. Individuals are attracted by leverage, the accessibility of the product, visible survivorship-biased success stories, and the fact that a high-win-rate strategy feels like skill for a long time before it fails. The last group is the one SEBI's statistics are measuring.
Can I start F&O with ₹50,000?
After the 2024–25 minimum contract size increases, one index option lot often requires several thousand rupees of premium and one futures or short-option lot requires ₹2 lakh or more of margin. With ₹50,000 you are restricted to buying one or two lots of options, which means a single position is 15–30% of your capital — a position size that guarantees ruin over a sample of trades. The honest answer is that ₹50,000 is not enough capital to size derivative positions responsibly, and the correct use of ₹50,000 for almost everyone is a low-cost index fund. Verify current lot sizes and margin requirements at nseindia.com.
Is option buying safer than option selling because the loss is capped?
The maximum loss is capped, which is a genuine advantage. The probability of incurring that maximum loss is very high — for an out-of-the-money option it is typically 60–90%. Option buying has a low win rate with a bounded loss; option selling has a high win rate with an unbounded loss. Both have negative expectancy for a participant without an edge; they fail in different ways. Buying fails through a slow grind of 100% losses; selling fails through one catastrophic event after a long winning run. Buying is survivable; selling can end your financial life. In that narrow sense, yes, buying is "safer".
Everyone says option sellers win because most options expire worthless. Is that right?
The statistic is often misquoted. A commonly cited figure is that a large share of options expire worthless, but a substantial proportion of positions are closed before expiry rather than held to it, so "expire worthless" is not the same as "sellers profit". More importantly, the statistic is irrelevant to expectancy. A seller can win 90% of the time and still lose money, because the losses are many times the size of the wins. The worked short-strangle expectancy calculation in this guide shows a negative expected value despite an 86% win probability. Win rate without loss magnitude is a meaningless number.
What is the single most dangerous thing a beginner can do in F&O?
Sell uncovered (naked) options, particularly short straddles and strangles, and particularly on expiry day. It combines a high win rate (which builds false confidence and encourages size escalation), unbounded loss, vega exposure that hurts you on a volatility spike with no price move, margin that expands exactly when the position is losing, and gap risk that no stop loss can protect against. The second most dangerous thing is buying cheap out-of-the-money options on expiry day, which has a deeply negative expected value and a 10–20% cost ratio.
I was right about the direction but still lost money. How?
Three common causes, usually in combination. First, the move did not exceed your break-even — a long call needs the index to rise by more than the premium paid. Second, implied volatility fell (IV crush), which reduces the premium regardless of direction; this is routine after a scheduled event. Third, time passed (theta), which erodes premium every day. The worked example in the volatility section shows a correct directional call losing 35% because the vega and theta losses exceeded the delta gain. Buying an option is a bet on direction and magnitude and speed and volatility, not direction alone.
What exactly is the STT trap on exercised options?
STT on a sold option is charged on the premium, which is small. STT on an option that is exercised at expiry is charged on the settlement (intrinsic) value, which for a deep in-the-money option can be many times the premium you paid. A position with a small profit can become a loss purely from this. The worked example in the costs section shows a ₹750 gross profit turning into a ₹1,125 net loss. The rule: sell your in-the-money options in the market before expiry rather than letting them be exercised, unless you have computed that exercise is cheaper. Verify current STT rates and bases at incometax.gov.in.
What happens if I hold a stock option into expiry and it is in the money?
It is automatically exercised and settles by physical delivery. Depending on which of the four positions you hold, you will either need to produce the full cash value of the contract (lakhs of rupees, not your margin) or produce the actual shares in your demat account. If you cannot deliver shares you owe, the exchange buys them in an auction at your cost plus penalty, which can be far worse than the market price. From roughly four sessions before expiry you also face escalating delivery margin. The safe rule is to close all single-stock F&O positions before expiry week unless you specifically intend to settle physically and have the cash or shares ready.
Did SEBI's 2024–25 curbs fix the problem?
Partially. Weekly expiries were limited to one benchmark index per exchange, minimum contract sizes were raised substantially, option premium must be collected upfront from buyers, the calendar-spread margin benefit was removed on expiry day, position limits are monitored intraday, and an additional tail-risk margin applies to short options on expiry day. Short-dated option volumes and the number of unique individual participants fell. But SEBI's 2025 follow-up found roughly 91% of individual index-option traders still losing — the loss rate barely moved. The measures reduced access and leverage; they did not change the zero-sum structure, the cost stack or the skill gap. Verify the current framework at sebi.gov.in.
How are F&O profits and losses taxed in India?
Income from exchange-traded equity derivatives is generally treated as non-speculative business income under the Income-tax Act, taxed at your slab rate, with losses able to be set off against other business income and carried forward (subject to conditions). This is different from capital gains treatment on shares. Expenses including brokerage, internet, and depreciation on equipment may be deductible. Tax audit requirements may apply above certain turnover thresholds, and the definition of "turnover" for F&O is specific. Advance tax obligations apply. This is a genuinely complex area — as at FY 2026–27, verify at incometax.gov.in and engage a chartered accountant. Note also that the Income-tax Act 2025 renumbered and restructured provisions from 1 April 2026.
Should I use a stop loss on option positions?
A stop loss on a long option is of limited use because the instrument already has a defined maximum loss and because option prices gap and spike on thin liquidity, triggering stops on noise. A stop loss on a short option is close to useless in the scenario that matters — an overnight gap bypasses it entirely, and that is precisely the event that destroys short-option positions. The better risk controls for options are position sizing by delta-equivalent exposure, buying protective wings so the maximum loss is structurally capped, and simply not holding positions whose worst case you cannot absorb.
Can I hedge my ₹5 lakh portfolio with index options?
With difficulty. After the minimum contract size increases, one index option lot typically covers ₹15–20 lakh of notional. Buying one put to hedge ₹5 lakh over-hedges by 3–4x, which converts your long portfolio into a net short position in a crash — that is a new bet, not a hedge. For portfolios below roughly ₹15–20 lakh, the practical hedging tools are reducing equity exposure, holding more cash or short-duration debt, or simply accepting the volatility if your horizon is long. Verify current lot sizes at nseindia.com.
What is a realistic return expectation if I am good at this?
The honest answer is that the question is poorly framed. Professional systematic traders with genuine edges, infrastructure and risk teams target returns measured in the tens of percent with significant drawdowns, and many fail. For a retail participant, the realistic expectation before developing a demonstrated edge is a loss. If you are determined to have a number: a plausible outcome for a disciplined, well-capitalised retail trader with a genuine small edge is to roughly match a broad index after costs, with far more work, far more stress and far more tax friction. That is the good outcome, and it is a strong argument for simply buying the index.
Is paper trading actually useful, or is it a waste of time?
It is useful for exactly one thing: discovering whether your strategy has positive expectancy before you pay to find out. It is not useful for developing psychology, because there is no money at stake. It also systematically overstates results — no slippage, perfect fills, no margin calls, no emotional pressure, and hindsight contaminating your "would have done" decisions. Model costs aggressively, assume you get the worse side of every spread, keep a real journal, and treat a marginally positive paper expectancy as equivalent to a negative live one. Six months and 100+ trades is a reasonable minimum before risking money.

Decision checklist and strategy reference

Two tools to close with. First, an honest test of whether you should be in this market at all. Second, a one-page reference for the structures covered.

Should I trade derivatives at all?

Answer each honestly. Every "no" in Part A is disqualifying on its own.

Part A — financial preconditions (all must be "yes")

  • I have 6–12 months of expenses in an emergency fund, untouched.
  • I have health insurance and, if anyone depends on me, term life cover.
  • I have no outstanding high-interest debt (credit cards, personal loans).
  • I have a funded, automated, long-term investment plan already running.
  • The capital I would trade with is money whose total loss changes nothing material in my life.
  • None of that capital is borrowed, and none of my long-term portfolio is pledged as margin for it.
  • I can afford one full contract lot and still size positions at a small fraction of my trading capital.

Part B — competence preconditions (all must be "yes")

  • I can compute intrinsic value, time value and break-even for any option without looking it up.
  • I can state my position's net delta, gamma, theta and vega, converted into rupees.
  • I can compute the expected 1-standard-deviation move from implied volatility and compare it to my break-even.
  • I know my round-trip transaction cost as a percentage of the capital at risk, for the specific contracts I trade.
  • I understand physical settlement and I have a calendar process for expiry week.
  • I have a written strategy specification with entry, exit, stop and size rules.
  • I have paper-traded that specification for at least six months and 100 trades with realistic cost modelling.
  • I have a pre-written drawdown level at which I stop completely.

Part C — the disqualifiers (any "yes" means stop)

  • I would hold uncovered short options.
  • I would trade primarily on expiry day.
  • I follow tips, signal groups or paid "calls".
  • I have increased position size to recover a loss.
  • I expect derivatives to replace or supplement my income within a year.
  • My trading affects my sleep, my work or my relationships.
  • I cannot articulate, in one sentence, what edge I have and who is on the other side of my trade.
The most defensible outcome of this checklistFor the large majority of Indian software engineers reading this, the correct answer is: do not trade derivatives. Use the time you would have spent on it to increase your income and your savings rate, and put the money in a low-cost index fund. That is not a cop-out — it is what the evidence supports, and it is what most people who have been through a full cycle in derivatives eventually conclude.

A pre-trade routine, if you proceed

  1. State the view in one sentenceIncluding magnitude and timeframe. "Up" is not a view; "up more than 2% within 12 trading days" is.
  2. Compute the implied moveSpot × IV × √t. Compare your target to it. If the market already prices your move, you have no trade.
  3. Select the structureFrom the reference table above. Prefer defined risk. Never uncovered short options.
  4. Compute max loss in rupeesNot percentage. The actual number. Then ask whether you would be comfortable if that happened today.
  5. Compute delta-equivalent exposureLots × lot size × delta × spot. Check it against your net worth, not your trading account.
  6. Compute the round-trip costAs a percentage of capital at risk. If it exceeds 2–3%, reconsider the contract.
  7. Check the margin requirementOn the exchange calculator. Read it as a risk opinion, not an obstacle.
  8. Write the exit planProfit target, loss limit, time stop, and what you do on a gap — before entering.
  9. Check the expiry calendarEspecially for single stocks. Set a reminder three sessions before expiry.
  10. Log the tradePre-trade entry in your journal, with the hypothesis. See the trading strategy guide for a full template.
Closing noteThis guide has given you the mechanics, the arithmetic and the risk structure of Indian equity derivatives. It has deliberately not given you a strategy, because no honest guide can.
