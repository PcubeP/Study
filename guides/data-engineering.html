The Data Layer You Inherit

You will almost never build a data platform from scratch. You will be dropped into a customer who already has seventeen years of accumulated decisions — a mainframe extract that runs at 02:00, a Snowflake account nobody can fully explain, four hundred stored procedures, a Power BI dataset that one analyst refreshes manually, and a Kafka cluster that was installed for a project that got cancelled. Your first job is not to improve any of it. Your first job is to build an accurate map, because every estimate you give and every architecture you draw will be wrong if the map is wrong.

The first five questions

Ask these in the first meeting, write the answers down verbatim, and send them back as a written summary. Half the value of a Forward Deployed Engineer in week one is forcing a customer to state things they have never written down.

  1. Where does the data originate, and who owns the system of record? Not "where is the data" — where is it born. If an order is created in SAP S/4HANA and copied into Snowflake nightly, SAP is the system of record and Snowflake is a derivative. Anyone who wants to change the data must change it in SAP. If two systems both claim to be the system of record for customers, you have found the project's central political problem on day one.
  2. What is the required freshness, stated as a number? "Real-time" is not an answer. Push until you get "the sales dashboard must reflect orders placed up to 15 minutes ago" or "finance only needs the previous business day by 07:00 IST". The difference between 15 minutes and 24 hours is the difference between a streaming architecture and a cron job — roughly a 5-10x difference in cost and complexity.
  3. What is the volume, stated as rows per day and bytes per day? Then ask for the largest table's row count and the daily growth of the largest table. Teams routinely describe themselves as "big data" at 40 GB total, which fits in a laptop's RAM, and routinely under-describe a 2 TB/day clickstream as "some web logs".
  4. What happens today when a pipeline fails? Who gets paged, what is the runbook, how often does it happen, and how long until anyone notices? If the answer is "an analyst notices the dashboard is stale and emails Rajesh", you have learned that there is no monitoring, no SLA, and no on-call, and that your deliverable must include those things or it will rot within a quarter.
  5. Who is allowed to see this data, and what is in it? PII, payment data, health data, employee data, data subject to data-residency rules. The answer determines whether you can take an extract to your own laptop, whether you can use a hosted LLM API on it, and whether the project needs a DPA before you write a line of code.
The question behind the questionsEverything above is really asking: what decision does this data support, and what is the cost of that decision being wrong or late? A pipeline that feeds a quarterly board slide and a pipeline that feeds a fraud block have the same SQL and completely different engineering budgets. Get the decision, then size the engineering.

Doing an honest inventory in week one

Read-only access plus half a day of SQL gives you more than three weeks of meetings. In Snowflake, these queries tell you what is actually used, which is rarely what the documentation says.

SQL
-- Which tables actually get queried? (Snowflake)
select
    lower(value:objectName::string)        as table_name,
    count(*)                               as query_count,
    count(distinct user_name)              as distinct_users,
    max(start_time)                        as last_queried
from snowflake.account_usage.access_history,
     lateral flatten(input => base_objects_accessed)
where query_start_time >= dateadd(day, -90, current_timestamp())
group by 1
order by query_count desc
limit 100;

-- Which tables are large but never read? (deletion candidates)
select t.table_catalog, t.table_schema, t.table_name,
       t.row_count,
       round(t.bytes / power(1024, 3), 2) as gb
from snowflake.account_usage.tables t
where t.deleted is null
  and t.bytes > 10 * power(1024, 3)
order by t.bytes desc
limit 50;

-- Where is the money going?
select warehouse_name,
       sum(credits_used)            as credits,
       sum(credits_used) * 3.0      as approx_usd   -- check your contract rate
from snowflake.account_usage.warehouse_metering_history
where start_time >= dateadd(day, -30, current_timestamp())
group by 1
order by credits desc;

OLTP vs OLAP: Warehouses, Lakes and Lakehouses

Every argument about data platforms reduces to one physical fact: transactional systems are optimised to read and write a single row quickly, and analytical systems are optimised to read one column across a billion rows quickly. Row storage and column storage are different enough that no single engine is excellent at both, which is why your customer has at least two, and why the copying between them is your job.

The physical difference

Consider a table of 100 million orders with 40 columns. A query asks for sum(amount) group by region, touching two columns.

The number one anti-pattern you will be asked to build"Can we just point the dashboard at the production Postgres replica?" For a startup with 2 GB of data, yes, and you should say yes. At scale it means analysts' six-way joins compete for buffer cache with the checkout flow. The standard progression is: read replica → nightly dump into a warehouse → CDC into a warehouse. Know where the customer is on that line before you propose Kafka.

Warehouse, lake, lakehouse — the actual distinctions

The words are abused. Here is what they mean operationally.

Data warehouse

Storage and compute are managed together by one vendor, data is loaded into proprietary (or vendor-optimised) storage, and SQL is the only interface. Strong consistency, strong governance, excellent performance, poor fit for images/audio/arbitrary files, and you pay the vendor for storage. Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift, Synapse.

Data lake

Files in object storage (S3, ADLS, GCS) in open formats, read by whatever engine you like. Cheap ($20-25/TB/month), infinitely flexible, handles any file type. Historically no transactions, no schema enforcement, no indexes — which is how lakes became swamps full of 4 KB JSON files nobody can query.

Lakehouse

A lake plus a table format (Delta/Iceberg/Hudi) that adds ACID transactions, schema enforcement, time travel and statistics on top of the files. You get warehouse semantics on open storage you own, queryable by multiple engines. Databricks, Snowflake-on-Iceberg, Fabric, Athena/Trino + Iceberg.

The honest summary in 2025-2026 is that the categories have converged. Snowflake reads and writes Iceberg; Databricks SQL is a competent warehouse; BigQuery reads external Iceberg tables; Fabric puts Delta under everything. The question is no longer "warehouse or lake" but "who owns the storage, and can a second engine read it without a migration?".

File Formats and Physical Layout

Format choice is the single highest-leverage, lowest-effort decision in a data platform. Converting a customer's CSV landing zone to properly sized, partitioned, Snappy-compressed Parquet routinely cuts scan cost by 10-30x and query time by a similar factor — for a day of work and no change to anyone's SQL. It is the easiest win you will ever present.

Why Parquet is fast — the internals that matter

A Parquet file is a sequence of row groups (typically 128 MB), each containing one column chunk per column, each chunk split into pages (~1 MB). The footer holds the schema plus, crucially, min/max statistics and null counts per column chunk.

Predicate pushdown works because of footer statistics. Unsorted data defeats it.

Encodings matter too. Parquet applies dictionary encoding to low-cardinality string columns (store each distinct value once, then store small integer codes), run-length encoding for repeated values, and delta encoding for sorted integers and timestamps. This is why sorting matters twice: it tightens statistics and makes RLE effective.

PYTHON
import pyarrow as pa
import pyarrow.parquet as pq
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

n = 5_000_000
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "order_id":   np.arange(n, dtype="int64"),
    "order_ts":   pd.Timestamp("2025-01-01") + pd.to_timedelta(np.random.randint(0, 86400*180, n), "s"),
    "region":     np.random.choice(["APAC", "EMEA", "NA", "LATAM"], n),   # low cardinality
    "status":     np.random.choice(["NEW", "PAID", "SHIPPED", "CANCELLED"], n),
    "amount":     np.round(np.random.gamma(2.0, 120.0, n), 2),
    "customer_id":np.random.randint(1, 400_000, n),
})

df.to_csv("orders.csv", index=False)                                   # ~390 MB

pq.write_table(table, "orders_zstd.parquet",   compression="zstd")     # ~72 MB

pq.write_table(sorted_tbl, "orders_sorted.parquet", compression="zstd",
               row_group_size=1_000_000,
               use_dictionary=["region", "status"],
               write_statistics=True)

Partitioning: the right way and the two wrong ways

Hive-style partitioning encodes partition values in the directory path, so an engine can skip entire directories without opening files:

TEXT
s3://lake/orders/
  event_date=2025-11-01/region=APAC/part-0000.parquet
  event_date=2025-11-01/region=EMEA/part-0000.parquet
  event_date=2025-11-02/region=APAC/part-0000.parquet
  ...

A query with where event_date = '2025-11-02' and region = 'APAC' opens exactly one directory. This is partition pruning and it is the cheapest performance you will ever buy.

Wrong way 1: too fine

Partitioning by customer_id with 400,000 customers creates 400,000 directories each holding a 2 KB file. Every query now does hundreds of thousands of LIST and GET calls against object storage. The metadata operation dominates and the query takes minutes instead of seconds. Rule of thumb: aim for partitions of at least 128 MB, ideally 256 MB-1 GB.

Wrong way 2: too coarse or wrong column

Partitioning by year only, when every query filters on a single day, means every query scans a year. Or partitioning by a column nobody filters on. Partition by what appears in WHERE clauses — overwhelmingly that is a date — and nothing else. Use clustering/sorting for the second dimension.

The small-file problem

This is the most common pathology in real customer lakes. A streaming job writes a file every 30 seconds, giving 2,880 files per day per partition. After a year you have a million files averaging 400 KB. Symptoms:

  • Query planning takes longer than query execution — the engine spends minutes listing objects.
  • S3 request charges appear as a significant line item (LIST/GET at ~$0.0004 per 1,000 requests adds up at tens of millions of requests).
  • Spark jobs show thousands of tiny tasks, each with ~100 ms of overhead, so overhead exceeds work.
  • Compression ratio is poor because each file has its own dictionary and no run-lengths.

The fix is compaction: periodically rewrite many small files into few large ones. Every table format gives you a command for this; in raw Parquet you write it yourself.

Table Formats: Delta, Iceberg and Hudi

Object storage gives you durable, cheap, infinitely scalable files — and nothing else. No transactions, no schema enforcement, no way to know which files belong to "the table" right now. Table formats are a metadata layer on top of your Parquet files that restores database semantics. Understanding how they do it will make you the person in the room who can explain why a reader saw half an update.

The problem being solved

Imagine a naive lake table at s3://lake/orders/ with 500 Parquet files. You want to delete customer 1234 for a GDPR erasure request. That means rewriting the 12 files that contain their rows. Between deleting the old files and writing the new ones, a reader doing SELECT count(*) sees an inconsistent table. If your job crashes halfway, the table is corrupt and there is no rollback. There is also no way to answer "what did this table look like last Tuesday".

Table formats fix this with one idea: the table is not "whatever files are in this prefix" — the table is an explicit, versioned list of files. Writers produce new files and then atomically publish a new version of the list. Readers pin a version at query start and see a consistent snapshot.

The whole mechanism: immutable data files plus an atomically-published, versioned file list.

ACID on object storage, concretely

Here is a Delta table being created, updated, time-travelled and rolled back. Note that every operation is an atomic version bump.

PYTHON
from pyspark.sql import SparkSession
from delta.tables import DeltaTable

spark = (SparkSession.builder
    .config("spark.sql.extensions", "io.delta.sql.DeltaSparkSessionExtension")
    .config("spark.sql.catalog.spark_catalog",
            "org.apache.spark.sql.delta.catalog.DeltaCatalog")
    .getOrCreate())

# v0: create
(spark.read.parquet("s3://lake/raw/orders/")
     .write.format("delta")
     .partitionBy("order_date")
     .mode("overwrite")
     .save("s3://lake/silver/orders"))

# v1: merge today's CDC batch (upsert + delete)
tgt = DeltaTable.forPath(spark, "s3://lake/silver/orders")
src = spark.read.json("s3://lake/cdc/orders/dt=2025-11-18/")

(tgt.alias("t")
    .merge(src.alias("s"), "t.order_id = s.order_id")
    .whenMatchedDelete(condition="s.op = 'D'")
    .whenMatchedUpdateAll(condition="s.op = 'U' AND s.updated_at > t.updated_at")
    .whenNotMatchedInsertAll(condition="s.op != 'D'")
    .execute())

# Inspect history - this is the audit trail auditors actually accept
tgt.history().select("version", "timestamp", "operation",
                     "operationMetrics").show(truncate=False)

# Time travel
yesterday = spark.read.format("delta").option("timestampAsOf", "2025-11-17").load("s3://lake/silver/orders")
v0       = spark.read.format("delta").option("versionAsOf", 0).load("s3://lake/silver/orders")

# Did the merge do something insane? Roll back in one statement.
spark.sql("RESTORE TABLE delta.`s3://lake/silver/orders` TO VERSION AS OF 0")

-- The same ideas in Iceberg SQL
CREATE TABLE lake.silver.orders (
  order_id      bigint,
  customer_id   bigint,
  order_ts      timestamp,
  amount        decimal(12,2),
  status        string
) USING iceberg
PARTITIONED BY (days(order_ts))          -- hidden partitioning: no order_date column needed
TBLPROPERTIES (
  'write.target-file-size-bytes' = '268435456',
  'write.parquet.compression-codec' = 'zstd',
  'format-version' = '2'
);

MERGE INTO lake.silver.orders t
USING staged_cdc s ON t.order_id = s.order_id
WHEN MATCHED AND s.op = 'D' THEN DELETE
WHEN MATCHED AND s.op = 'U' THEN UPDATE SET *
WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT *;

-- Time travel and rollback
SELECT * FROM lake.silver.orders VERSION AS OF 3821;
SELECT * FROM lake.silver.orders FOR SYSTEM_TIME AS OF '2025-11-17 23:59:59';
CALL catalog.system.rollback_to_snapshot('lake.silver.orders', 3821);

-- Change partitioning WITHOUT rewriting history - Iceberg's party trick
ALTER TABLE lake.silver.orders REPLACE PARTITION FIELD days(order_ts) WITH hours(order_ts);

Copy-on-write vs merge-on-read

When you update one row inside a 256 MB file, you have two choices, and the choice is a write/read latency trade-off you will have to explain.

Copy-on-write (CoW)

Rewrite the entire file with the change applied. Writes are expensive (amplification: 1 row changed → 256 MB rewritten) but reads are fast because files are clean Parquet. Default for Delta and Iceberg. Right for batch pipelines that update once an hour or once a day.

Merge-on-read (MoR)

Write a small delete file or delta log alongside, and reconcile at query time. Writes are cheap, reads pay a merge cost, and you must compact regularly or read performance decays. Right for high-frequency streaming upserts. Hudi's speciality; available in Iceberg v2 and as Delta deletion vectors.

The failure mode to watch forA customer enables MoR for write throughput, never schedules compaction, and six weeks later every read merges thousands of delete files. Query latency has quintupled and nobody knows why. If you enable MoR, you own the compaction schedule — put it in the DAG on the same day.

Retention, vacuum and the GDPR trap

Time travel works because old files are not deleted immediately. That is also a compliance hazard: if you "delete" a customer's row but the previous version is retained for 30 days, you have not actually erased it.

SQL
-- Delta: files older than the retention window become eligible for deletion
VACUUM delta.`s3://lake/silver/orders` RETAIN 168 HOURS;   -- 7 days

-- Iceberg: expire snapshots first, then clean orphans
CALL catalog.system.expire_snapshots(
  table => 'lake.silver.orders',
  older_than => TIMESTAMP '2025-11-11 00:00:00',
  retain_last => 5
);
CALL catalog.system.remove_orphan_files(table => 'lake.silver.orders');
  • Pick a retention window deliberately: 7 days is a good default for operational rollback; 30+ days costs real storage and extends erasure latency.
  • Write the window into the data-retention policy document and tell the privacy team the actual erasure SLA is "delete + vacuum", not "delete".
  • Never run VACUUM with a retention shorter than your longest-running query, or you will delete files out from under a reader.
  • Schedule OPTIMIZE + VACUUM (or rewrite_data_files + expire_snapshots) as explicit DAG tasks with monitoring.

Ingestion Patterns

Ingestion is where projects actually die. The transformation layer is SQL and it is pleasant; ingestion is credentials, firewalls, undocumented APIs, rate limits, partners who change a CSV header without telling anyone, and a mainframe that emits EBCDIC. Plan for ingestion to be 60-80% of the work and you will be approximately right.

Source→ Extract→ Land raw (immutable)→ Validate→ Normalise→ Publish

The cardinal rule: land raw, untouched, forever

Whatever arrives from a source should be written to immutable storage exactly as received, with a timestamp and a batch identifier, before any parsing, casting or cleaning. This single discipline saves more projects than any other.

You can replay

When you discover in March that your February timezone handling was wrong, you reprocess from raw. Without raw, the data is gone and you are writing an apology email.

You can prove provenance

"Our dashboard says 4,312 and yours says 4,318." You open the raw file for that day and settle it in ten minutes instead of three days.

You decouple schedules

Extraction and transformation become independent. A transformation bug no longer forces you to re-hit a rate-limited partner API.

Pattern 1: full-table batch extract

Pull the whole table every run. Crude, but correct, idempotent and trivial to reason about. Entirely appropriate below roughly 10-50 million rows.

PYTHON
import os, uuid, datetime as dt
import sqlalchemy as sa
import pyarrow as pa, pyarrow.parquet as pq

def full_extract(table: str, out_root: str, chunk_rows: int = 500_000) -> dict:
    engine = sa.create_engine(os.environ["SOURCE_DSN"], pool_pre_ping=True)
    run_id = str(uuid.uuid4())
    day    = dt.date.today().isoformat()
    base   = f"{out_root}/{table}/ingest_date={day}/run_id={run_id}"
    rows, files = 0, 0

    with engine.connect().execution_options(stream_results=True, yield_per=chunk_rows) as conn:
        result = conn.execute(sa.text(f"select * from {table}"))
        batch  = []
        for row in result:
            batch.append(dict(row._mapping))
            if len(batch) >= chunk_rows:
                pq.write_table(pa.Table.from_pylist(batch),
                               f"{base}/part-{files:05d}.parquet", compression="zstd")
                rows += len(batch); files += 1; batch = []
        if batch:
            pq.write_table(pa.Table.from_pylist(batch),
                           f"{base}/part-{files:05d}.parquet", compression="zstd")
            rows += len(batch); files += 1

    return {"run_id": run_id, "table": table, "rows": rows, "files": files, "path": base}
Stream, do not fetchallstream_results=True plus yield_per is the difference between a job that handles 50M rows on a 4 GB worker and a job that OOMs. The same applies to pandas.read_sql — always pass chunksize.

Pattern 2: incremental extract by watermark

Pull only rows changed since the last run, using an updated_at column or a monotonically increasing id.

PYTHON
def incremental_extract(table: str, cursor_col: str, state_store) -> dict:
    last = state_store.get(f"{table}.cursor") or "1970-01-01T00:00:00"
    # Overlap window: re-pull a few minutes to tolerate clock skew and long transactions.
    # Downstream MERGE makes the duplicates harmless.
    low = (dt.datetime.fromisoformat(last) - dt.timedelta(minutes=15)).isoformat()

    sql = sa.text(f"""
        select * from {table}
        where {cursor_col} > :low
        order by {cursor_col}
    """)
    rows = list(engine.connect().execution_options(stream_results=True).execute(sql, {"low": low}))
    if rows:
        new_cursor = max(r._mapping[cursor_col] for r in rows).isoformat()
        write_raw(table, rows)
        state_store.set(f"{table}.cursor", new_cursor)   # only AFTER a successful write
    return {"rows": len(rows), "from": low}
Three ways watermarks silently lose data1. Hard deletes. A deleted row has no updated_at change, so it stays in your warehouse forever. 2. Long transactions. A row written at 10:00 but committed at 10:20 with its original timestamp is skipped if your cursor already passed 10:00 — this is why you need the overlap window, and why reading from a replica makes it worse. 3. Backdated updates. A correction job sets updated_at to the original order date; your cursor never sees it. Mitigate with a weekly full reconciliation that compares row counts and checksums per partition.

Pattern 4: API pulls

Every SaaS API is different and all of them will hurt you in one of four ways: pagination, rate limits, auth expiry, and silent schema drift. Write one hardened client and reuse it.

PYTHON
import time, logging, random
import httpx
from tenacity import retry, stop_after_attempt, wait_exponential_jitter, retry_if_exception_type

log = logging.getLogger(__name__)

class RetryableHTTP(Exception): pass

@retry(stop=stop_after_attempt(6),
       wait=wait_exponential_jitter(initial=1, max=60),
       retry=retry_if_exception_type(RetryableHTTP),
       reraise=True)
def _get(client: httpx.Client, url: str, **kw) -> httpx.Response:
    r = client.get(url, **kw)
    if r.status_code == 429:
        # Honour the server. Retry-After may be seconds or an HTTP date.
        wait = float(r.headers.get("Retry-After", 5))
        log.warning("rate limited, sleeping %.1fs", wait)
        time.sleep(wait + random.uniform(0, 1))
        raise RetryableHTTP("429")
    if 500 <= r.status_code < 600:
        raise RetryableHTTP(f"{r.status_code}")
    r.raise_for_status()
    return r

def paginate_cursor(base_url: str, token: str, page_size: int = 500):
    """Cursor pagination - the only kind that is safe while data is changing."""
    with httpx.Client(timeout=httpx.Timeout(30.0, connect=10.0),
                      headers={"Authorization": f"Bearer {token}"}) as client:
        cursor, pages = None, 0
        while True:
            params = {"limit": page_size}
            if cursor:
                params["cursor"] = cursor
            data = _get(client, base_url, params=params).json()
            yield from data["results"]
            cursor = data.get("next_cursor")
            pages += 1
            if not cursor:
                break
            if pages > 10_000:
                raise RuntimeError("pagination did not terminate - check next_cursor semantics")
Offset pagination is a data-loss bug?offset=1000&limit=100 over a table that is being inserted into will skip and duplicate rows as the underlying result set shifts. If the API only offers offset pagination, either freeze the result with a server-side filter (updated_at < run_start_time) or accept that you must reconcile. Always prefer cursor/keyset pagination.

            f"header drift in {path.name}\n  expected: {EXPECTED_HEADER}\n  got:      {header}"
        )

    rows = list(reader)
    write_raw_bytes(f"raw/partner_x/orders/ingest_date={today()}/{digest}.csv.gz", raw)
    seen_hashes.add(digest)
    return {"status": "ok", "rows": len(rows), "sha256": digest}

ELT vs ETL and Why ELT Won

For twenty years, transformation happened in a dedicated server between source and warehouse, because warehouse compute was scarce and expensive. Cloud warehouses inverted that: compute is elastic and storage is cheap, so it became better to load raw data first and transform inside the warehouse with SQL. That one inversion is the reason the modern data stack looks the way it does.

ETL (Extract → Transform → Load)

Transform in Informatica/SSIS/Talend/custom Spark before loading. The warehouse only ever sees clean, modelled data. Transformation logic lives in a proprietary tool, often in a GUI, often not in version control. Changing a rule means reprocessing from source, which you may no longer have.

ELT (Extract → Load → Transform)

Load raw into the warehouse, then transform with SQL in place. Logic is SQL files in git, reviewed in pull requests, tested in CI. Raw is retained so you can rebuild any downstream model at will. Costs more storage and more warehouse compute — both of which are cheap relative to engineer time.

Data Modelling

Modelling is the part customers under-value and later blame you for. Badly modelled data produces two dashboards with different revenue numbers, and that single fact destroys trust in the entire platform. The modelling choices are well understood and a century old in accounting terms; you mostly need to know when to apply which one, and how to handle the one genuinely hard problem — history.

Normalisation (3NF) — why OLTP looks the way it does

Third normal form means every non-key attribute depends on the key, the whole key, and nothing but the key. It minimises redundancy, which minimises update anomalies. That is exactly right for a system taking orders and exactly wrong for a system answering questions, because answering a question requires joining eight tables.

Kimball dimensional modelling — the default for analytics

Split the world into facts (things that happened: measurements, events, transactions — long and narrow, append-mostly) and dimensions (the context: who, what, where, when — wide, relatively small, slowly changing). Arrange them in a star schema: one fact table in the middle, dimension tables around it, each one join away.

One join from fact to any dimension. Predictable performance, predictable SQL, BI tools understand it natively.

Grain — declare it before you write a line of SQL

The grain is the answer to "what does one row of this table mean?" It must be a single, unambiguous sentence. "One row per order line per day" is a grain. "Orders data" is not.

The classic fan-out bugYou join a fact at order-line grain to a shipments table at shipment grain. An order with 3 lines shipped in 2 parcels now produces 6 rows, and sum(order_amount) is double the truth. Finance notices in the board meeting. The defence: write the grain as a comment at the top of every model, add a uniqueness test on the grain columns, and never join two facts directly — join both to conformed dimensions, or aggregate one to the other's grain first.

MERGE INTO dim_customer AS t

    SELECT s.customer_id, s.name, s.segment, s.country, s.updated_at

The medallion architecture

Bronze/silver/gold is just a naming convention for layering, but it is a very useful one because it gives you a shared vocabulary with the customer about what is and is not trustworthy.

BRONZE: raw, as-landed→ SILVER: cleaned, typed, deduped, conformed→ GOLD: business entities, metrics, marts

Bronze

Exact copy of source, append-only, schema-on-read, PII intact, retained for years. Nobody but pipelines reads it. Columns: the source payload plus _ingested_at, _source_file, _batch_id.

Silver

Typed, deduplicated on a business key, timezone-normalised to UTC, light conformance (country codes, currency codes), SCD2 applied to dimensions. Tested. This is where data quality lives. Engineers and advanced analysts read it.

Gold

Business-facing: facts, dimensions, metric tables, OBTs. Named in business language (net_revenue, not amt_net_fin). Documented, SLA'd, owned. This is the only layer the business should ever point a dashboard at.

The layer that gets skippedUnder schedule pressure, teams build bronze → gold and skip silver. It works for two months. Then every gold model contains its own copy of the deduplication logic, three of them disagree, and fixing a source quirk means editing eleven files. Silver is where you pay once for correctness. Defend it.

SQL for Data Engineers

Application SQL and analytical SQL are different dialects of the same language. Application SQL is about correctness under concurrency; analytical SQL is about expressing a windowed, ranked, period-over-period question in a way that reads clearly and scans as little as possible. The handful of constructs below cover the overwhelming majority of real pipeline code.

Window functions: the single highest-value skill

Window functions compute across a set of rows related to the current row, without collapsing them. If you are writing a self-join to find "the previous order", you want a window function.

SQL
select
    order_id,
    customer_id,
    order_ts,
    amount,

    -- Ranking within a partition
    row_number() over (partition by customer_id order by order_ts)            as order_seq,
    rank()       over (partition by customer_id order by amount desc)         as amount_rank,
    dense_rank() over (partition by customer_id order by amount desc)         as amount_dense_rank,
    ntile(10)    over (order by amount)                                       as amount_decile,

    -- Looking at neighbours
    lag(order_ts)  over (partition by customer_id order by order_ts)          as prev_order_ts,
    lead(order_ts) over (partition by customer_id order by order_ts)          as next_order_ts,
    datediff('day',
        lag(order_ts) over (partition by customer_id order by order_ts),
        order_ts)                                                             as days_since_prev,

    -- Running aggregates (frame: start of partition to current row)
    sum(amount) over (partition by customer_id order by order_ts
                      rows between unbounded preceding and current row)       as lifetime_value,

    -- Moving window (frame: the 6 preceding rows plus this one)
    avg(amount) over (partition by customer_id order by order_ts
                      rows between 6 preceding and current row)               as avg_last_7_orders,

    -- Partition-wide aggregate repeated on every row
    count(*)    over (partition by customer_id)                               as total_orders,
    amount / nullif(sum(amount) over (partition by customer_id), 0)           as pct_of_customer_total,

    -- First/last value within the partition
    first_value(amount) over (partition by customer_id order by order_ts
                              rows between unbounded preceding and unbounded following) as first_order_amount

from fct_orders;
The frame clause default will bite youIf you write order by inside over() without a frame clause, most engines default to RANGE BETWEEN UNBOUNDED PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW. RANGE treats ties as a single group, so two orders with the same timestamp both get the full running total including each other. ROWS is positional and almost always what you meant. Be explicit: write the frame clause every time.

Deduplication: the QUALIFY pattern

You have a CDC feed with multiple versions of each row and you want the latest. This is the most-written query in data engineering.

SQL
-- Snowflake, Databricks, BigQuery, DuckDB: QUALIFY filters on window results directly
select *
from raw_orders
qualify row_number() over (
    partition by order_id
    order by updated_at desc, _ingested_at desc, _file_seq desc   -- deterministic tiebreak
) = 1;

-- Portable version for engines without QUALIFY (Postgres, Redshift, MySQL 8)
select * from (
    select *,
           row_number() over (partition by order_id
                              order by updated_at desc, _ingested_at desc) as rn
    from raw_orders
) t
where rn = 1;
Always add a deterministic tiebreakorder by updated_at desc alone is non-deterministic when two rows share a timestamp — and CDC produces ties constantly. Your pipeline will then produce different results on reruns, which makes every reconciliation argument unwinnable. Add a monotonic secondary key: LSN, Kafka offset, file sequence, or ingest timestamp.

where event_date = '2025-11-18';

-- 2) INSERT-OVERWRITE BY PARTITION - idempotent reprocessing of a window.
--    Safest batch pattern: rerunning the same day produces the same result.
delete from fct_orders where order_date between '2025-11-15' and '2025-11-18';
insert into fct_orders
select * from stg_orders where order_date between '2025-11-15' and '2025-11-18';
-- Snowflake/Databricks/Spark can do this atomically:
--   insert overwrite table fct_orders partition (order_date) select ...

-- 3) MERGE / UPSERT - mutable rows keyed by a business key.
merge into fct_orders t
using (
    select * from stg_orders
    qualify row_number() over (partition by order_id order by updated_at desc) = 1
) s
on t.order_id = s.order_id
when matched and s.updated_at > t.updated_at then update set
    t.status      = s.status,
    t.amount      = s.amount,
    t.updated_at  = s.updated_at,
    t._merged_at  = current_timestamp()
when not matched then insert
    (order_id, customer_id, order_ts, amount, status, updated_at, _merged_at)
values
    (s.order_id, s.customer_id, s.order_ts, s.amount, s.status, s.updated_at, current_timestamp());

-- 4) SCD2 APPEND - never update; always add a new version row.
--    Readers filter with is_current or a point-in-time BETWEEN.
MERGE requires a deduplicated sourceIf stg_orders contains two rows for order_id = 99, Snowflake raises "Duplicate row detected during DML action" and Databricks raises a similar error. This failure appears in production the first time a source re-sends a batch. Always dedupe in the USING clause, as shown above. The alternative — setting ERROR_ON_NONDETERMINISTIC_MERGE = FALSE — silently picks an arbitrary row and is almost always wrong.

Worked example: sessionisation

A classic request: group a user's events into sessions, where a new session starts after 30 minutes of inactivity. This combines lag, a boolean flag, and a running sum — a pattern worth memorising because it generalises to any "group consecutive runs" problem.

SQL
with events as (
    select user_id, event_ts, event_name
    from fct_page_views
    where event_ts >= dateadd(day, -7, current_date())
),

flagged as (
    select *,
           lag(event_ts) over (partition by user_id order by event_ts) as prev_ts,
           case
             when lag(event_ts) over (partition by user_id order by event_ts) is null then 1
             when datediff('minute',
                           lag(event_ts) over (partition by user_id order by event_ts),
                           event_ts) > 30 then 1
             else 0
           end as is_session_start
    from events
),

numbered as (
    select *,
           sum(is_session_start) over (partition by user_id order by event_ts
                                       rows between unbounded preceding and current row) as session_num
    from flagged
)

select
    user_id,
    user_id || '-' || session_num                 as session_id,
    min(event_ts)                                 as session_start,
    max(event_ts)                                 as session_end,
    datediff('second', min(event_ts), max(event_ts)) as duration_s,
    count(*)                                      as page_views,
    count(distinct event_name)                    as distinct_pages
from numbered
group by user_id, session_num
having count(*) > 1          -- drop single-hit bounces if the business wants that
order by user_id, session_start;

Worked example: period-over-period with gap filling

"Show revenue by month with month-over-month growth" is easy until a month has no sales and the growth calculation silently skips it. Always join to a generated date spine.

SQL
with spine as (
    -- Snowflake; BigQuery: generate_date_array, Postgres: generate_series
    select dateadd(month, seq4(), date_trunc('month', dateadd(year, -2, current_date()))) as month_start
    from table(generator(rowcount => 24))
),

monthly as (
    select date_trunc('month', order_ts) as month_start,
           sum(net_amount)               as revenue,
           count(distinct customer_id)   as customers
    from fct_orders
    group by 1
),

filled as (
    select s.month_start,
           coalesce(m.revenue, 0)   as revenue,
           coalesce(m.customers, 0) as customers
    from spine s
    left join monthly m using (month_start)
)

select
    month_start,
    revenue,
    lag(revenue) over (order by month_start)                                     as prev_month_revenue,
    revenue - lag(revenue) over (order by month_start)                           as mom_delta,
    round(100.0 * (revenue - lag(revenue) over (order by month_start))
          / nullif(lag(revenue) over (order by month_start), 0), 1)              as mom_pct,
    round(avg(revenue) over (order by month_start rows between 2 preceding and current row), 2) as rolling_3m_avg,
    sum(revenue) over (partition by year(month_start) order by month_start)      as ytd_revenue
from filled
order by month_start;

SQL habits that separate professionals from amateurs

  • Never select * in a model that writes a table. Column order changes break downstream inserts; new source columns silently widen your table.
  • Use nullif(x, 0) in every denominator. Division by zero either errors or produces infinity, and both ruin a dashboard.
  • Prefer is distinct from to <> when NULLs are possible. NULL <> 'A' is NULL, not true — this is the single most common change-detection bug.
  • Cast explicitly. Implicit casts differ across engines and will change a result when you migrate dialects.
  • Use decimal for money, never float. 0.1 + 0.2 != 0.3; finance will find it.
  • Store timestamps in UTC with an explicit type. Keep the original local time and offset in separate columns if the business reasons in local time.
  • Alias every table and qualify every column in any query with more than one table. Unqualified columns become ambiguous the moment a source adds a column.
  • Put the grain in a comment at the top of every model file.

dbt: The Transformation Layer

dbt turned SQL transformation into software engineering: version control, code review, automated tests, dependency resolution, documentation and lineage, all generated from SQL files with a templating layer. It is the de-facto standard for the T in ELT, and at a customer site it is frequently the single artefact that most improves how their team works — because it gives analysts the engineering practices they never had.

Demystified: dbt reads .sql files containing select statements, resolves ref() calls into a dependency graph, wraps each select in the right DDL for your materialisation, and executes them against your warehouse in topological order. -- models/marts/core/fct_orders.sql
-- Grain: one row per order. Incremental by ordered_at_utc with a 3-day lookback.
{{ config(
    materialized   = 'incremental',
    unique_key     = 'order_id',
    incremental_strategy = 'merge',
    on_schema_change = 'append_new_columns',
    cluster_by     = ['ordered_at_utc'],
    partition_by   = {'field': 'ordered_at_utc', 'data_type': 'timestamp', 'granularity': 'day'}
) }}

    select * from {{ ref('stg_shopify__orders') }}

    {% if is_incremental() %}
      -- Lookback window absorbs late-arriving and backdated rows.
      -- Without it, anything that lands after the watermark is lost forever.
      where ordered_at_utc >= (
          select coalesce(dateadd(day, -3, max(ordered_at_utc)), '1900-01-01')
          from {{ this }}
      )
    {% endif %}
),

refunds as (
    select order_id, sum(refund_amount) as refund_amount
    from {{ ref('stg_shopify__refunds') }}
    group by 1
),

customers as (
    select customer_id, customer_sk, segment
    from {{ ref('dim_customers') }}
    where is_current
),

final as (
    select
        o.order_id,
        o.customer_id,
        c.customer_sk,
        c.segment                                       as customer_segment,
        o.ordered_at_utc,
        cast(o.ordered_at_utc as date)                  as order_date,
        o.gross_amount,
        coalesce(r.refund_amount, 0)                    as refund_amount,
        o.gross_amount - coalesce(r.refund_amount, 0)   as net_amount,
        o.currency_code,
        o.payment_status,
        current_timestamp()                             as _dbt_updated_at
    from orders o
    left join refunds   r on r.order_id    = o.order_id
    left join customers c on c.customer_id = o.customer_id
)

select * from final
Three ways incremental models lose data silently1. No lookback. where ts > max(ts) drops every row that arrives late. Always use a lookback window sized to your observed lateness (measure it; do not guess). 2. Joins that change. If a dimension attribute changes, already-materialised rows keep the old value forever. Either accept that (it is often correct — the value as of order time) or schedule periodic full refreshes. 3. on_schema_change defaults. If the default is ignore, a new source column silently never appears. Set it explicitly to append_new_columns or sync_all_columns.

Snapshots capture history only while they runIf the snapshot job is paused for a week, the changes in that week are gone — there is no way to recover them from a mutable source. Snapshots must be the first thing in your DAG, must run at least as often as the source changes meaningfully, and must be monitored separately from the rest of the pipeline. This is a genuinely unrecoverable failure mode; treat it as such.

Orchestration

An orchestrator answers four questions: what runs, in what order, what happens when something fails, and how do you rerun a window of history. Cron answers the first and none of the others, which is why every customer who started with cron has a folder of shell scripts with sleep 600 in them to "make sure the previous job finished".

Airflow: the concepts that actually matter

DAG and logical date

A DAG run is identified by its logical date (formerly execution_date) — the start of the interval the run covers, not when it ran. A daily DAG for 2025-11-18 starts after 2025-11-18 ends. Misunderstanding this causes the single most common Airflow bug: off-by-one-day data.

Operators and sensors

An operator is a unit of work (PythonOperator, BashOperator, SQLExecuteQueryOperator). A sensor waits for a condition (a file, a partition, another DAG). Always run sensors in reschedule mode or as deferrable operators, or they occupy a worker slot for hours doing nothing.

XCom and the anti-pattern

XCom passes small values between tasks via the metadata database. It is for identifiers and counts. Pushing a DataFrame through XCom will bloat and eventually break the metadata DB. Pass a path or a key; store the data in object storage.

Idempotency: the property everything depends on

A task is idempotent if running it twice with the same parameters produces the same result as running it once. Without this, retries corrupt data and backfills double-count, and you cannot safely operate the pipeline at all.

Not idempotentIdempotent
insert into t select … where date = current_date()delete from t where date = '{{ ds }}'; insert … where date = '{{ ds }}'
update counters set n = n + 1merge … when matched then update set n = source.n
Append to a fileWrite to path/run_id={{ run_id }}/ and publish by swapping a pointer
Read "the last 24 hours" from wall clockRead the window derived from {{ data_interval_start }} and {{ data_interval_end }}
Send an email at the end of the taskSend from a separate task guarded by a dedupe key
Never use current_date() inside a scheduled taskIt makes the task's behaviour depend on when it runs rather than what interval it covers. A retry at 00:05 the next day reads the wrong window; a backfill reads today's data for every historical date. Every date in a task body must derive from the orchestrator's interval parameters. This one rule prevents an entire category of incident.

Dagster inverts the model. Instead of declaring tasks and edges, you declare assets — the tables and files that should exist — and their dependencies. The scheduler works out what to run. This maps much more naturally onto data work, where you care about the state of a table rather than the execution of a script.

Streaming

Streaming is batch processing where the batch size is one and the job never ends. Everything that is hard about it follows from one fact: in an unbounded stream you can never be certain that you have seen all the data for a given time window, so every aggregate is a guess that you refine. If a customer asks for streaming, your first job is to find out whether they need seconds or whether "real-time" means "not a day old".

When streaming is actually justified

Use caseNeeded latencyStreaming justified?
Fraud blocking at authorisation< 200 msYes — and it is really an online serving problem, not analytics
Operational dashboard for a logistics control roomSeconds to a minuteYes
Inventory sync between systemsMinutesUsually CDC + micro-batch, not true streaming
Personalisation / next-best-actionSecondsYes for features; the model itself is often batch-trained
Executive revenue dashboardHours to a dayNo. Batch. Say so clearly.
Regulatory reportingDaily / monthlyNo, and streaming makes reconciliation harder
The cost of "real-time" is mostly operationalA streaming pipeline runs 24/7, so it has an on-call rotation, state to manage, consumer lag to monitor, schema compatibility to maintain, and no natural "just rerun it tomorrow" recovery. A customer with a three-person data team should not own one unless the business value is unambiguous. Micro-batch every 5-15 minutes gives you 90% of the perceived freshness for 20% of the operational burden, and you can build it with the batch tools they already run.

Kafka in the terms that matter for pipelines

Topic and partition

A topic is an append-only log, split into partitions. Ordering is guaranteed only within a partition. If you need per-customer ordering, key by customer_id so all of a customer's events land in the same partition. If you key by something random, you have no useful ordering at all.

Offset and consumer group

Each consumer group tracks its own offset per partition. Committing an offset says "I have processed up to here". Commit after writing, not before, or a crash loses data. Partition count caps consumer parallelism — 6 partitions means at most 6 useful consumers in a group.

Retention and compaction

Time/size retention deletes old records (default 7 days). Log compaction instead keeps the latest value per key forever, which turns a topic into a replayable snapshot of current state — ideal for CDC and reference data.

                "_partition": m.partition(),
                "_offset":    m.offset(),
                "_kafka_ts":  m.timestamp()[1],

            write_parquet_to_s3(buffer)       # must be durable before the next line
            consumer.commit(asynchronous=False)
            buffer, last_flush = [], time.time()

Write-then-commit gives you at-least-onceIf the process dies between the write and the commit, you reprocess some messages and write duplicates. That is fine provided downstream is idempotent — dedupe on a business key plus Kafka offset, or MERGE on the key. Committing first would give you at-most-once, i.e. silent data loss, which is almost never acceptable. Choose duplicates over loss, then design the dedupe.

Event time vs processing time

Every streaming bug ultimately comes from confusing these two. Event time is when the thing happened (a phone in a tunnel at 10:00). Processing time is when your system saw it (10:47, when the phone reconnected). Windowed aggregates must use event time, or your "orders per minute at 10:00" chart is really "orders received per minute", which is a chart of your network.

Watermarks and late data

A watermark is the engine's assertion: "I believe I have seen all events with event time ≤ T." It is a heuristic, usually "max event time seen minus a slack period". When the watermark passes a window's end, the window is finalised and its state is dropped. Anything that arrives after that is late.

The watermark trade-off: completeness versus latency versus state size. There is no setting that gives all three.
PYTHON
from pyspark.sql import functions as F
from pyspark.sql.types import StructType, StructField, StringType, TimestampType, DoubleType

    StructField("order_id",    StringType()),
    StructField("customer_id", StringType()),
    StructField("event_time",  TimestampType()),
    StructField("amount",      DoubleType()),
])

    .format("kafka")
    .option("kafka.bootstrap.servers", "kafka-1:9092")
    .option("subscribe", "orders")
    .option("startingOffsets", "latest")
    .option("maxOffsetsPerTrigger", 500_000)       # cap batch size; prevents a huge first batch
    .load()

    .select("d.*"))

    .withWatermark("event_time", "10 minutes")      # tolerate 10 min of lateness
    .groupBy(
        F.window("event_time", "5 minutes"),
        F.col("customer_id"))

        F.sum("amount").alias("amount_5m"),
        F.count("*").alias("orders_5m"),
        F.approx_count_distinct("order_id").alias("distinct_orders")))

    .format("delta")
    .outputMode("append")                           # append is only valid WITH a watermark
    .option("checkpointLocation", "s3://lake/_checkpoints/orders_5m")

The checkpoint directory is the job's identitySpark Structured Streaming stores offsets and aggregation state there. Delete it and the job restarts from startingOffsets, re-reading or skipping data. Point two different queries at the same checkpoint and both corrupt. Change the query's logical plan materially (add a grouping key, change the aggregation) and Spark will refuse to resume — you need a new checkpoint plus a backfill plan. Treat checkpoint paths as versioned, named artefacts, never as scratch.

Data Quality and Contracts

Every data platform eventually fails the same way: the numbers are wrong and nobody noticed for three weeks. Quality is not a dashboard you build at the end; it is assertions embedded in the pipeline that stop bad data from propagating. The test to apply to any check you write is: would this have caught the last incident, and does it fail loudly enough that someone acts?

The six dimensions, and the check for each

DimensionQuestionConcrete check
FreshnessIs it recent enough?max(updated_at) > now() - interval '6 hours'
VolumeDid roughly the right amount arrive?Row count within ±40% of the same weekday's 4-week median
SchemaAre the columns and types as expected?Column set and types asserted; new columns flagged, removed columns fail

Quarantine instead of fail-the-whole-batch

def load_with_quarantine(df, contract, run_id: str) -> dict:
    """Split a batch into valid and invalid rather than failing everything.
    One malformed row out of 2 million should not block the pipeline, but it
    must not silently vanish either."""
    valid_mask = (
        df["order_id"].notna()
        & df["amount"].between(0, 1_000_000)
        & df["currency"].isin(contract["currencies"])
        & df["ordered_at"].notna()
    )
    good, bad = df[valid_mask], df[~valid_mask].copy()

    if len(bad):
        bad["_quarantine_reason"] = bad.apply(explain_failure, axis=1)
        bad["_run_id"] = run_id
        write_parquet(bad, f"s3://lake/quarantine/orders/run_id={run_id}/")

    bad_pct = len(bad) / max(len(df), 1)
    if bad_pct > 0.05:                      # a flood means the source changed - stop
        raise ValueError(f"{bad_pct:.1%} of rows quarantined - halting, investigate source")

    write_to_silver(good)
    emit_metric("pipeline.quarantined_rows", len(bad), tags={"table": "orders"})
    return {"loaded": len(good), "quarantined": len(bad)}

Pipeline Reliability

The difference between a pipeline and a production pipeline is entirely in how it behaves when things go wrong — which they will, roughly weekly, because you depend on other people's systems. Reliability work is unglamorous and it is the reason your platform is still running a year after you leave.

Design for reruns, because you will rerun

  • Every task takes an explicit time window as a parameter; no wall-clock reads.
  • Every write is delete-then-insert by partition, or a MERGE on a business key.
  • Every output path includes a run_id until the moment of atomic publication.
  • Every external side effect (email, webhook, reverse-ETL push) is guarded by an idempotency key and skipped during backfills.
  • Every task is safe to kill at any moment — no partial state outside a transaction.

PYTHON
import random, time, logging
from functools import wraps

log = logging.getLogger(__name__)

RETRYABLE = (TimeoutError, ConnectionError, TransientBackendError)

def with_retry(max_attempts=5, base=1.0, cap=60.0):
    def deco(fn):
        @wraps(fn)
        def wrapper(*a, **kw):
            for attempt in range(1, max_attempts + 1):
                try:
                    return fn(*a, **kw)
                except RETRYABLE as e:
                    if attempt == max_attempts:
                        log.error("%s failed after %d attempts: %s", fn.__name__, attempt, e)
                        raise
                    # Full jitter: prevents synchronised retry storms across many workers
                    delay = random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** (attempt - 1)))
                    log.warning("%s attempt %d failed (%s); sleeping %.1fs",
                                fn.__name__, attempt, e, delay)
                    time.sleep(delay)
        return wrapper
    return deco

Late-arriving data

Measure your lateness distribution before choosing a strategy. Most teams guess and are wrong by an order of magnitude in one direction or the other.

SQL
-- How late does data actually arrive? Run this before designing your lookback.
select
    datediff('hour', event_ts, _ingested_at)                 as lateness_hours,
    count(*)                                                 as events,
    round(100.0 * count(*) / sum(count(*)) over (), 3)       as pct,
    round(100.0 * sum(count(*)) over (order by datediff('hour', event_ts, _ingested_at))
          / sum(count(*)) over (), 3)                        as cumulative_pct
from bronze.events
where _ingested_at >= dateadd(day, -30, current_date())
group by 1
order by 1;
-- Typical result: 99.0% within 1h, 99.8% within 6h, 99.95% within 24h,
-- and a long tail of rows that are 30+ days late from one broken partner integration.

Lookback window

Reprocess the last N days on every run. Simple, idempotent, costs N× the compute of one day. Size N at your p99.9 lateness. The default choice for batch.

Late side-channel

Route events past the watermark into a separate table, then merge them during a nightly reconciliation. Keeps the hot path fast and makes lateness visible as a number.

Restatement

Accept that the last N days are provisional and republish them. Tell consumers explicitly: "figures for the last 3 days may change." This is honest and avoids a doomed quest for immediate perfection.

Governance, Lineage and Privacy

Governance is where a technically excellent platform gets blocked for six months. Access control that survives your departure

  • Grant to roles, never to individuals. People leave; roles persist.
  • Use functional roles (ANALYST_FINANCE, ENGINEER_PLATFORM) mapped from the customer's identity provider groups, so joiners/leavers are handled by HR processes, not by you.
  • Default deny on raw and bronze. Almost nobody needs raw access; those who think they do usually need a documented silver model.
  • Separate read from write. Analysts read gold; only service principals write.
  • One service principal per pipeline, with the minimum grants that pipeline needs — not one superuser shared by everything.
  • Time-bounded elevated access for debugging, with an audit trail, not a permanent admin grant "just in case".
  • Write the role matrix into a document and hand it over. The matrix, not the grants, is the deliverable.

Partition pruning: the biggest single lever

Pruning means the engine skips data without reading it. Defeating pruning is the most common performance mistake in customer code, and it is almost always a function wrapped around the partition column.

SQL
-- DEFEATS PRUNING: the function hides the column from the optimiser.
-- Engine must evaluate date_trunc on every row of every partition.
select count(*) from fct_orders
where date_trunc('month', order_date) = '2025-11-01';

-- PRUNES: a bare range predicate on the partition column.
select count(*) from fct_orders
where order_date >= '2025-11-01' and order_date < '2025-12-01';

-- DEFEATS PRUNING: implicit cast on the column side.
select * from fct_orders where cast(order_date as string) like '2025-11%';

-- DEFEATS PRUNING: a non-deterministic or correlated subquery in the predicate
-- can prevent static pruning on some engines; materialise the bound first.
select * from fct_orders
where order_date = (select max(order_date) from fct_orders);   -- check the plan!

-- PRUNES: compute the bound, then pass it as a literal/parameter.
set max_dt = (select max(order_date) from fct_orders);
select * from fct_orders where order_date = $max_dt;
Read the query plan, alwaysEXPLAIN in Snowflake/Databricks, the execution details pane, or BigQuery's "bytes processed" estimate before you run. The number you want is partitions scanned / partitions total. If a query on a 1,000-partition table scans 1,000 partitions to answer a one-day question, you have found a ten-minute fix worth thousands of dollars a year.

Spilling: the silent performance killer

When a join or sort does not fit in memory, the engine spills to local disk, and then to remote storage. Remote spill is catastrophic — often a 10-50x slowdown. If you see bytes_spilled_to_remote_storage > 0, fix it before anything else.

  • Filter before joining, not after — push predicates into CTEs that feed the join.
  • Select only needed columns; a 40-column join spills where a 6-column join does not.
  • Aggregate before joining when the join is many-to-one and you only need the aggregate.
  • Check for an accidental cross join — a missing join condition shows as an enormous intermediate row count.
  • Only after the above: size the warehouse up, which doubles memory.

Feeding AI and ML

Almost every AI project you are brought in for is, under the hood, a data engineering project with a model at the end. The model is a commodity; the pipeline that keeps its inputs correct, fresh and consistent between training and serving is where the engineering is. If you can make a customer's data reliable, the AI part usually becomes straightforward.

Training/serving skew — the defect that destroys models in production

A model scores 0.91 AUC offline and performs like a coin flip in production. Nine times out of ten the cause is that a feature was computed one way in the training pipeline and a different way at inference time.

-- For each label event, compute features using ONLY data available before that moment.
with labels as (
    select customer_id, churn_date as as_of_ts, 1 as label
    from churn_events
    union all
    select customer_id, sample_date as as_of_ts, 0 as label

                   then o.order_id end)                          as orders_30d,
        coalesce(sum(case when o.order_ts >= dateadd(day, -90, l.as_of_ts)
                          then o.net_amount end), 0)             as spend_90d,

    from labels l

The leak that makes a model look brilliantA churn model with 0.99 AUC almost certainly has a leaked feature — something that only exists because the customer churned, like cancellation_reason_filled or an account status that was updated at churn time. Feature pipelines and feature stores

Offline store

Historical feature values, point-in-time correct, used to build training sets. Usually just tables in the warehouse. The important property is that you can reconstruct any feature as of any timestamp.

Online store

Low-latency key-value lookup of the current feature vector for one entity. Redis, DynamoDB, Bigtable. Written by the same pipeline that writes offline, which is what prevents skew.

Registry

The definition of each feature — name, owner, source, transformation, freshness. Feast, Tecton, Databricks Feature Store, or a dbt project with conventions. Start with the last one; most customers do not need a feature store.

Do they need a feature store?Almost certainly not in the first engagement. A feature store earns its keep when you have many models sharing many features and genuinely need sub-50 ms online lookups. One model with daily batch scoring needs a well-tested dbt model and a table. Proposing Tecton to a team with two models is how you spend a quarter on infrastructure instead of value.

Document ingestion for RAG at scale

Prototypes load twenty PDFs in a notebook. Production handles a hundred thousand documents that change, with permissions, and must be re-indexed incrementally. The difference is entirely data engineering.

Discover→ Extract text→ Chunk→ Enrich metadata→ Embed→ Upsert to index→ Reconcile deletes
PYTHON
import hashlib, json
from dataclasses import dataclass, asdict

@dataclass
class Chunk:
    chunk_id: str          # deterministic: hash(doc_id, chunk_index, text)
    doc_id: str
    doc_version: str       # source etag / modified time / content hash
    chunk_index: int
    text: str
    token_count: int
    # Metadata is what makes retrieval filterable - this is where most
    # production RAG quality comes from, far more than the embedding model.
    source_path: str
    title: str
    section: str
    acl_groups: list[str]  # MUST be carried through; filter at query time
    effective_from: str
    effective_to: str | None
    language: str

def chunk_id_for(doc_id: str, idx: int, text: str) -> str:
    h = hashlib.sha256(f"{doc_id}|{idx}|{text}".encode()).hexdigest()[:32]
    return f"{doc_id}:{idx}:{h}"

def incremental_index(docs, vector_store, embedder, state) -> dict:
    """Only embed what changed. Embedding is the expensive step; re-embedding
    100k unchanged documents every night is a pure waste of money and time."""
    seen_doc_ids, embedded, skipped = set(), 0, 0

    for doc in docs:
        seen_doc_ids.add(doc.doc_id)
        prev_version = state.get_version(doc.doc_id)
        if prev_version == doc.version:
            skipped += 1
            continue                                   # unchanged, nothing to do

        chunks = chunk_document(doc)                   # your chunking strategy
        vectors = embedder.embed_batch([c.text for c in chunks], batch_size=256)

        vector_store.delete(filter={"doc_id": doc.doc_id})    # remove old chunks first
        vector_store.upsert([
            {"id": c.chunk_id, "values": v, "metadata": asdict(c)}
            for c, v in zip(chunks, vectors)
        ])
        state.set_version(doc.doc_id, doc.version)
        embedded += len(chunks)

    # Deletion reconciliation: documents that disappeared from the source
    # must disappear from the index, or the assistant will cite dead policy.
    for stale_id in state.all_doc_ids() - seen_doc_ids:
        vector_store.delete(filter={"doc_id": stale_id})
        state.delete(stale_id)

    return {"embedded_chunks": embedded, "skipped_docs": skipped}
The two RAG pipeline failures that reach the customer1. Permissions are not carried into the index. Every chunk must store the ACL of its source document, and every query must filter on the caller's groups. Otherwise your assistant will cheerfully quote the redundancy plan to the person named in it. 2. Deletions are not reconciled. A policy document is withdrawn, but its chunks remain in the vector store, so the assistant keeps citing a policy that no longer exists. Both are data-engineering problems, and both are found in demos, not in design reviews.

Embedding pipeline economics

DecisionImpact
Chunk size (256 vs 512 vs 1024 tokens)Smaller = more chunks = more vectors, more cost, finer retrieval but less context per hit. 400-800 tokens with 10-15% overlap is a sane starting point.
Re-embed on every run vs incremental100k docs × 5 chunks × re-embedding daily is 150M embeddings/month versus a few thousand. Usually a 100x cost difference.
Batch sizeEmbedding APIs are far more efficient batched. 128-512 texts per call typically; respect the provider's token limit per request.
Model dimension (384 vs 1536 vs 3072)Storage and index memory scale linearly. 1M chunks at 1536 float32 dims is ~6 GB before index overhead. Consider dimension reduction or quantisation.
Where embedding runsA hosted API sends document text to a third party — this is a data-residency and confidentiality question, not a technical one. Check before you build.

Monitoring an ML/AI data pipeline

  • Feature freshness per feature, not per table — a stale feature silently degrades predictions.
  • Feature distribution drift: PSI or KL divergence against the training distribution, per feature, weekly.
  • Null rate per feature — a sudden jump usually means an upstream schema change.
  • Unseen-category rate for encoded columns.
  • Training/serving consistency check: replay a sample of production inference inputs through the training feature code and assert equality. Run this nightly; it is the only test that actually catches skew.
  • Index coverage for RAG: documents in source vs documents indexed vs chunks in the vector store, reconciled daily.

When something eventually does need to run in production, it goes through their deployment process — which is also the thing you want them using after you leave.

Sandboxes and sample data

Zero-copy clone (Snowflake, BigQuery snapshots, Delta shallow clone)

Clone production into a dev schema instantly, with no storage cost until you modify it. This is the single best thing to ask for: full-fidelity data, zero risk to production, no copying. create schema dev_pranit clone analytics;

Masked or synthetic extracts

When data may not leave the tenancy or PII cannot be exposed, ask for a masked view or a generated sample preserving distributions, cardinalities and null rates. Insist on realistic skew — a uniform synthetic dataset hides every performance problem you were hired to find.

Working with the people

  • Find the person who actually knows how things work. It is rarely the architect. It is usually someone who has been there seven years and maintains the critical job. Buy them coffee in week one.
  • Do not criticise the existing system in front of its author. Every bad design was a reasonable decision under constraints you do not know about. Ask "what drove this?" instead.
  • Ship something small in week one. A cost report, a freshness dashboard, one fixed query. Credibility compounds and it buys you latitude later.
  • Work in their tools. Their git, their ticketing, their Slack, their naming conventions. A perfect system in your tooling is a system they will abandon.
  • Write down decisions. Short ADRs: context, options, decision, consequences. Six months later this is what prevents the "why did we do it this way?" spiral.
  • Demo weekly to the business, not just to engineering. The person funding this needs to see progress in their own terms.
  • Plan the handover from day one. Pairing sessions, a recorded walkthrough, a runbook, and a named owner per pipeline — agreed in writing before you leave.
The handover testBefore you leave, do this: have a member of their team, not you, respond to a deliberately broken pipeline using only your runbook, while you watch and say nothing. Every gap you see is a gap that will become a 2 a.m. phone call. Fix them, then repeat. This single exercise is worth more than any amount of documentation written in isolation.

Interview Questions

Walk me through what happens when you run dbt build.
dbt parses every .sql and .yml file, renders the Jinja to resolve ref(), source(), macros and variables, and builds a DAG from those dependencies, producing a manifest.json. It then executes nodes in topological order, respecting the thread count. For each model it wraps the compiled select in DDL appropriate to the materialisation — create or replace view, create or replace table as, or for incremental models a merge or insert against the existing relation, creating it first if it does not exist. Crucially, build interleaves tests, snapshots and seeds with model execution in DAG order, so if a staging model's uniqueness test fails, the downstream models that depend on it are skipped rather than built on bad data. That is the difference from dbt run && dbt test, where bad data reaches your marts before any test runs. Results are written to run_results.json, which you can feed into monitoring or into a subsequent --select state:modified+ run in CI.
A nightly pipeline failed at 02:00. You find out at 09:00 because an analyst complains. What do you fix?
Two things, in order. First the immediate data problem: identify the failed task and logical date, confirm the loads are idempotent, clear the failed task and its downstream, rerun, and verify row counts and a reconciliation query against the source. Second — and more important — fix the detection gap, because a seven-hour mean time to detection by human complaint is the actual defect. That means a failure callback from the orchestrator into the team's alert channel with the DAG, date and error, plus an independent freshness heartbeat that asserts max(_loaded_at) > now() - SLA for every tier-1 table and runs outside the orchestrator so it still fires when the orchestrator itself is down. Then I would write a short incident note and add whichever test would have caught the root cause earlier. The pattern I am applying is that every incident should produce exactly one new automated check.
Explain the difference between an incremental model with and without a lookback window.
Without a lookback, the incremental filter is typically where event_ts > (select max(event_ts) from {{ this }}). That is correct only if data always arrives in event-time order, which is never true in practice — mobile clients buffer offline, partner feeds are late, upstream systems retry, and some rows are backdated deliberately. Anything whose event time is older than the current maximum will never be picked up, so you lose data silently and permanently. A lookback changes the filter to >= max(event_ts) - interval 'N days', which reprocesses a window on every run. Combined with a merge on the unique key, the reprocessing is idempotent — the same rows are simply rewritten with the same values. You size N from measured lateness, typically p99.9, not from intuition: query the distribution of datediff(hour, event_ts, _ingested_at) over the last month. The trade-off is cost, since you reprocess N days of data every run, so for very large tables you combine a short lookback with a periodic full refresh or a separate late-data reconciliation job.
What is a slowly changing dimension type 2 and how do you query it correctly?
SCD2 preserves history by never updating a row in place. When a tracked attribute changes, you close the existing row by setting valid_to and is_current = false, and insert a new row with a new surrogate key, the new values, valid_from set to the change time, valid_to set to a high sentinel like 9999-12-31, and is_current = true. There are then two correct query patterns and they answer different questions. For "current state", filter on is_current. For "as it was at the time of an event" — which is what any historically accurate report needs — join with a half-open interval: on d.customer_id = f.customer_id and f.event_ts >= d.valid_from and f.event_ts < d.valid_to. The half-open interval matters because closed intervals on both ends create either overlaps or gaps at the boundary, and you will get duplicate or missing rows. Using a sentinel end date rather than NULL keeps that predicate simple; with NULL you need an OR in every query and someone will forget it.
Your warehouse bill tripled in two months. How do you investigate?
I start with attribution before hypotheses. Query the metering history grouped by warehouse and by day to see whether it is a step change on a date or gradual growth, and which warehouse. Then join query history to find the most expensive queries by elapsed time and bytes scanned, grouped by user and by query shape. In my experience four causes cover most cases. First, a warehouse whose auto_suspend was removed or lengthened, so it bills while idle — compare hours billed against hours actually executing queries; utilisation under about 20% is the tell. Second, a BI tool refreshing on a short timer against daily data, keeping a warehouse permanently warm. Third, a model that was incremental and became full-refresh, often because someone added --full-refresh to the scheduled command and never removed it. Fourth, a query that lost partition pruning because someone wrapped the partition column in a function; look for queries where partitions_scanned / partitions_total is near 1 on a large table. I would fix the top two or three, set a resource monitor as a hard guardrail, and publish a weekly cost-by-warehouse view so it stays visible.
When would you choose streaming over batch, and what does it cost the customer?
I would choose streaming only when a business decision genuinely depends on data that is seconds or low minutes old — fraud blocking, operational control rooms, live personalisation, inventory that drives a customer-facing promise. For anything consumed by a human looking at a dashboard, micro-batch every 5-15 minutes is almost always sufficient and is perceived as real-time. The costs are mostly operational rather than infrastructural: a streaming job runs continuously, so it needs an on-call rotation, consumer lag monitoring, checkpoint and state management, schema compatibility enforcement at the registry, and it has no natural "just rerun it tomorrow" recovery path. Backfilling a streaming aggregate requires a separate batch job, so you often end up maintaining two implementations of the same logic. I would ask the customer what decision changes if the data is 15 minutes old instead of 15 seconds; if they cannot answer concretely, we build micro-batch and revisit in six months with evidence.
What is the difference between a data lake, a warehouse and a lakehouse, in terms a CTO would care about?
The difference a CTO should care about is who owns the storage and what it costs to change engines. A warehouse keeps your data in the vendor's storage behind their SQL engine, which gives excellent performance and governance but means switching vendors is a migration project. A lake keeps data as files in your own object storage in open formats, which is cheap and flexible but historically had no transactions, no schema enforcement and no indexes, so it degrades into an unqueryable swamp without discipline. A lakehouse adds a table format — Delta, Iceberg or Hudi — over those files, which restores ACID transactions, schema enforcement, time travel and statistics while keeping the data in storage you control and readable by multiple engines. Practically, the categories have converged: Snowflake, BigQuery and Databricks all read Iceberg now. So my advice to a CTO is less "pick a category" and more "make sure your lowest layer is an open table format in your own account, so the engine above it is a reversible decision." That single choice preserves negotiating leverage for years.
How do you handle a schema change in a source system?
The handling depends on the type of change, and the goal is that breaking changes fail loudly and non-breaking ones flow through. Additive changes — a new nullable column — should be detected and either ignored or propagated depending on on_schema_change in dbt, which I set explicitly rather than relying on the default. Type widening, like int to bigint, is usually safe. Type narrowing, column removal and renames are breaking, and the right behaviour is to fail the staging model with a clear error rather than silently producing nulls — a rename in particular looks like a delete plus an add, so under backward compatibility the registry accepts it while every consumer silently starts getting defaults. Structurally, I defend against all of this in three places: a schema assertion at the landing boundary that compares the observed column set and types against an expected manifest; a schema registry with at least BACKWARD compatibility enforced for streaming sources; and a data contract with a notice period for breaking changes and a named owner. Then I make the staging layer the single place where a source quirk is absorbed, so one file changes rather than twelve.
Explain idempotency in a pipeline and why it matters so much.
A task is idempotent if running it twice with the same parameters leaves the system in the same state as running it once. It matters because retries, backfills and partial failures are normal operating conditions, not exceptions — if a task is not idempotent, every retry corrupts data and you cannot safely operate the pipeline at all. In practice it means three disciplines. Writes are delete-then-insert scoped to the partition the run owns, or a MERGE on a business key, never a bare append. Every date in the task body comes from the orchestrator's interval parameters rather than current_date(), so behaviour depends on the window being processed rather than on when the code happens to run. And external side effects — emails, webhooks, reverse-ETL pushes — are guarded by an idempotency key and suppressed during backfills, because the warehouse part being idempotent does not help if the last task re-sends ninety days of marketing emails. The test I apply is: can I kill this task at any instant and rerun it with no cleanup? If not, it is not production-ready.
A stakeholder says two dashboards show different revenue. How do you approach it?
I resist the urge to debug SQL immediately and instead establish what each number means, because in my experience the majority of these cases are definitional rather than defects. I ask for the exact filters, the date range, and the timezone of each dashboard, then trace both back through lineage to the models and ultimately to the source tables. Common causes, roughly in order: different timezone boundaries so one dashboard's "yesterday" is shifted by five and a half hours; different inclusion rules, like one counting cancelled orders or including internal test accounts; different handling of refunds, gross versus net; a fan-out from a fact-to-fact join inflating one side; and genuinely different freshness, where one source is a day behind. I would reproduce both numbers with a single query per definition, present the reconciliation line by line, and then — this is the durable fix — define the metric once in a governed model or semantic layer and point both dashboards at it, with a reconciliation test against the source system so the drift cannot silently return. The output of the incident should be one canonical definition, not a fixed dashboard.
What are the risks of CDC with Debezium against a customer's production database?
The headline risk is the replication slot. Postgres cannot recycle WAL segments that a slot has not confirmed, so if your consumer stops — a deploy, a crash, a network partition, a Kafka outage — WAL accumulates and the source database's disk fills, which can take the customer's production system down. That is a severe incident caused by the analytics pipeline, and it is the fastest way to lose trust. Mitigations are monitoring the slot's byte lag with alerts well below free disk, setting max_slot_wal_keep_size so the database sacrifices the slot rather than itself, and having a documented re-snapshot procedure. Secondary risks: the initial snapshot can take long-held locks or saturate IO on a large table, so it should be run in a maintenance window or use an incremental snapshot mode; REPLICA IDENTITY FULL is needed for full before-images on updates and deletes but increases WAL volume; schema changes in the source propagate as events that can break consumers; and the DBA team must grant replication privileges and change wal_level, which requires a restart and therefore a change request. I would always raise the slot risk explicitly with the DBA team before proposing CDC, because they will respect that you knew about it, and because their buy-in is what makes the project possible.
You have two weeks at a customer and their data is a mess. What do you do?
I would pick one high-value, end-to-end slice rather than attempting breadth, because a working vertical slice changes the conversation and a half-finished platform does not. Week one: get access, map the landscape, and run the usage and cost audit queries, because that produces a concrete, surprising artefact I can present by day four. In parallel I agree with the sponsor on a single business question that matters — usually one dashboard or one metric — and its source tables. Week two: build that slice properly, which means raw landing preserved immutably, staging models with casts and renames, a dimension with SCD2 if history matters, a fact with a declared grain, tests on uniqueness, not-null, accepted values and a reconciliation test against the source, one orchestrated DAG that is idempotent and rerunnable, and a freshness check. Then a runbook and a handover session. I would deliberately leave the other eleven sources untouched and instead deliver a prioritised gap list with estimates. The outcome is one thing their team can trust and a pattern they can copy, which is far more valuable than eleven half-migrated pipelines and a slide deck.

Checklist and Lab Plan

Reading about pipelines does not make you able to build one under pressure in someone else's cloud account. Build the labs below in order; each one is deliberately small enough to finish and realistic enough to teach the failure modes. Then use the checklists on real engagements.

Lab 1 — Local lakehouse in a day

  1. Generate realistic data. Write a Python script producing 10M order rows with skewed customers (80/20), 2% late-arriving events, 0.5% duplicates and 1% malformed rows. Realistic skew is the point — uniform data hides every problem.
  2. Land it raw. Write NDJSON to raw/orders/ingest_date=.../run_id=.../. Never modify these files again.
  3. Convert to Parquet. Use DuckDB or PyArrow. Measure file sizes for CSV, Snappy Parquet and Zstd Parquet. Measure query time for a filtered aggregate on each.
  4. Demonstrate the small-file problem. Write the same data as 5,000 small files, time the same query, then compact to 256 MB files and time it again. Write the two numbers down; you will quote them to customers.
  5. Add a table format. Convert to Delta or Iceberg (DuckDB, delta-rs or local Spark). Perform an upsert, a delete, time travel to the previous version, then roll back.
  6. Build dbt models. Point dbt-duckdb at it: staging views, a dimension with a snapshot, an incremental fact. Add unique, not_null, relationships and one singular reconciliation test.
  7. Break it deliberately. Add a duplicate key, a null in a required column, a new currency code. Watch which test fires and how clear the message is.

Lab 2 — Orchestration and reliability

  1. Run Airflow or Dagster locally in Docker.
  2. Build a daily DAG over Lab 1: extract window → validate → load (delete+insert by partition) → dbt build → freshness check.
  3. Make every task take the logical date as a parameter. Prove idempotency by running the same date three times and asserting identical row counts.
  4. Backfill 30 days with max_active_runs=1. Observe the duration and think about what that would cost on a real warehouse.
  5. Inject failures: kill the worker mid-task, make the source return 500 for 30 seconds, feed a malformed file. Verify retries, quarantine and alerts behave as designed.
  6. Write the runbook, then have someone else recover a failure using only your runbook.

Lab 3 — CDC and streaming

  1. docker compose up Postgres, Kafka, Schema Registry, Kafka Connect with Debezium.
  2. Configure logical replication, a publication, and a Debezium connector on one table.
  3. Insert, update and delete rows; inspect the before/after envelopes on the topic.
  4. Stop the consumer for ten minutes and watch pg_replication_slots lag grow. Set max_slot_wal_keep_size and watch the slot get invalidated instead.
  5. Write a consumer that batches to Parquet and commits offsets only after a durable write. Kill it mid-batch and verify at-least-once with no loss.
  6. MERGE the CDC output into a silver table. Verify deletes propagate and that reprocessing the same batch is a no-op.
  7. Register an Avro schema, then try a breaking change and a non-breaking one. Observe the registry's response under each compatibility mode.

Lab 4 — The customer simulation

  1. Take a public dataset you have not seen before and give yourself four hours.
  2. Produce: a landscape map, a profiling report (row counts, null rates, cardinalities, date ranges, obvious quality issues), and five questions you would ask the owner.
  3. Build one fact and one dimension with tests, orchestrated and documented.
  4. Write a one-page summary for a non-technical sponsor: what you found, what you built, what you would do next, what it would cost.
  5. That one page is the actual deliverable. Practise it until it takes twenty minutes.

Checklist: starting at a new customer

  • Access request sent before day one, with justification per item and blocking items flagged.
  • The five questions asked, answered, written down and confirmed back in writing.
  • Landscape diagram drawn and corrected by their lead engineer.
  • Usage, cost and freshness audit queries run; findings presented by end of week two.
  • Data classification understood: what is PII, where may it be processed, who approves access.
  • One small visible win shipped in week one.
  • Scope agreed in writing as a vertical slice, not a platform.

Checklist: before a pipeline goes to production

  • Raw data landed immutably with ingest timestamp, batch id and source path.
  • Every task idempotent; reruns proven to produce identical results.
  • Every date derived from the orchestrator's interval, never from wall clock.
  • Lookback window sized from measured lateness, not guessed.
  • Grain declared in a comment and enforced by a uniqueness test.
  • Tests on uniqueness, not-null, accepted values, referential integrity and freshness.
  • At least one reconciliation test against the source system for the headline metric.
  • Quarantine path for invalid rows, with a count on a dashboard and a named triager.
  • Retries configured with backoff; non-retryable errors fail fast.
  • Alerting on failure and on absence, with the absence check outside the orchestrator.
  • Compaction and vacuum/expire-snapshots scheduled as explicit tasks.
  • Cost reviewed: warehouse size, auto-suspend, incremental vs full refresh, pruning verified in the plan.
  • PII masked or tokenised; access granted to roles, not people; retention policy enforced.
  • Runbook written, tested by someone else, and a named owner agreed.
  • Lineage and documentation generated and published where their team will find it.

Checklist: handover

  • Named owner per pipeline, agreed in writing by their manager, not just by them.
  • Runbook validated by having their engineer recover a failure unaided.
  • Recorded walkthrough of the architecture and the code, stored where they keep documents.
  • All credentials rotated out of your control; your access revoked on an agreed date.
  • Backlog of known gaps and deferred work, with your honest estimates attached.
  • A 30-day check-in scheduled, and a clear statement of what is and is not included.
