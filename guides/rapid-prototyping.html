The Demo-in-a-Day Doctrine

A Forward Deployed Engineer's core move is to compress the distance between a customer saying "could it do X?" and seeing X working on their own data. A deck describing a capability generates a follow-up meeting. A working thing that answers a question they actually have generates a budget. The entire discipline of rapid prototyping is about making that second outcome achievable within days rather than quarters, without writing code you will be ashamed of.

Why the working prototype wins

Three things happen when a customer uses a prototype rather than hearing about it, and none of them happen in a slide review.

Requirements become real

In a workshop people describe the system they imagine. In front of a prototype they say "no, we'd never type the order number, we'd paste the whole email" — a sentence worth four weeks of guesswork. Prototypes extract tacit requirements that no amount of interviewing surfaces.

The sceptics move

Every room has someone who believes it cannot work on their messy data. You cannot argue them out of it. You can run it on their messy data in front of them. Their objection becomes a specific, solvable gap instead of a general doubt.

You find the real blockers early

Building even a toy version forces you to get data access, discover that the API needs a VPN, learn that the PDFs are scanned images, and find out that half the records have no customer id. These are the things that kill projects in month four. Find them in week one.

The rules of prototype scope

  1. One user, one task, one happy path. Pick the single most valuable workflow and make it genuinely excellent. Three mediocre workflows demo worse than one that works.
  2. Real data, always. A prototype on synthetic data proves nothing to the person whose data is messy. Get a sample — masked, sampled, whatever it takes — but make it theirs. The moment they see their own product names on screen, the conversation changes.
  3. Fake the expensive parts deliberately and honestly. Hardcode the auth. Stub the write-back to SAP. Use a CSV instead of the live feed. Then say so out loud during the demo: "the write-back is stubbed; it's two days of work once we have the API credentials." Honesty about stubs buys enormous trust.
  4. No infrastructure you do not need today. No Kubernetes, no message queue, no microservices, no auth service, no CI pipeline. A single process on one box is correct for a prototype and you should be comfortable defending that.
  5. Timebox hard and ship the timebox. Three days means three days. If something is not done, cut scope, never quality of the demo path. A rough edge off the happy path is invisible; a crash on the happy path is the only thing anyone remembers.
  6. Write down everything you deferred. A prototype debt register, started on day one, is the artefact that lets you answer "how long to productionise?" with a number instead of a shrug.
The prototype trapThe demo goes well, the customer says "great, can we just put that live next week?", and six months later you are still running a Streamlit app with a hardcoded API key serving forty people. The defence is not refusing — it is having stated from the first demo, in writing, what the graduation path costs. "This is a prototype. To run it for real needs authentication, tests, a database instead of local files, and deployment into your tenancy: roughly four weeks." Say that in demo one, repeat it in demo two, and put it in the summary email. Then the decision to ship it anyway is theirs, made with information.

The default that is right most oftenFastAPI for the logic, Streamlit for the face. The backend holds everything real — retrieval, model calls, business rules — behind typed endpoints that can be tested with pytest and called by anything. The frontend is a thin client. When the customer says "actually we want this inside our portal", you throw away the Streamlit app and keep 90% of the work. When they say "can you just run it as a batch job", same answer. This split costs you about two extra hours on day one and saves weeks later.

When to say no to a prototype

  • The data needed does not exist yet and will not within the engagement.
  • Legal has not cleared sending the data to the model provider you planned to use, and there is no on-prem alternative in scope.
  • The real question is organisational ("will the team adopt this?") and a prototype cannot answer it.
  • They want a production system and are using the word "prototype" to avoid a procurement process. Clarify before you build.
  • The demo audience is three levels above anyone who has used the current process — build it, but insist a real user is in the room.

Python Project Setup That Does Not Waste an Hour

Every hour spent on environment problems is an hour not spent on the demo, and environment problems compound: the thing works on your machine, then not on the customer's VM, then not in the container. Fix this once with a layout you can reproduce from muscle memory and you will never think about it again.

Use uv

uv replaces pip, virtualenv, pip-tools and most of poetry's role, and it is dramatically faster — resolving and installing a FastAPI + LangChain + pandas stack typically drops from minutes to seconds. Speed matters here because you will rebuild environments many times a day on a customer laptop.

The one rule that makes the layout worth itapp/services/*.py must not import FastAPI, and must not import Streamlit. They take plain arguments and return plain objects. The .env that ended up on GitHubAdd .env to .gitignore before your first commit, not after. If a key does leak, rotating it is the only fix — deleting the commit does not help, because the history and any fork retain it, and scrapers find committed keys within minutes. On a customer engagement a leaked key is not just embarrassing, it is a reportable security incident. Use SecretStr so the value does not appear in logs or tracebacks, and run gitleaks or detect-secrets in a pre-commit hook.

FastAPI Fundamentals

FastAPI gives you a typed, validated, self-documenting HTTP API with very little ceremony, and it is the right backend for anything a customer's systems will eventually call. The parts worth knowing well are Pydantic validation, dependency injection, and the async model — because those three determine whether your prototype survives being pointed at by something other than your browser.

The minimum real app

PYTHON
# app/main.py
import logging
from contextlib import asynccontextmanager

from fastapi import FastAPI, Request
from fastapi.middleware.cors import CORSMiddleware
from fastapi.responses import JSONResponse

from app.config import get_settings
from app.routers import ask, documents, health
from app.services.retrieval import VectorIndex

logging.basicConfig(level=get_settings().log_level,
                    format="%(asctime)s %(levelname)s %(name)s %(message)s")
log = logging.getLogger(__name__)

@asynccontextmanager
async def lifespan(app: FastAPI):
    """Load expensive things ONCE at startup, not per request.
    This is the difference between a 40 ms demo and a 4 s demo."""
    s = get_settings()
    log.info("loading vector index from %s", s.vector_store_path)
    app.state.index = VectorIndex.load(s.vector_store_path)
    log.info("index ready: %d chunks", app.state.index.size)
    yield
    log.info("shutting down")
    app.state.index.close()

def create_app() -> FastAPI:
    s = get_settings()
    app = FastAPI(
        title=s.app_name,
        version="0.1.0",
        description="Prototype document assistant. Not production hardened.",
        lifespan=lifespan,
    )
    app.add_middleware(
        CORSMiddleware,
        allow_origins=s.allowed_origins,       # never ["*"] with credentials
        allow_credentials=True,
        allow_methods=["*"],
        allow_headers=["*"],
    )
    app.include_router(health.router)
    app.include_router(ask.router,       prefix="/api/v1")
    app.include_router(documents.router, prefix="/api/v1")

    @app.exception_handler(Exception)
    async def unhandled(request: Request, exc: Exception):
        log.exception("unhandled error on %s %s", request.method, request.url.path)
        return JSONResponse(
            status_code=500,
            content={"error": "internal_error",
                     "detail": "Something went wrong. The team has been notified.",
                     "request_id": request.headers.get("x-request-id", "-")},
        )
    return app

app = create_app()

# app/models/schemas.py
from datetime import datetime
from enum import Enum
from typing import Annotated, Literal

from pydantic import BaseModel, Field, field_validator, model_validator

class Scope(str, Enum):
    all = "all"
    policies = "policies"
    contracts = "contracts"

class AskRequest(BaseModel):
    question: Annotated[str, Field(min_length=3, max_length=2000,
                                   examples=["What is the notice period for a fixed-term contract?"])]
    scope: Scope = Scope.all
    top_k: Annotated[int, Field(ge=1, le=20)] = 6
    temperature: Annotated[float, Field(ge=0.0, le=1.0)] = 0.0
    include_sources: bool = True

    @field_validator("question")
    @classmethod
    def strip_and_check(cls, v: str) -> str:
        v = v.strip()
        if not v:
            raise ValueError("question cannot be blank")
        return v

class Citation(BaseModel):
    document_id: str
    title: str
    page: int | None = None
    snippet: str
    score: float

class AskResponse(BaseModel):
    answer: str
    citations: list[Citation] = []
    model: str
    latency_ms: int
    request_id: str
    truncated: bool = False

class DateRange(BaseModel):
    start: datetime
    end: datetime

    @model_validator(mode="after")
    def ordered(self):
        if self.start >= self.end:
            raise ValueError("start must be before end")
        return self
PYTHON
# app/routers/ask.py
import time, uuid
from typing import Annotated

from fastapi import APIRouter, Depends, HTTPException, Query, status

from app.deps import get_index, get_llm, verify_api_key
from app.models.schemas import AskRequest, AskResponse
from app.services.retrieval import VectorIndex
from app.services.llm import LLMClient, LLMTimeout, LLMRefusal

router = APIRouter(tags=["ask"], dependencies=[Depends(verify_api_key)])

@router.post("/ask", response_model=AskResponse,
             responses={422: {"description": "Validation error"},
                        503: {"description": "Model unavailable"}})
async def ask(
    req: AskRequest,
    index: Annotated[VectorIndex, Depends(get_index)],
    llm: Annotated[LLMClient, Depends(get_llm)],
) -> AskResponse:
    started = time.perf_counter()
    request_id = str(uuid.uuid4())[:8]

    chunks = index.search(req.question, k=req.top_k,
                          filters={"scope": req.scope.value} if req.scope != "all" else None)
    if not chunks:
        raise HTTPException(
            status_code=status.HTTP_404_NOT_FOUND,
            detail="No relevant documents found. Try rephrasing or widening the scope.",
        )

    try:
        result = await llm.answer(req.question, chunks, temperature=req.temperature)
    except LLMTimeout:
        raise HTTPException(status.HTTP_504_GATEWAY_TIMEOUT,
                            detail="The model took too long. Try a shorter question.")
    except LLMRefusal as e:
        raise HTTPException(status.HTTP_422_UNPROCESSABLE_ENTITY, detail=str(e))

    return AskResponse(
        answer=result.text,
        citations=result.citations if req.include_sources else [],
        model=llm.model_name,
        latency_ms=int((time.perf_counter() - started) * 1000),
        request_id=request_id,
        truncated=result.truncated,
    )

# Path and query parameters, typed and validated automatically
@router.get("/documents/{document_id}/chunks")
async def list_chunks(
    document_id: str,
    index: Annotated[VectorIndex, Depends(get_index)],
    limit: Annotated[int, Query(ge=1, le=200)] = 50,
    offset: Annotated[int, Query(ge=0)] = 0,
):
    return index.chunks_for(document_id, limit=limit, offset=offset)

Dependency injection

FastAPI's Depends is the mechanism that keeps route handlers thin and makes everything testable. A dependency is just a callable whose result is injected; it can be cached, nested, and overridden in tests.

PYTHON
# app/deps.py
import secrets
from typing import Annotated

from fastapi import Depends, Header, HTTPException, Request, status

from app.config import Settings, get_settings
from app.services.llm import LLMClient
from app.services.retrieval import VectorIndex

SettingsDep = Annotated[Settings, Depends(get_settings)]

def get_index(request: Request) -> VectorIndex:
    """Reads the singleton created in lifespan. No per-request loading."""
    return request.app.state.index

def get_llm(settings: SettingsDep) -> LLMClient:
    return LLMClient(api_key=settings.openai_api_key.get_secret_value(),
                     model=settings.model_name,
                     timeout_s=settings.request_timeout_s)

def verify_api_key(settings: SettingsDep,
                   x_api_key: Annotated[str | None, Header()] = None) -> None:
    """Minimal gate for a prototype. compare_digest avoids timing leaks."""
    expected = settings.demo_api_key.get_secret_value()
    if not x_api_key or not secrets.compare_digest(x_api_key, expected):
        raise HTTPException(status.HTTP_401_UNAUTHORIZED, detail="Invalid or missing API key")

Error handling that reads well

PYTHON
from fastapi import Request, status
from fastapi.exceptions import RequestValidationError
from fastapi.responses import JSONResponse

class DomainError(Exception):
    """Base for errors we expect and can explain to a user."""
    status_code = status.HTTP_400_BAD_REQUEST
    code = "domain_error"
    def __init__(self, message: str, **ctx):
        super().__init__(message)
        self.message, self.ctx = message, ctx

class DocumentNotIndexed(DomainError):
    status_code = status.HTTP_409_CONFLICT
    code = "document_not_indexed"

class ContextTooLarge(DomainError):
    status_code = status.HTTP_413_REQUEST_ENTITY_TOO_LARGE
    code = "context_too_large"

def register_error_handlers(app):
    @app.exception_handler(DomainError)
    async def domain_handler(request: Request, exc: DomainError):
        return JSONResponse(
            status_code=exc.status_code,
            content={"error": exc.code, "detail": exc.message, "context": exc.ctx},
        )

    @app.exception_handler(RequestValidationError)
    async def validation_handler(request: Request, exc: RequestValidationError):
        # Default FastAPI output is verbose; flatten it for a UI to display.
        problems = [{"field": ".".join(str(p) for p in e["loc"][1:]), "message": e["msg"]}
                    for e in exc.errors()]
        return JSONResponse(status_code=422,
                            content={"error": "validation_failed", "problems": problems})

Async vs sync, and when it actually matters

This is the single most misunderstood part of FastAPI and it causes real demo failures.

Handler typeWhere it runsConsequence
async def with only awaited I/OThe event loopCorrect. Thousands of concurrent waits on one process.
async def containing a blocking call (requests.get, time.sleep, heavy CPU)The event loop — and blocks itDisaster. Every other request on that worker stalls. The classic cause of "the demo froze".
def (plain)A threadpool (default 40 threads)Safe for blocking I/O. Limited by threadpool size; GIL limits CPU work.
PYTHON
import asyncio, time
import httpx, requests
from fastapi import APIRouter
from fastapi.concurrency import run_in_threadpool

router = APIRouter()

# WRONG: blocking library inside async - freezes the whole event loop
@router.get("/bad")
async def bad():
    r = requests.get("https://slow.example.com/data", timeout=30)   # blocks everything
    return r.json()

# RIGHT (a): async client inside async
@router.get("/good-async")
async def good_async():
    async with httpx.AsyncClient(timeout=30) as c:
        r = await c.get("https://slow.example.com/data")
    return r.json()

# RIGHT (b): keep the blocking library, use a plain def - FastAPI threadpools it
@router.get("/good-sync")
def good_sync():
    return requests.get("https://slow.example.com/data", timeout=30).json()

# RIGHT (c): CPU-bound work must leave the event loop explicitly
@router.post("/embed")
async def embed(texts: list[str]):
    vectors = await run_in_threadpool(model.encode, texts)   # or a process pool
    return {"n": len(vectors)}

            r = await c.post(MODEL_URL, json={"prompt": prompt})
            r.raise_for_status()
            return r.json()["text"]

                                   return_exceptions=True)

FastAPI for AI Applications

AI workloads break the assumptions of a normal web API: responses take ten seconds instead of fifty milliseconds, users expect to see tokens as they arrive, uploads are large, some jobs run for minutes, and the upstream provider rate-limits you. These patterns are what separate an AI prototype that feels fast from one that feels broken.

Streaming responses (SSE)

Streaming is the single highest-impact UX change in an LLM app. Time-to-first-token of 400 ms with tokens flowing feels instant; a 9-second blank screen feels broken, even though total latency is identical.

PYTHON
import json
from collections.abc import AsyncIterator

from fastapi import APIRouter
from fastapi.responses import StreamingResponse

router = APIRouter()

async def sse_events(question: str, llm, index) -> AsyncIterator[str]:
    """Server-Sent Events. Each message is 'data: <payload>\\n\\n'."""
    # 1. Tell the client what is happening before the slow part starts
    yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type': 'status', 'message': 'Searching documents...'})}\n\n"

    chunks = await index.asearch(question, k=6)
    yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type': 'sources', 'sources': [c.brief() for c in chunks]})}\n\n"
    yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type': 'status', 'message': 'Generating answer...'})}\n\n"

    # 2. Stream the tokens
    try:
        async for token in llm.astream(question, chunks):
            yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type': 'token', 'text': token})}\n\n"
    except Exception as e:
        yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type': 'error', 'message': str(e)})}\n\n"
        return

    # 3. Explicit terminator so the client knows it is complete, not truncated
    yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type': 'done'})}\n\n"

                    continue
                event = json.loads(line[6:])

           "application/vnd.openxmlformats-officedocument.wordprocessingml.document"}

CHUNK = 1024 * 1024

@router.post("/documents", status_code=status.HTTP_202_ACCEPTED)
async def upload(background: BackgroundTasks, files: list[UploadFile] = File(...)):
    if len(files) > 20:
        raise HTTPException(400, "Maximum 20 files per upload")

    accepted, rejected = [], []
    for f in files:
        if f.content_type not in ALLOWED:
            rejected.append({"name": f.filename, "reason": f"unsupported type {f.content_type}"})
            continue

        # Stream to disk - never f.read() a 25 MB file into memory per request
        dest = Path("data/uploads") / f"{hashlib.sha256(f.filename.encode()).hexdigest()[:12]}_{f.filename}"
        dest.parent.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
        size, digest = 0, hashlib.sha256()
        with dest.open("wb") as out:
            while block := await f.read(CHUNK):
                size += len(block)
                if size > MAX_BYTES:
                    out.close(); dest.unlink(missing_ok=True)
                    rejected.append({"name": f.filename, "reason": "exceeds 25 MB"})
                    break
                digest.update(block)
                out.write(block)
            else:
                accepted.append({"name": f.filename, "bytes": size,
                                 "sha256": digest.hexdigest()[:16], "path": str(dest)})

    for doc in accepted:
        background.add_task(index_document, doc["path"], doc["sha256"])

    return {"accepted": accepted, "rejected": rejected,
            "message": f"{len(accepted)} file(s) queued for indexing"}

BackgroundTasks runs after the response is sent, in the same process. That is perfect for a prototype and wrong for anything that must survive a restart. Know the boundary.

Timeouts, retries and rate limits against the model provider

PYTHON
import asyncio, random, time
import httpx

class LLMTimeout(Exception): pass
class LLMRateLimited(Exception): pass

class LLMClient:
    def __init__(self, api_key: str, model: str, timeout_s: float = 60.0,
                 max_concurrency: int = 8):
        self.model = model
        self._sem = asyncio.Semaphore(max_concurrency)
        self._client = httpx.AsyncClient(
            base_url="https://api.openai.com/v1",
            headers={"Authorization": f"Bearer {api_key}"},
            timeout=httpx.Timeout(timeout_s, connect=10.0, read=timeout_s),
            limits=httpx.Limits(max_connections=max_concurrency * 2,
                                max_keepalive_connections=max_concurrency),
        )

    async def _post(self, path: str, payload: dict, attempts: int = 4) -> dict:
        async with self._sem:
            for attempt in range(1, attempts + 1):
                try:
                    r = await self._client.post(path, json=payload)
                except httpx.ReadTimeout:
                    if attempt == attempts:
                        raise LLMTimeout(f"model timed out after {attempts} attempts")
                    await asyncio.sleep(random.uniform(0, 2 ** attempt))
                    continue

                if r.status_code == 429:
                    wait = float(r.headers.get("retry-after", 2 ** attempt))
                    if attempt == attempts:
                        raise LLMRateLimited("rate limited; try again shortly")
                    await asyncio.sleep(wait + random.uniform(0, 1))
                    continue
                if r.status_code >= 500:
                    if attempt == attempts:
                        r.raise_for_status()
                    await asyncio.sleep(random.uniform(0, 2 ** attempt))
                    continue

                r.raise_for_status()
                return r.json()

    async def answer(self, question: str, chunks: list, temperature: float = 0.0):
        context = "\n\n---\n\n".join(c.text for c in chunks)
        data = await self._post("/chat/completions", {
            "model": self.model,
            "temperature": temperature,
            "messages": [
                {"role": "system",
                 "content": "Answer ONLY from the provided context. "
                            "If the answer is not present, say so explicitly."},
                {"role": "user", "content": f"Context:\n{context}\n\nQuestion: {question}"},
            ],
        })
        return data["choices"][0]["message"]["content"]
Unbounded concurrency is how you get your key suspendedA demo where someone pastes 200 questions into a loop can fire 200 simultaneous requests, hit the provider's rate limit, trigger aggressive retries, and in the worst case get the organisation's key throttled for everyone — including the production system of a different team at the customer. The semaphore above is four lines and prevents this entirely. Set it before the first demo, not after the incident.

Streamlit Deep Dive

Streamlit's entire design rests on one idea that is bizarre until it clicks: your script re-runs from top to bottom on every single interaction. There is no component tree, no virtual DOM, no event handlers in the usual sense. Once you accept that, everything else — session state, caching, forms, fragments — is a consequence, and you can build a convincing app in an afternoon.

The rerun model

Everything confusing about Streamlit follows from this single loop. Memorise the two boxes.

The two session_state traps1. Assigning to a widget-bound key after the widget is created raises StreamlitAPIException. Mutate it in an on_click callback instead, which runs before the rerun. 2. session_state is per browser session, in memory, in one process. For a prototype that is acceptable; just never put anything in session_state you cannot afford to lose, and pin the deployment to one replica.

Caching: the two decorators and when each is wrong

@st.cache_data@st.cache_resource
CachesA copy of the return value (pickled)The object itself, shared globally
Use forDataFrames, query results, API responses, computationsML models, DB connections, HTTP clients, vector indexes
Mutation safetySafe — each caller gets its own copyUnsafe — all sessions share one object, so it must be thread-safe
ScopeShared across all users of the processShared across all users of the process

# app/services/retrieval.py - NO FastAPI imports. Pure logic, fully testable.
from __future__ import annotations

import hashlib
import re
from dataclasses import dataclass
from pathlib import Path

import chromadb
from chromadb.utils import embedding_functions


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Chunk:
    chunk_id: str
    document_id: str
    title: str
    page: int | None
    text: str
    score: float = 0.0

    def brief(self) -> dict:
        return {"document_id": self.document_id, "title": self.title, "page": self.page,
                "score": round(self.score, 3),
                "snippet": self.text[:280] + ("..." if len(self.text) > 280 else "")}


def chunk_text(text: str, target_tokens: int = 600, overlap_ratio: float = 0.15) -> list[str]:
    """Paragraph-aware chunking. Roughly 4 chars per token for English.
    Splitting on paragraph boundaries matters far more than the exact size:
    a chunk that ends mid-sentence retrieves badly and reads badly in a citation."""
    target_chars = target_tokens * 4
    overlap = int(target_chars * overlap_ratio)

    paras = [p.strip() for p in re.split(r"\n\s*\n", text) if p.strip()]
    chunks, current = [], ""
    for p in paras:
        if len(current) + len(p) + 2 <= target_chars:
            current = f"{current}\n\n{p}" if current else p
        else:
            if current:
                chunks.append(current)
            # Carry the tail of the previous chunk forward so a fact that
            # straddles a boundary is retrievable from either side.
            current = (current[-overlap:] + "\n\n" + p) if current and overlap else p
    if current:
        chunks.append(current)
    return chunks


class VectorIndex:
    def __init__(self, client, collection):
        self._client, self._col = client, collection

    @classmethod
    def load(cls, path: str, embedding_model: str = "all-MiniLM-L6-v2") -> "VectorIndex":
        Path(path).mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
        client = chromadb.PersistentClient(path=path)
        ef = embedding_functions.SentenceTransformerEmbeddingFunction(model_name=embedding_model)
        col = client.get_or_create_collection(
            name="documents", embedding_function=ef,
            metadata={"hnsw:space": "cosine"},
        )
        return cls(client, col)

    @property
    def size(self) -> int:
        return self._col.count()

    def add_document(self, document_id: str, title: str, pages: list[str]) -> int:
        """Idempotent: deletes existing chunks for this doc before inserting."""
        self._col.delete(where={"document_id": document_id})

        ids, docs, metas = [], [], []
        for page_no, page_text in enumerate(pages, start=1):
            for i, chunk in enumerate(chunk_text(page_text)):
                cid = hashlib.sha256(f"{document_id}|{page_no}|{i}".encode()).hexdigest()[:24]
                ids.append(cid)
                docs.append(chunk)
                metas.append({"document_id": document_id, "title": title, "page": page_no})

        # Chroma has a per-call batch limit; chunk the upsert.
        for s in range(0, len(ids), 256):
            self._col.upsert(ids=ids[s:s+256], documents=docs[s:s+256], metadatas=metas[s:s+256])
        return len(ids)

    def search(self, query: str, k: int = 6, filters: dict | None = None) -> list[Chunk]:
        res = self._col.query(query_texts=[query], n_results=k, where=filters or None)
        if not res["ids"] or not res["ids"][0]:
            return []
        out = []
        for cid, doc, meta, dist in zip(res["ids"][0], res["documents"][0],
                                        res["metadatas"][0], res["distances"][0]):
            out.append(Chunk(chunk_id=cid, document_id=meta["document_id"],
                             title=meta["title"], page=meta.get("page"),
                             text=doc, score=1.0 - dist))   # cosine distance -> similarity
        return out

    def documents(self) -> list[dict]:
        all_meta = self._col.get(include=["metadatas"])["metadatas"]
        seen: dict[str, dict] = {}
        for m in all_meta:
            d = seen.setdefault(m["document_id"], {"document_id": m["document_id"],
                                                   "title": m["title"], "chunks": 0})
            d["chunks"] += 1
        return sorted(seen.values(), key=lambda d: d["title"])

    def close(self) -> None:
        pass

from __future__ import annotations

import json

import httpx

from app.services.retrieval import Chunk

SYSTEM_PROMPT = """You answer questions using ONLY the numbered context passages provided.

Rules:
- Cite the passage number in square brackets for every factual claim, e.g. [2].
- If the context does not contain the answer, reply exactly:
  "I could not find that in the indexed documents."
  Do not use outside knowledge to fill the gap.
- Be concise. Three sentences unless the question needs more.
- If passages disagree, say so and cite both."""


@dataclass
class Answer:
    text: str
    citations: list[dict]
    truncated: bool = False


def build_context(chunks: list[Chunk], max_chars: int = 12_000) -> tuple[str, bool]:
    parts, used, truncated = [], 0, False
    for i, c in enumerate(chunks, start=1):
        block = f"[{i}] {c.title}" + (f" (page {c.page})" if c.page else "") + f"\n{c.text}"
        if used + len(block) > max_chars:
            truncated = True
            break
        parts.append(block)
        used += len(block)
    return "\n\n---\n\n".join(parts), truncated


class LLMClient:
    def __init__(self, api_key: str, model: str, timeout_s: float = 60.0, base_url: str | None = None):
        self.model = model
        self._client = httpx.AsyncClient(
            base_url=base_url or "https://api.openai.com/v1",
            headers={"Authorization": f"Bearer {api_key}"},
            timeout=httpx.Timeout(timeout_s, connect=10.0),
        )

    async def answer(self, question: str, chunks: list[Chunk], temperature: float = 0.0) -> Answer:
        context, truncated = build_context(chunks)
        r = await self._client.post("/chat/completions", json={
            "model": self.model,
            "temperature": temperature,
            "messages": [
                {"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM_PROMPT},
                {"role": "user", "content": f"Context:\n\n{context}\n\nQuestion: {question}"},
            ],
        })
        r.raise_for_status()
        text = r.json()["choices"][0]["message"]["content"]
        return Answer(text=text, citations=[c.brief() for c in chunks], truncated=truncated)

    async def astream(self, question: str, chunks: list[Chunk],
                      temperature: float = 0.0) -> AsyncIterator[str]:
        context, _ = build_context(chunks)
        payload = {
            "model": self.model, "temperature": temperature, "stream": True,
            "messages": [
                {"role": "system", "content": SYSTEM_PROMPT},
                {"role": "user", "content": f"Context:\n\n{context}\n\nQuestion: {question}"},
            ],
        }
        async with self._client.stream("POST", "/chat/completions", json=payload) as r:
            r.raise_for_status()
            async for line in r.aiter_lines():
                if not line.startswith("data: "):
                    continue
                body = line[6:]
                if body == "[DONE]":
                    return
                delta = json.loads(body)["choices"][0]["delta"]
                if "content" in delta:
                    yield delta["content"]

@router.post("/ask/stream")
async def ask_stream(req: AskRequest,
                     index: Annotated[VectorIndex, Depends(get_index)],
                     llm: Annotated[LLMClient, Depends(get_llm)]):
    async def events():
        rid = str(uuid.uuid4())[:8]
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type':'status','message':'Searching documents'})}\n\n"

        chunks = index.search(req.question, k=req.top_k)
        if not chunks:
            yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type':'error','message':'No relevant passages found.'})}\n\n"

            return

        log_query(rid, req.question, "".join(buf), [c.chunk_id for c in chunks], latency)
        yield f"data: {json.dumps({'type':'done','request_id':rid,'latency_ms':latency})}\n\n"

DB = Path("data/app.db")

def _conn():
    DB.parent.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    c = sqlite3.connect(DB, check_same_thread=False)
    c.execute("""create table if not exists queries (
        request_id text primary key, ts text, question text, answer text,
        chunk_ids text, latency_ms integer)""")
    c.execute("""create table if not exists feedback (
        request_id text, ts text, rating integer, comment text)""")

def log_query(request_id, question, answer, chunk_ids, latency_ms):
    with _conn() as c:
        c.execute("insert or replace into queries values (?,?,?,?,?,?)",
                  (request_id, datetime.datetime.now(datetime.UTC).isoformat(),
                   question, answer, json.dumps(chunk_ids), latency_ms))

def log_feedback(request_id, rating, comment=None):
    with _conn() as c:
        c.execute("insert into feedback values (?,?,?,?)",
                  (request_id, datetime.datetime.now(datetime.UTC).isoformat(), rating, comment))

    """Everything the customer asked during the demo, ready to become test cases."""
    with _conn() as c:
        rows = c.execute("""
            select q.question, q.answer, q.chunk_ids, f.rating
            from queries q left join feedback f using (request_id)
            order by q.ts""").fetchall()
    with open(path, "w", encoding="utf-8") as fh:

# scripts/ingest_pdfs.py - run this BEFORE the demo, not during it.
import sys, time
from pathlib import Path

import pypdf

from app.services.retrieval import VectorIndex


def extract_pages(path: Path) -> list[str]:
    reader = pypdf.PdfReader(str(path))
    pages = [(p.extract_text() or "").strip() for p in reader.pages]
    text_chars = sum(len(p) for p in pages)
    if text_chars < 200 * len(pages):
        # A scanned PDF. You will meet these at every customer.
        print(f"  WARNING: {path.name} looks scanned ({text_chars} chars across "
              f"{len(pages)} pages). OCR needed - skipping for now.")
    return pages


def main(folder: str, index_path: str = "./data/index"):
    index = VectorIndex.load(index_path)
    pdfs = sorted(Path(folder).rglob("*.pdf"))
    print(f"Found {len(pdfs)} PDFs")

    total_chunks, skipped, t0 = 0, 0, time.perf_counter()
    for i, path in enumerate(pdfs, start=1):
        try:
            pages = extract_pages(path)
            if not any(pages):
                skipped += 1
                continue
            n = index.add_document(document_id=path.stem, title=path.stem.replace("_", " "),
                                   pages=pages)
            total_chunks += n
            print(f"[{i}/{len(pdfs)}] {path.name}: {len(pages)} pages, {n} chunks")
        except Exception as e:
            skipped += 1
            print(f"[{i}/{len(pdfs)}] FAILED {path.name}: {e}")

    print(f"\nDone in {time.perf_counter() - t0:.1f}s. "
          f"{total_chunks:,} chunks, {skipped} files skipped. Index size: {index.size:,}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main(sys.argv[1] if len(sys.argv) > 1 else "./data/pdfs")

import hashlib, json
from pathlib import Path

CACHE = Path("data/demo_cache.json")

class DemoCache:
    """Answers canned responses for known questions. Two purposes:
    1) the demo works with no network at all
    2) known questions answer in 50 ms instead of 6 s, which looks far better"""

    def __init__(self, enabled: bool):
        self.enabled = enabled
        self._data = json.loads(CACHE.read_text()) if CACHE.exists() else {}

    @staticmethod
    def _key(question: str) -> str:
        norm = " ".join(question.lower().split()).rstrip("?.")
        return hashlib.sha256(norm.encode()).hexdigest()[:16]

    def get(self, question: str) -> dict | None:
        return self._data.get(self._key(question)) if self.enabled else None

    def put(self, question: str, answer: str, citations: list[dict]) -> None:
        self._data[self._key(question)] = {"answer": answer, "citations": citations}
        CACHE.write_text(json.dumps(self._data, indent=2))

The pre-demo checklist

  • Full run-through on the actual machine and network you will present from, at least two hours before.
  • python scripts/seed.py run; data is clean, realistic and contains nothing embarrassing.
  • Demo cache populated for every question in your script.
  • Backend warm — one dummy request fired so no model loading happens live.
  • Browser at the resolution you will project; zoom set; only the demo tab open.
  • Notifications off, sleep disabled, Do Not Disturb on, phone silenced.
  • Mobile hotspot tethered and tested as a fallback.
  • A screen recording of the full happy path saved locally, ready to play if everything fails.
  • Reset button tested — you will need it between sections.
  • Three improvised questions tried, so you know the error path looks acceptable.
  • Decided in advance what you will say when something breaks: "that's the prototype's error path doing its job — let me reset" is a perfectly good line.
  • Someone else on the call ready to drive the conversation while you fix anything.
How to handle a live failureDo not apologise repeatedly, do not start debugging on the shared screen, and never show a terminal full of red. Say what happened in one sentence, hit reset, and move on. If it fails twice, switch to the recording and say "I'll show you the recorded version and we'll debug offline — the interesting part is what it does, not whether the demo wifi holds." Customers forgive technical failure handled calmly; they remember panic.

import hmac, hashlib, time, base64

def sign_link(resource_id: str, ttl_s: int = 86400) -> str:
    secret = get_settings().link_secret.get_secret_value().encode()
    expires = int(time.time()) + ttl_s
    payload = f"{resource_id}:{expires}".encode()
    sig = base64.urlsafe_b64encode(hmac.new(secret, payload, hashlib.sha256).digest()).decode()
    return f"/shared/{resource_id}?expires={expires}&sig={sig}"

def verify_link(resource_id: str, expires: int, sig: str) -> bool:
    if time.time() > expires:
        return False
    secret = get_settings().link_secret.get_secret_value().encode()
    payload = f"{resource_id}:{expires}".encode()
    expected = base64.urlsafe_b64encode(hmac.new(secret, payload, hashlib.sha256).digest()).decode()
    return hmac.compare_digest(sig, expected)

Observability and Feedback in a Prototype

A prototype with no logging teaches you nothing. A prototype that captures every question, every retrieval, every latency and every thumbs-down turns a one-hour demo into a labelled dataset you can use to improve the system and to justify the next phase. This is the cheapest high-leverage thing in the entire guide, and almost nobody does it.

Structured request logging

PYTHON
# app/middleware.py
import json, logging, time, uuid

from fastapi import Request
from starlette.middleware.base import BaseHTTPMiddleware

log = logging.getLogger("requests")


class RequestLogMiddleware(BaseHTTPMiddleware):
    async def dispatch(self, request: Request, call_next):
        rid = request.headers.get("x-request-id") or str(uuid.uuid4())[:8]
        request.state.request_id = rid
        t0 = time.perf_counter()

        try:
            response = await call_next(request)
            status = response.status_code
        except Exception:
            status = 500
            raise
        finally:
            log.info(json.dumps({
                "request_id": rid,
                "method": request.method,
                "path": request.url.path,
                "status": status,
                "duration_ms": round((time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000, 1),
                "user": request.headers.get("x-auth-request-email", "anonymous"),
                "ua": request.headers.get("user-agent", "")[:80],
            }))

        response.headers["X-Request-ID"] = rid
        return response

async def embed_many(texts: list[str], batch_size: int = 256) -> list[list[float]]:
    """One API call per 256 texts instead of one per text.
    On a 10k-chunk corpus this is the difference between 20 minutes and 90 seconds."""
    out: list[list[float]] = []
    for i in range(0, len(texts), batch_size):
        batch = texts[i:i + batch_size]
        r = await client.post("/embeddings",
                              json={"model": "text-embedding-3-small", "input": batch})
        r.raise_for_status()
        out.extend(d["embedding"] for d in sorted(r.json()["data"], key=lambda d: d["index"]))
    return out

The Graduation Path

At some point the prototype gets used by real people, and everything you deliberately skipped becomes a liability. This section is the honest list of what changes, so you can estimate it, price it, and have the conversation before the customer discovers it the hard way.

The graduation gap. Estimate it once and reuse the estimate; it is remarkably consistent across projects.

The nine things that must change

  1. State moves out of the process. session_state and local files become Postgres (or the customer's standard store) with schema migrations via Alembic, backups, and a restore you have actually tested. Until this is done the app cannot be restarted safely or scaled at all.
  2. Real authentication and authorisation. SSO through their IdP, roles, and — critically for document systems — per-document permissions enforced at retrieval time, not just at the UI. A RAG system that ignores source ACLs is a data-leak machine.
  3. Secrets management. .env becomes Key Vault / Secret Manager / Parameter Store, injected at runtime, with rotation and an audit trail. No secret in an image, a repo or an environment variable printed in logs.
  4. Tests. Unit tests on the services, integration tests on the API, and an evaluation suite that runs your golden question set and asserts quality thresholds — because a prompt change can silently degrade answers in a way no unit test catches.
  5. CI/CD. Build, test, scan, push, deploy to staging, smoke test, promote. Plus a rollback that someone has performed at least once as a drill.
  6. Observability. Structured logs shipped somewhere searchable, metrics with dashboards, traces for the slow path, alerts with an on-call rotation, and an SLO everyone has agreed to.
  7. Scaling and resilience. Stateless replicas behind a load balancer, autoscaling rules, graceful shutdown, circuit breakers on the model provider, and a defined behaviour when the provider is down.
  8. A frontend that is not Streamlit. If more than a handful of people use it daily, you will hit the limits of the rerun model, the lack of sticky sessions, and the styling ceiling. Budget for React or their standard frontend stack.
  9. Ownership. A named team, documentation they wrote or reviewed, a runbook they have exercised, and an agreed support model. Without this the system decays regardless of its technical quality.

Anti-Patterns and How Prototypes Fail

These are the specific, repeated ways prototypes fail in front of customers. Each one is cheap to avoid and expensive to experience.

The infinite prototype

Four months in, still "a prototype", now with twelve features and six users, no tests, and nobody willing to own it. Caused by never defining what "done" means. Fix: a written scope and end date before you start, and a go/no-go decision meeting at the end.

The demo-only path

It works beautifully for the three questions you rehearsed and crashes on the fourth. Caused by testing only the script. Fix: have someone who has not seen it use it for ten minutes before the demo, and fix whatever they break.

The synthetic-data illusion

Built on clean generated data, it falls apart on the customer's actual records with their missing fields, duplicate ids and 1990s encodings. Fix: insist on real (masked) data in week one, even if it is only 500 rows.

The hidden stub

You stubbed the write-back and forgot to mention it. The customer thinks it is working end to end and tells their boss. Later this looks like dishonesty. Fix: say every stub out loud during the demo and list them in the follow-up email.

Over-engineering on day one

Three days spent on Kubernetes manifests, a message queue and a CI pipeline for a demo seen by four people once. Fix: the infrastructure rule — nothing that is not needed for this week's demo.

The silent failure

The model call fails, the code swallows the exception, and the UI shows an empty answer. The customer concludes the model is stupid rather than that the network is broken. Fix: never catch broadly without surfacing something to the user.

Cherry-picked examples

You only ever show the five documents you know retrieve well. Someone picks a sixth and it fails. Fix: test against a random sample before the demo and know your actual hit rate — then you can say "it handles roughly 8 in 10 of these; here's what the failures look like", which is far more credible.

The unreproducible environment

It runs on your laptop and nowhere else, because of an unpinned dependency, a local file path, or a tool you installed six months ago. Fix: build the Docker image on day one and do your own testing through it.

The failure that ends engagementsA prototype containing the customer's confidential documents left running on a public URL after the demo. Nobody turned it off; a scanner found it; the customer's security team found out from someone else. Everything you built becomes irrelevant. Have a written teardown step: when the demo period ends, the app is stopped, the data is deleted, the keys are rotated, and you send a short email confirming all three. Put it in your calendar before the demo, not after.

Interview Questions

A customer wants a demo in three days of an AI assistant over their internal documents. Walk me through your plan.
Day zero, before anything technical: I ask for a sample of the documents and confirm in writing whether that data may be sent to a hosted model API, because the answer determines the entire architecture and I cannot un-ask it later. Day one: ingestion, because that is where the surprises are. Extract text from the real files, discover which are scanned images, chunk, embed, index, and run ten questions through retrieval alone to see whether the right passages come back — if retrieval is bad the model cannot save it. Day two: FastAPI backend with a streaming endpoint and a Streamlit frontend with chat, sources panel, upload and a reset button, plus structured logging of every query. Day three: harden the demo — seed data, populate a demo cache by running the full script once, test on the actual network, write the pre-demo checklist, and spend an hour letting a colleague try to break it. I would deliberately not build authentication beyond a password gate, not deploy to the cloud unless they need remote access, and not add any feature beyond the single workflow we agreed. Everything I skipped goes in a debt register that I hand over with the demo.
Why would you build a FastAPI backend rather than putting everything in Streamlit?
Three reasons, and the first is the decisive one. First, reusability: when the customer says "actually we want this inside our existing portal" or "can it run as a batch job over the whole queue", a FastAPI backend already answers both and only the thin UI is thrown away. If the logic lives inside Streamlit callbacks, that request means a rewrite. Second, testability: service functions that take plain arguments and return plain objects can be tested in two seconds with pytest, with no browser and no server, which matters enormously when you are changing things an hour before a demo. Third, concurrency and streaming: Streamlit's one-script-per-session model makes real concurrency control, background jobs and token streaming awkward, whereas FastAPI gives you async, semaphores, SSE and proper timeouts. The cost is about two hours on day one plus one extra container. In the rare case where the thing genuinely is a single-user notebook replacement that will never be integrated, I would skip it and say so.
Explain Streamlit's execution model and the most common bug it causes.
Streamlit re-runs the entire script from top to bottom on every interaction. There is no component tree and no persistent handler scope — when you click a button, your script executes again from line one, and each widget function returns its current value. The most common bug is losing state: people store conversation history or a loaded model in a local variable and are confused when it resets on every click, because that variable is recreated each run. The fix is st.session_state for per-session data, initialised behind an if key not in session_state guard so it is set once, and the caching decorators for expensive objects — cache_resource for models, clients and connections which are shared by reference, and cache_data for serialisable results which are copied per caller. The second-most-common bug follows from the same model: loading a model at module level without caching, so every single keystroke in a text input reloads it and the app feels broken. Once you internalise "the script reruns, so anything that must survive goes in session_state or a cache", the framework stops being surprising.
What is the difference between async def and def in a FastAPI route, and when does it bite you?
An async def handler runs directly on the event loop, which is correct and efficient when everything slow inside it is awaited. A plain def handler is run in a threadpool, which is safe for blocking I/O at the cost of being bounded by the thread count. The bite is putting blocking code inside async def — calling requests.get, time.sleep, a synchronous database driver, or doing heavy CPU work. That blocks the event loop, so every other request handled by that worker stalls until it finishes. In single-user testing you never notice; in a demo where the customer opens three tabs, everything appears to hang and it looks like the model is broken. The rules I apply: use async def with async clients like httpx for I/O; use plain def if you want to keep a synchronous library; push CPU-bound work to run_in_threadpool or a process pool; and if unsure, write def, because the threadpool is marginally slower in theory and dramatically safer in practice.
How do you make a prototype resilient to a bad network during a customer demo?
Layered defence. First, a demo cache: I run the full demo script once on a good connection the day before, which stores the answer for every planned question keyed on a normalised hash, so on the day those questions return in milliseconds with no network at all. Second, graceful degradation: a short timeout on the model call — around ten seconds rather than sixty — falling back to the cache, and failing that to a message plus the retrieved passages, so the customer still sees the retrieval working even if generation fails. Third, no stack traces ever reach the screen; there is a catch-all handler that renders a readable message with the technical detail hidden behind an expander. Fourth, operational preparation: a tethered mobile hotspot tested in advance, minimum replicas set to one so there is no cold start, a warm-up request fired before they walk in, and a screen recording of the happy path saved locally. And finally a rehearsed sentence for when something fails anyway, because the recovery is what the room remembers, not the failure.
A customer wants to put your prototype straight into production. How do you respond?
I do not refuse, because refusing makes me an obstacle and they may have a legitimate reason to accept the risk. Instead I make the risk explicit and priced. I show the prototype debt register — which I started on day one — and separate it into three tiers: blockers that create unacceptable risk, items needed for minimum viable production, and nice-to-haves. For a document assistant the blocker is almost always authorisation: the prototype does not enforce per-document permissions, so users can retrieve content they are not entitled to, which is a data-leak risk regardless of how good the answers are. I would quantify that as, say, six days of work, and the minimum viable production path as around four to six weeks including auth, durable state, secrets management, an evaluation suite, deployment automation and monitoring. Then I let them choose. Very often they pick a middle path: fix the blockers, run a limited pilot with twenty named users, and schedule the rest. What matters is that the decision is theirs and informed, and that it is in writing — because in six months somebody will ask why there are no tests.
How do you handle authentication for a prototype that needs to be accessible to the customer?
The governing rule is that customer data is never reachable from the public internet without authentication — not "probably nobody will find it", because scanners find new hosts within minutes. Within that constraint I scale the effort to the context. If it only needs to be seen in a meeting, I screen-share from localhost and there is no auth problem to solve. If a handful of named people need to click around, a shared password gate plus HTTPS is defensible for non-sensitive data. The answer I actually push for, because it takes one to two hours and is what their security team wants to see, is an OAuth proxy in front of the app — oauth2-proxy in compose, or Azure Easy Auth, or Google IAP — which handles their identity provider entirely outside my code and passes the authenticated email in a header. My app stays auth-free and I get real SSO and an audit trail. What I would never do is a public Gradio share link or a cloud endpoint with open ingress containing their documents, because that is a reportable incident rather than a shortcut.
What would you log in a prototype, and why bother?
I log every interaction with the question, the retrieved chunk ids and their scores, the generated answer, the model, latency, and any thumbs-up or thumbs-down — into SQLite, which is one file I can hand over. The reason is that a demo is the only time you get real user queries before launch, and you cannot invent them. After a one-hour session you have thirty to sixty genuine questions with retrieval traces and partial human judgements, which is your first evaluation set and is far better than anything you would have written. It also lets you answer, concretely, the questions the customer will ask next: what was the p95 latency, what fraction of questions could not be answered from the corpus, which documents were never retrieved, what did a question cost on average. Sending that analysis in the follow-up email — "here are the forty questions you asked, here's where it struggled, here's what that implies for scope" — does more for the next phase than any slide. It costs about twenty lines of code.
Your demo app is slow — eight seconds per answer. What do you do?
First I measure where the time goes rather than guessing: retrieval, model generation, and any pre- or post-processing, timed separately. Usually generation dominates, in which case the highest-impact fix is not making it faster but making it feel faster by streaming — time to first token of a few hundred milliseconds with text flowing reads as instant, even though total latency is unchanged. That is a thirty-minute change and it is the single biggest perceived win available. After that: check that models, clients and the index are loaded once in the lifespan rather than per request, which often removes several seconds that nobody noticed were there; cache answers for repeated questions; reduce the number of retrieved chunks, since going from ten to five cuts input tokens by roughly half with little quality loss in most corpora; batch anything that loops over items; and consider a smaller model for the demo path. If retrieval is the slow part, the index is usually either too large for the machine or doing something pathological like re-embedding the query with a cold model. I would also set minimum replicas to one so there is no cold start, which is otherwise the worst eight seconds of the demo.
When would you choose Gradio over Streamlit?
Gradio when the app is fundamentally a model with inputs and outputs, and especially when the inputs or outputs are images, audio or video, because its components for those are much better. Also when I want a chat UI in under ten lines with streaming, cancellation and a queue already handled — ChatInterface does in five lines what takes thirty in Streamlit. Also when deploying to Hugging Face Spaces, which is Gradio's native home, or when I want the UI to double as a callable API, which Gradio exposes automatically. Streamlit when the app is more of an internal business tool — multiple pages, dataframes, filters, metrics, a sidebar of controls — because it has better primitives for data display and navigation and it looks more like software the customer's staff already use. For typical enterprise FDE work the answer is Streamlit more often than not, because the demos are usually chat plus a table plus an upload. The one thing I would never do is choose Gradio because of the share=True link when the app contains customer data, since that is a public tunnel with no authentication.
How do you keep a three-day prototype from becoming unmaintainable?
One structural rule does most of the work: no business logic inside a framework callback. Everything real — retrieval, prompt construction, model calls, parsing, validation — lives in plain functions in a services module that imports neither FastAPI nor Streamlit. The route handlers and the UI only call those functions and display results. That single discipline means the logic is testable without a server, reusable from a script or a notebook, and survives replacing the UI. On top of that: pin dependencies with a lockfile so the environment is reproducible, keep configuration in one pydantic-settings class rather than scattered os.getenv calls, write three tests covering the happy path, a validation failure and an upstream failure, and build the Docker image on day one so you discover environment problems immediately rather than the night before. None of this is heavyweight — it is maybe two extra hours across three days — and it is the difference between a prototype that can be graduated and one that can only be rewritten.
What would make you refuse to build a prototype?
A few situations. If the data required does not exist and will not exist within the engagement, a prototype would have to run on fiction, and a demo on synthetic data proves nothing to a room full of people who know their data is messy. If legal or privacy has not cleared sending the data to the model provider I planned to use, and there is no viable on-premise or in-tenancy alternative within scope, I would stop and resolve that first rather than build something I may have to delete. If the real question is organisational — will people actually change their workflow — a prototype cannot answer it, and I would propose a different intervention such as shadowing the current process. And if they are describing a production system but calling it a prototype to bypass procurement and security review, I would clarify that explicitly, because building to prototype standards something that will be used in production is how people get hurt. In every one of these cases the right move is to say what I think is actually needed, propose the smaller thing that would genuinely de-risk the decision, and get agreement in writing.

Build-a-Demo Lab Plan and Checklists

The only way to get fast at this is to do it repeatedly under a clock. Each lab below is deliberately timeboxed; the discipline of stopping when the box ends is as much of the skill as the code.

Lab 1 — Four hours: the baseline demo

  1. 00:00-00:20 — uv init, pin Python, add FastAPI, Streamlit, httpx, pydantic-settings. Create the folder layout. Commit.
  2. 00:20-01:00 — Backend: /health, /ask returning a hardcoded answer, settings class, three pytest tests with fakes. Prove the tests run in under five seconds.
  3. 01:00-01:40 — Frontend: chat UI calling the backend. Sidebar with health status. Reset button. The entire frontend talks to the backend through one api_client.py.
  4. 01:40-02:30 — Make it real: ingest 20 documents you actually have, wire retrieval, wire a model. Fix whatever breaks.
  5. 02:30-03:00 — Add streaming end to end. Measure time to first token before and after; write both numbers down.
  6. 03:00-03:30 — Harden: catch-all error handler, friendly error states, loading indicators, demo cache, seed script.
  7. 03:30-04:00 — Dockerfiles and compose. Run docker compose down && up and confirm the index survives. Write a five-line README.

Lab 2 — Two hours: deploy and lock down

  1. Push both images to a registry.
  2. Deploy to Container Apps or Cloud Run with internal ingress on the API and external on the UI.
  3. Put secrets in the platform's secret store; confirm nothing sensitive is in the image (docker history and a grep of the layers).
  4. Add an OAuth proxy or the platform's built-in auth. Confirm that an unauthenticated request gets a 401 — actually test it from a private browser window.
  5. Verify streaming still works through the load balancer. If it buffers, fix the headers. This is the step everyone skips and everyone regrets.
  6. Set minimum replicas to one, measure cold vs warm first-request latency, write both into your runbook.
  7. Write the teardown script: stop, delete data, rotate keys.

Lab 3 — One hour: break it on purpose

  1. Disconnect the network mid-answer. What does the user see? Fix it.
  2. Set the model API key to garbage. What does the user see? Fix it.
  3. Upload a 40 MB file, a zero-byte file, a .exe renamed to .pdf, and a scanned PDF.
  4. Paste 50,000 characters into the question box.
  5. Open five tabs and submit simultaneously. Does anything block? If yes, you have a blocking call inside async def.
  6. Restart the container mid-conversation. What is lost? Is that acceptable? Write it in the debt register.
  7. Ask ten questions you know are not covered by the corpus. Does it hallucinate or say it cannot find them? This is the behaviour the customer will test first.

Lab 4 — Repeat under pressure

  1. Pick a dataset or document corpus you have never seen. Give yourself four hours total.
  2. Build the demo, deploy it, and present it to someone for ten minutes without rehearsing.
  3. Log every question they ask. Export the eval set. Write the one-page follow-up.
  4. Do this three times with different domains. By the third you will have internalised the layout, the error handling and the checklist, and four hours will feel comfortable.
