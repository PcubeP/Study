Why Writing Is the Highest-Leverage Engineering Skill

A Forward Deployed Engineer's code runs inside one customer's estate and is read by a handful of people. Their writing is read by everyone who decides, funds, operates or extends that work — and continues to be read after they have left the site. Writing is the only artefact that scales past your presence, survives your departure, and determines whether the thing you built is understood, trusted, adopted and maintained. It is not a soft skill adjacent to engineering; it is the mechanism by which engineering work produces durable value.

The departure test

Everything in this guide reduces to one question: when you leave the engagement, what remains that works without you? Code remains, but code answers "what does this do", never "why is it like this", "what did we deliberately not do", "what happens when it breaks at 3 a.m.", or "who do I call". Those answers exist only in writing or not at all.

Writing is decision infrastructure

An unwritten decision gets re-litigated every quarter by whoever joins next. A written decision with its reasoning and its rejected alternatives ends that conversation in ninety seconds, permanently.

Writing scales your attention

You can explain the architecture to four people. A good architecture overview explains it to forty, including people who join next year, without consuming any of your time.

Writing forces real thinking

Vague understanding survives conversation and dies on the page. The act of writing a design doc routinely reveals that you do not know how the failure path works. That discovery is the point.

Writing is how you are evaluated

The people who renew contracts and decide promotions almost never see your code. They see your status reports, design docs and summaries. Fairly or not, that is the sample they judge from.

Writing reduces support load

Every question answered in a doc is a question not asked in a Slack channel at 22:00. The runbook that prevents one incident call pays for itself ten times over.

Writing is the handover

The handover pack is the most commercially valuable artefact an FDE produces. A system that cannot be operated from written material has not been delivered; it has been lent.

The asymmetry that justifies the effortWriting a page well might cost you two hours instead of thirty minutes. That page will be read by thirty people over two years. If the clearer version saves each reader five minutes of confusion, the return is 150 minutes against 90 minutes invested — and that ignores the far larger cost of the decisions made wrongly because the unclear version was misread. Documentation economics almost always favour spending more time than feels natural.

Why engineers write badly, specifically

  1. The curse of knowledge. You cannot un-know the context, so you cannot see which assumptions you have skipped. The fix is mechanical: give the draft to someone outside the project and watch where they stop.
  2. Writing in discovery order. You narrate the journey — investigation, dead ends, eventual conclusion — because that is how you experienced it. Readers need the conclusion first and the journey optional.
  3. Precision anxiety. Fear of being wrong produces hedges: "may", "could potentially", "in certain circumstances". Three hedges in a sentence leave the reader unable to act. Be specific about conditions instead of vague about everything.
  4. Writing to impress rather than to transfer. Complexity signals effort; it does not signal competence. The highest-status writing in engineering is the clearest.
  5. Treating documentation as a phase. Docs written after the work, in a rush, from memory, are bad. Docs written during the work, as the thinking happens, are good and cost less.
  6. No audience model. Writing one document for executives, engineers and operators simultaneously produces something useless to all three.

Audience, Purpose and the Cost of Ambiguity

Before any sentence, two questions: who is reading this, and what do they need to do afterwards? Everything else — length, structure, vocabulary, level of detail — follows deterministically from those answers. Most bad technical documents are not badly written; they are correctly written for a reader who does not exist.

Same facts, three documents

The single most useful exercise in technical writing is taking one set of facts and rendering it for three audiences. Below is the same incident — a data pipeline failure that produced incorrect month-end figures — written three ways. The facts do not change. Everything else does.

Version 1 — for the executive sponsor

TEXT
Subject: Month-end revenue figures were overstated by 2.1% - corrected,
         root cause fixed

The revenue figures in the 3 March month-end pack were overstated by
EUR 412,000 (2.1%). Corrected figures are attached. No external reporting
or customer invoicing used the incorrect numbers.

What happened: a currency-conversion step silently skipped 1,840 GBP
transactions on 1 March because an upstream rate feed returned an empty
response and our job treated that as "no transactions to convert" rather
than as a failure.

Impact: internal management reporting only, for 4 days, between the pack
being issued on 3 March and correction on 7 March. The Q1 board pack had
not yet been produced. Nothing was filed, invoiced or communicated
externally on these numbers.

What we have done: the job now fails loudly rather than continuing when
the rate feed is empty, and a daily reconciliation check compares
transaction counts before and after conversion. Both are live as of
6 March.

What we are still doing: reviewing the other eleven jobs in this pipeline
for the same pattern. Complete by 21 March. I will confirm.

Your decision needed: none. Flagging in case you want to inform the CFO
ahead of the Q1 pack, which I would recommend.

Arun

What changed between the threeThe executive version answers "do I need to act, and is my reputation exposed?" in the first paragraph, and contains no mechanism at all. The engineering version is a timeline plus causes plus tracked fixes with owners and dates, and its most valuable line is the generalised lesson. The operator version is imperative, numbered, has exact commands, a verification step, and an explicit "what if it is already too late" branch. One document attempting all three would fail all three. Writing three documents took about ninety minutes total and each one is shorter than the merged version would have been.

Choosing the level of detail

  • Cut any sentence whose removal does not change what the reader does.
  • If a detail matters only to one reader group, move it to an appendix or a separate document and link it.
  • Give the reader an exit: "if you only need X, stop after section 2."
  • State the thing, then the reasoning — never the reasoning then the thing.
  • Replace any internal-journey narrative ("we first tried…") with a rejected-options section if it matters, or delete it if it does not.
  • Assume the reader will not read the next paragraph, and write accordingly.

BLUF and the Pyramid Principle

Bottom Line Up Front means the conclusion is the first thing on the page, followed by the support that justifies it, followed by the detail that justifies the support. It is the opposite of how investigation feels and the opposite of how academic writing is taught, and it is correct for essentially all professional engineering communication. The reader must be able to stop after any paragraph and still have the most important remaining information.

The pyramid: answer, then the reasons that make it true, then the evidence under each reason. Each level answers "why?" for the level above.

The rules

  1. Lead with the conclusion. The first sentence states the answer, the decision needed, or the thing that happened. Not the context, not the background, not the process.
  2. Group supporting points MECE. Three to five, non-overlapping, collectively covering the case. If you have nine points, you have not found the right grouping.
  3. Each group answers "why?" for the level above. If a point does not support the conclusion, it belongs elsewhere or nowhere.
  4. Order by importance, not chronology. Chronology is for timelines and runbooks only.
  5. Make every heading informative. "Options considered" says nothing; "Managed Postgres costs 30% more but removes the DBA dependency" is itself an argument.
  6. Pass the skim test. Read only the title, headings and first sentence of each paragraph. If that is not a coherent, complete summary, the structure is wrong.

 C. Managed RabbitMQ  [RECOMMENDED]

The Diátaxis Framework

Most documentation is bad not because the sentences are poor but because four fundamentally different kinds of document have been blended into one. Diátaxis (sometimes called the Divio system) separates them by what the reader is doing: learning or working, and studying or applying. Once you internalise the four quadrants, the question "where does this paragraph go?" has an answer instead of being a matter of taste.

Four quadrants, four reader states. A document that tries to occupy two of them serves neither well.

The failure modes of mixing

TEXT
FAILURE 1: TUTORIAL CONTAMINATED WITH EXPLANATION
"Now run `app init`. This uses the Raft consensus protocol under the
 hood, which was chosen because of its understandability compared to
 Paxos. Note that in a multi-region setup you would instead want..."

 WHY IT FAILS: the beginner now has three new concepts, two of which
 are irrelevant to the task, and has lost the thread. Tutorials must
 be ruthlessly linear. Move all of that to an Explanation page and
 link it at the END.

 FIX: "Run `app init`. You should see 'Initialised in ./data'.
       (Curious about how clustering works? See Architecture.)"

FAILURE 2: HOW-TO THAT IS SECRETLY A TUTORIAL
 "How to configure SSO" that begins by explaining what SAML is, then
 sets up a local dev environment, then installs dependencies.

 WHY IT FAILS: the reader already has a running system and a specific
 goal. Making them wade through setup is why they give up and ask you
 in Slack instead.

 FIX: start with prerequisites as a checklist, then go straight to
 the steps that achieve the stated goal.

FAILURE 3: REFERENCE WITH OPINIONS
 "timeout - the request timeout in seconds. We recommend 30 for most
  use cases, although in high-latency environments you may want..."

 WHY IT FAILS: reference must be scannable and complete. Recommendations
 belong in a how-to. Mixed in, they bloat the table and age badly.

 FIX: reference says: "timeout | integer | seconds | default 30 |
      max 300 | applies per attempt, not per request including retries"
      The recommendation goes in "How to tune for high-latency networks".

FAILURE 4: EXPLANATION PRETENDING TO BE REFERENCE
 An "Architecture" page that is actually a narrative of how the system
 evolved, with no diagram and no component list.

 WHY IT FAILS: someone looking for "what talks to what" cannot find it.

 FIX: split. A reference-style component and interface list, plus an
 explanation of why the boundaries are where they are.

FAILURE 5: THE EVERYTHING PAGE
 A single README containing a quickstart, full config reference, design
 rationale, troubleshooting and contribution guidelines, at 4,000 words.

 WHY IT FAILS: nobody can find anything, and nobody maintains it
 because every change touches a 4,000-word file.

 FIX: README is a map plus a quickstart. Everything else is linked.

Sentence-Level Craft

Structure gets a reader to the right paragraph; sentences determine whether they understand it. Technical prose has one job — to transfer a precise idea with minimum resistance — and almost all of the resistance comes from four habits: the passive voice hiding the actor, nominalisations turning verbs into fog, hedges removing all actionable content, and imprecision about numbers and conditions.

TEXT

The ruthless editing checklist

Run this on every document before you publish. It takes ten minutes and typically removes 20–30% of the words.

  • Is the conclusion in the first two sentences?
  • Does every heading say something, rather than naming a category?
  • Read only the headings and first sentences — is that a complete summary?
  • Delete every instance of: basically, essentially, actually, really, quite, very, in order to, it should be noted, there is/are (where a real verb is hiding).
  • Find every "is", "are", "was", "were" — is there a hidden passive or nominalisation?
  • Find every vague quantifier — some, many, most, few, several — and replace with a number or justify it.
  • Find every "should", "may", "could" — is it a real conditional or a hedge?
  • Does every number have a unit, a basis and a source?
  • Does every action have an owner and a date?
  • Are list items grammatically parallel?
  • Is any sentence longer than 35 words? Split it.
  • Is any paragraph longer than 6 lines? Split it or make it a list.
  • Is there a sentence whose removal would not change what the reader does? Remove it.
  • Does any acronym appear before it is expanded?
  • Would someone outside this project understand the first paragraph?
The 30% ruleWrite the draft, then cut 30% without losing information. It is almost always possible, and the result is almost always better. The cuts come from hedges, throat-clearing openings, restatements of the previous paragraph, and sentences that explain something the reader already understood. If you cannot find 30%, you have probably under-written rather than over-written — which is a different and rarer problem.

Structuring a Document

Readers do not read documents; they scan them, drop into the part that looks relevant, and read a few paragraphs. Structure is therefore not decoration but navigation, and a document with uninformative headings and uniform paragraph blocks is functionally unreadable regardless of the quality of its prose.

Headings that carry information

Category heading (weak)Informative heading (strong)

OptionsThree ways to decouple the warehouse call
Option 3Option 3: managed RabbitMQ — 6 days, no new vendor

ConclusionRecommendation: RabbitMQ, starting 14 March

The table-of-contents testGenerate the TOC from your headings and read it on its own. If it reads as a coherent argument or a complete map, the structure works. If it reads as a list of nouns — Background, Scope, Approach, Options, Risks, Conclusion — you have a document whose shape conveys nothing, and every reader will have to read the body to find out what you are proposing.

Chunking and progressive disclosure

  1. One idea per paragraph. If a paragraph contains two ideas, the second one is invisible. Split it.
  2. Lead each paragraph with its point. The first sentence is the claim; the rest supports it. This makes the document skimmable by first sentences alone.
  3. Summary first, detail behind a link or a fold. A reader who needs the detail will click; a reader who does not should never have to scroll past it.
  4. Use collapsible sections for reference material inside a narrative document. Full config dumps, long error lists, SQL — present only when wanted.
  5. Put the thing people need most at the top, even if it is logically last. Document order is a service to the reader, not a reflection of the problem's structure.
  6. Keep nesting to three levels. A fourth level of heading means the document should be split.

If you find yourself writing nested bullets three levels deep, you are writing an outline of a document you have not written. Scannability mechanics

  • Bold the two or three phrases per page that a skimmer must not miss — and no more, or bolding stops meaning anything.
  • Keep paragraphs to 3–6 lines on screen; whitespace is a navigation aid, not wasted space.
  • Put numbers in tables, not in sentences, when there are more than three of them.
  • Use a consistent vocabulary: pick "job" or "task" and never alternate, even for variety. Elegant variation is a bug in technical prose.
  • Front-load list items with the distinguishing word: "Timeout errors — …" not "If you see an error relating to timeouts, …".
  • Never make the reader scroll back to understand a sentence; repeat a short definition rather than relying on memory.
  • Give every document a stable anchor-able heading structure so people can link to a specific section in chat.

Architecture Decision Records

An ADR captures one decision, its context, and its consequences, at the moment it is made. It exists because the most expensive missing information in any inherited system is why — why this database, why this boundary, why this odd-looking constraint that everyone is now afraid to remove. An FDE who leaves thirty ADRs behind has given the customer something more durable than the code.

Why ADRs, specifically

They stop re-litigation

Without a record, every new senior engineer re-opens the same three debates. With one, the conversation is ninety seconds: here is what we knew, here is what we chose, here is what would change the answer.

They capture what was rejected

The rejected options are usually more valuable than the chosen one, because they encode the constraints. "We rejected Kafka because we needed work-queue semantics, not a log" is a sentence that saves a future team two weeks.

They are cheap

A good ADR is one page and takes twenty minutes while the decision is fresh. Reconstructing the same reasoning a year later takes a day and produces a worse result.

They are honest about uncertainty

An ADR can say "we are not sure; we chose the reversible option." That is impossible to express in code and invaluable to the person who inherits it.

They date the context

Decisions are correct relative to what was known at a point in time. An ADR makes that explicit, so a later reversal is an update rather than an accusation.

They survive you

When the FDE leaves and the two engineers who were in the room move on, the ADR directory is the only remaining record of the system's reasoning.

# ADR-0014: Use a managed message queue for warehouse integration

- Status: Accepted
- Date: 2026-03-11
- Deciders: A. Krishnan (FDE), T. Berg (enterprise architect), S. Ali
- Consulted: platform team, warehouse ops lead
- Supersedes: -
- Superseded by: -

## Context

The Order API calls the warehouse system synchronously inside the
customer request. Measured over 90 days:

- Warehouse call p95 is 2.4 s, p99 is 11 s
- Order API p99 is 12.3 s against a 2 s target; the warehouse call is
  89% of it
- 34 orders were written to our database and then failed at the
  warehouse step, leaving them in a state no process owns
- The warehouse vendor's Sunday maintenance window (02:00-06:00) takes
  our order API down with it

Constraints at the time of this decision:

- The platform team already operates a managed RabbitMQ cluster, which
  is on the security-approved list
- We have 6 weeks remaining in the engagement
- The customer has no Kafka operational experience and no appetite to
  acquire it during this engagement
- Eventual consistency of up to ~60 s is acceptable to warehouse ops,
  confirmed by M. Hoeffner on 2026-03-06 (their pick waves run every
  15 minutes)

## Decision

Publish an `OrderPlaced` message to the existing managed RabbitMQ
cluster from the Order API, and handle the warehouse call in a separate
consumer with retries and a dead-letter queue.

## Options considered

### A. Thread pool and in-process retry
2 days. Rejected: messages are lost on pod restart, which is exactly
the failure mode described in the context. It would make the system
look more reliable while leaving the correctness problem intact.

### B. Database-backed outbox with a poller
4 days, no new infrastructure. Genuinely viable. Rejected because we
would be implementing queue semantics ourselves - visibility timeouts,
dead-lettering, poison-message handling, backpressure - and the
platform team already operates a queue that provides them.

If RabbitMQ were not already available, this would have been the choice.

### C. Managed RabbitMQ  [CHOSEN]
6 days. Durable, already operated and monitored by the platform team,
already security-approved, and the team has existing runbooks for it.

### D. Kafka
Rejected. ~3 weeks including operational onboarding. We need work-queue
semantics with competing consumers, not an ordered replayable log.
Neither ordering across partitions nor replay is a requirement. The
operational cost is not justified by any requirement we have.

## Consequences

Positive:
- Order API p99 expected to drop from 12.3 s to ~320 ms
- Warehouse outages no longer affect order acceptance
- Failed warehouse calls land in a DLQ that can be inspected and
  replayed, rather than vanishing

Negative:
- The warehouse may not know about an order for up to ~60 s, and longer
  during an outage. Order status in the UI must reflect this; see
  ADR-0015.
- The DLQ needs an owner, an alert and a runbook. Without these it
  becomes a silent data loss mechanism. Covered by runbook RB-009.
- One more piece of infrastructure in the request path, albeit one the
  customer already runs.

Neutral:
- Testing becomes asynchronous, which requires a different integration
  test approach (see test/integration/README.md).

## What would change this decision

- If message volume exceeded ~5,000/s sustained, or if replay of the
  full order history became a requirement, Kafka would be worth
  re-evaluating.
- If the platform team stopped operating RabbitMQ, option B becomes the
  fallback and is about 4 days of work.

# ADR-0021: Do not build a customer-facing status portal

- Status: Accepted
- Date: 2026-04-02
- Deciders: P. Menon (sponsor), A. Krishnan
- Consulted: CS team lead, 3 CS agents, 2 key-account managers

## Context

Customers email to ask "where is my order?" - 4,210 messages in the
Jan 2026 baseline, 31% of all inbound volume and 21% of agent minutes.
The obvious response is a self-service status portal.

Before building one we checked three things:

1. **Do customers have the information to use a portal?** We sampled
   200 "where is my order" emails. 71% did not quote an order number;
   they identified the order by PO number, by product description or by
   "the one we discussed on Tuesday". A portal keyed on order number
   serves at most 29% of these.

2. **Do these customers want a portal?** We asked eight accounts,
   including both key accounts. Six said they would not use one -
   several explicitly said they email precisely because they want a
   human to confirm. Two said they would use it.

3. **Does one already exist?** Yes. The vendor-supplied customer portal
   has an order status page. Usage over 90 days: 11 distinct customers
   out of 340 active accounts.

## Decision

We will not build a customer-facing status portal.

Instead we will auto-respond to "where is my order" messages with the
current status, resolved from the PO number or the sender's recent
orders where the order number is absent, with a human in the loop for
anything ambiguous. That is tracked as a separate proposal.

## Options considered

### A. Build a new status portal

The most valuable ADRs are the ones that say noA directory full of "we chose X" records is useful. A directory that also contains "we deliberately did not build Y, and here is the evidence" is transformative, because the default assumption about any missing capability is that nobody thought of it. Keeping ADRs alive

  1. Store them in the repository, as numbered markdown files in docs/adr/, reviewed in pull requests like code. ADRs in a wiki rot; ADRs in the repo get reviewed by people who care.
  2. Never edit an accepted ADR's substance. Write a new one that supersedes it, and add a "Superseded by ADR-00NN" line to the old one. The historical record is the point.
  3. Write them at the moment of decision, not retrospectively. A retrospective ADR documents the rationalisation, not the reasoning.
  4. Keep them to one page. If an ADR needs five pages, the decision is actually several decisions and should be split.
  5. Include a "what would change this" section. It converts a historical record into a trigger that someone can watch for.
  6. Link them from the code. A comment saying // see ADR-0014 above an unusual-looking construct is the cheapest possible documentation of a surprise.
  7. Review the index at handover. Walk the customer's team through the ten most consequential ADRs in person; it is the fastest way to transfer the system's reasoning.

Design Documents and RFCs

A design document exists to get a decision made before code is written, and to make the reasoning reviewable by people who will not read the code. For an FDE it has a second function: it is the artefact that gets you through the customer's architecture and security gates, and writing it in the shape their reviewers expect is the difference between conditional approval in week two and rejection in week eight.

# Design: asynchronous warehouse integration for the order pipeline

- Status: In review (decision needed by 2026-03-11)
- Author: A. Krishnan
- Reviewers: T. Berg (architecture), K. Frey (security), S. Ali
- Related: ADR-0014, RB-009, ticket NSD-412

## TL;DR

Move the warehouse call out of the synchronous order path and behind
the platform team's existing managed RabbitMQ. 6 days of work. Expected
Order API p99 drops from 12.3 s to ~320 ms, and the 34-orders-in-90-days
inconsistency class disappears. One decision needed: approval to use the
shared RabbitMQ cluster for customer-order traffic.

## Problem

### What is happening

The Order API performs four steps inside the customer request:
validate, write to the database, notify, then call the warehouse. The
warehouse call is slow and unreliable, and it is the last step.

| Metric | Current | Target |
|---|---|---|
| Order API p50 | 410 ms | 250 ms |
| Order API p95 | 3.1 s | 500 ms |
| Order API p99 | 12.3 s | 2 s |
| Warehouse call p99 | 11 s | n/a (moves off path) |
| Orders stranded in 90 days | 34 | 0 |

### Why it matters

- The customer charter commits to a 2 s order confirmation. We breach
  it on roughly 4% of orders.
- 34 orders in 90 days were committed to our database and then failed
  at the warehouse step. Each required manual reconciliation by the
  order management team, taking roughly 25 minutes each.
- The warehouse vendor's Sunday maintenance window takes our order
  acceptance down with it. This has caused two Monday-morning backlogs
  in the last quarter.

### Why now

The order volume forecast for Q3 is +22%. At current latencies the p99
breach rate grows super-linearly because the warehouse call queues
behind itself under load. We measured this in a load test on 2026-03-04
(results in appendix A).

## Goals

1. Order acceptance is not blocked by warehouse availability or latency.
2. No order is ever committed to our database without a guaranteed,
   observable path to the warehouse.
3. Order API p99 under 2 s at 2x current peak load.
4. No new vendor, no new operational burden on the customer's team.

## Non-goals

- We are not changing the warehouse API or anything on the vendor side.
- We are not introducing event sourcing or a general eventing platform.
  This is one integration, not a platform migration.
- We are not changing the order data model.
- We are not addressing the notification service's latency (separate,
  smaller problem - ticket NSD-418).

## Proposed design

### Flow

1. Order API validates and writes the order with `status = ACCEPTED`.
2. In the same database transaction, it writes a row to an `outbox`
   table.
3. A relay publishes outbox rows to the `orders` exchange and marks them
   sent. This gives us atomicity between the order write and the
   intent to publish.
4. A warehouse consumer reads `OrderPlaced`, calls the warehouse API,
   and updates `status = CONFIRMED`.
5. Failures retry with exponential backoff; after 5 attempts the message
   goes to `orders.dlq` and an alert fires.

### Why outbox rather than publishing directly

Publishing directly from the API after the database commit introduces a
window where the order exists and the message was never published - the
same class of bug we are trying to remove, just smaller. The outbox
makes the publish intent part of the transaction. Cost: one extra table
and a relay process, roughly a day.

### Message schema

```json
{
  "message_id": "01HQ8F4K2N9VZ...",
  "type": "OrderPlaced",
  "version": 1,
  "occurred_at": "2026-03-14T09:14:22.113Z",
  "order_id": "ORD-2026-0038412",
  "customer_id": "CUST-00291",
  "ship_to": { "...": "..." },
  "lines": [ { "sku": "...", "qty": 4 } ]
}
```

`message_id` is used for idempotency on the consumer side; the warehouse
API is not idempotent, so we maintain a `processed_messages` table and
skip duplicates.

### Failure behaviour

| Failure | Behaviour | Observable as |
|---|---|---|
| RabbitMQ unavailable at publish | Outbox row stays unsent; relay retries every 5 s | `outbox_unsent_age_seconds` gauge |
| Warehouse API 5xx | Retry 5x with backoff (1, 4, 16, 64, 256 s) | `warehouse_retry_total` counter |
| Warehouse API 4xx | No retry; straight to DLQ | `orders_dlq_depth` gauge, alert at > 0 |
| Consumer crash mid-call | Message redelivered; `processed_messages` prevents a duplicate warehouse call | `duplicate_skipped_total` |
| Poison message | 5 failures then DLQ; never blocks the queue | DLQ alert |

## Alternatives considered

See ADR-0014 for the full comparison. Summary: in-process thread pool
(loses messages on restart), database outbox with our own poller
(reimplements queue semantics we already have), Kafka (3 weeks, wrong
semantics for a work queue).

# RB-009: orders dead-letter queue is non-empty

## Trigger

Alert `orders_dlq_depth_nonzero` (Grafana / PagerDuty service
`data-platform`). Fires when `orders.dlq` has one or more messages for
more than 5 minutes.

## Severity

P2. Orders are accepted but not reaching the warehouse. Not customer-
visible immediately; becomes visible when a delivery is missed, which
is typically the next pick wave (every 15 minutes, 06:00-20:00).

## Impact

Each message in the DLQ is one order that the warehouse does not know
about. The customer has received an order confirmation.

## Preconditions

- `kubectl` access to namespace `orders` (role `orders-operator`)
- RabbitMQ management UI access: https://rabbit.internal/#/queues
- Read access to the `orders` Postgres database
- If you do not have these, escalate immediately - do not spend time
  requesting access during an incident.

## Diagnosis

**1. How many messages, and are they still arriving?**

```bash
kubectl exec -n orders deploy/rabbit-tools -- \
  rabbitmqadmin list queues name messages --vhost=orders
```

- `orders.dlq` = 1-5 and stable &rarr; likely a single bad message. Go to 2.
- `orders.dlq` growing &rarr; systemic. Go to 3.

**2. Inspect the messages (does not consume them)**

```bash
kubectl exec -n orders deploy/rabbit-tools -- \
  rabbitmqadmin get queue=orders.dlq count=5 ackmode=reject_requeue_true
```

Look at `x-death` in the headers for the rejection reason.

- `reason: rejected` with HTTP 400 from the warehouse &rarr; bad payload.
  Go to remediation A.
- `reason: expired` &rarr; the message sat longer than the 24 h TTL. The
  warehouse was down for a long period. Go to remediation B.

**3. Is the warehouse API up?**

```bash
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code} %{time_total}s\n" \
  https://warehouse.vendor.example/api/health
```

- Non-200 or no response &rarr; vendor outage. Go to remediation B and
  notify warehouse ops (see Afterwards).
- 200 &rarr; our consumer is failing. Check consumer logs:

```bash
kubectl logs -n orders deploy/warehouse-consumer --tail=200 | grep ERROR
```

## Remediation

### A. Single bad payload

1. Identify the order id from the message body.
2. Check the order in the database:

   ```sql
   SELECT order_id, status, created_at, ship_to_country
   FROM orders WHERE order_id = 'ORD-...';
   ```

3. If `ship_to_country` is null or not a 2-letter ISO code, this is the
   known defect in issue #211. Fix the record:

   ```sql
   UPDATE orders SET ship_to_country = 'DE'
   WHERE order_id = 'ORD-...' AND ship_to_country IS NULL;
   ```

   Only do this if you can confirm the country from the ship-to address.
   If you cannot, escalate - do not guess.

4. Replay the message:

   ```bash
   kubectl exec -n orders deploy/rabbit-tools -- \
     rabbitmqadmin publish exchange=orders routing_key=order.placed \
     payload="$(cat /tmp/message.json)"
   ```

### B. Warehouse outage - bulk replay after recovery

1. Confirm the warehouse is healthy (diagnosis step 3 returns 200).
2. Replay the whole DLQ:

   ```bash
   kubectl create job -n orders --from=cronjob/dlq-replay dlq-replay-manual
   kubectl logs -n orders job/dlq-replay-manual -f
   ```

3. The job replays at 10 messages/second to avoid overwhelming a
   just-recovered warehouse. For more than 500 messages this takes
   over a minute; let it finish.

## Verification

All three must be true:

1. DLQ is empty:

   ```bash
   kubectl exec -n orders deploy/rabbit-tools -- \
     rabbitmqadmin list queues name messages --vhost=orders
   ```

2. No orders stuck in ACCEPTED:

   ```sql
   SELECT count(*) FROM orders
   WHERE status = 'ACCEPTED' AND created_at < now() - interval '10 minutes';
   ```

   Expected: 0.

3. The alert has cleared in Grafana (allow 5 minutes).

## Rollback

Replaying is idempotent - the consumer checks `processed_messages`
before calling the warehouse, so a duplicate replay does not create a
duplicate shipment. There is nothing to roll back.

If a replay causes the main queue to back up, pause the replay job:

```bash
kubectl delete job -n orders dlq-replay-manual
```

Messages remain in the DLQ; nothing is lost.

## Escalation

1. Data on-call (PagerDuty `data-platform`) - you are here
2. After 30 minutes unresolved: S. Ali, +49 ... (Signal preferred)
3. After 60 minutes, or any suspicion of duplicate shipments:
   M. Hoeffner, order management, +49 ... - he can halt the pick wave
4. Vendor outage lasting over 2 hours: warehouse ops lead, who owns the
   vendor relationship

## Afterwards

- Record the incident in the incident log with the DLQ count and cause.
- If the cause was a bad payload, add it to issue #211 with the order id.
- If more than 20 messages were affected, or any order was delayed past
  its pick wave, tell M. Hoeffner by 08:00 so customer service can get
  ahead of the calls.
- Postmortem required if: duration over 1 hour, more than 50 orders, or
  any customer-visible delivery impact.

common cause by far is a null `ship_to_country`, which the warehouse API
rejects with HTTP 400; this originates from the Hamburg SFTP source file

for the April release). The second cause is a warehouse outage lasting

Labelling conventions

  • Label every arrow with the interaction and, where useful, the protocol: "publishes OrderPlaced (AMQP)", not a bare line.
  • State direction unambiguously. If the arrow means "calls", say so; if it means "data flows", say that instead — they are often opposite.
  • Put the technology on each box: "Postgres 16", "Python/FastAPI". A future reader needs it and it costs four characters.
  • Mark trust boundaries and anything that leaves your control, explicitly. Security reviewers look for this first.
  • Use the same name for a component as the repository, the Kubernetes deployment and the dashboard. Synonyms across artefacts cost hours.
  • Date the diagram and name its owner, in the caption or the file header.
  • Write a caption that states the diagram's claim, not its subject: "Order acceptance does not depend on warehouse availability" beats "Order pipeline architecture".

# Migrating from v1 to v2 of the Order API

**Who this affects:** anyone calling `POST /v1/orders` or
`GET /v1/orders`. If you only consume webhooks, no action is needed.

**Deadline:** v1 is supported until **2027-09-30**. After that date v1
endpoints return HTTP 410.

**Estimated effort:** 2-4 hours for a typical integration.

## Summary of breaking changes

| # | Change | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `total` is now an object, not a string | Read `total.amount` and `total.currency` |
| 2 | `lines[].unit_price` removed | Use `lines[].price.amount` |
| 3 | `status` values renamed | Map old to new (table below) |
| 4 | `GET /orders` is cursor-paginated | Replace offset paging |

## 1. Money is now an object

**Before**
```json
{ "total": "966.40", "currency": "EUR" }
```

**After**
```json
{ "total": { "amount": "966.40", "currency": "EUR" } }
```

**Why:** line items and order totals could have different currencies in
multi-currency accounts, and a single top-level `currency` field could
not express that.

**Do this:** replace `order.total` with `order.total.amount` and
`order.currency` with `order.total.currency`. The string format of
`amount` is unchanged - a decimal string, never a float.

## 3. Status values renamed

| v1 value | v2 value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| `PENDING` | `ACCEPTED` | Same meaning |
| `PROCESSING` | `CONFIRMED` | Same meaning |
| `COMPLETE` | `DELIVERED` | Same meaning |
| `ERROR` | `FAILED` | Same meaning |
| - | `CANCELLED` | **New.** Previously a cancelled order was `ERROR`. If you treat `ERROR` as "retry", you must now exclude `CANCELLED`. |

## How to migrate safely

1. **Run both in parallel.** v1 and v2 serve the same orders; you can
   call v2 read endpoints against existing orders today.
2. **Compare for one week.** Call both, log any differences. We provide
   a comparison helper: `https://docs.../tools/v1v2-diff`.
3. **Switch writes last.** Move `GET` calls to v2 first; they are
   read-only and reversible.
4. **Tell us when you have finished** so we can stop sending reminders:
   reply to the migration email or set `migrated: true` in the portal.

Localisation considerations for an FDE

  • UI strings live in resource files from day one, even if there is only one language today. Retrofitting is five times the work.
  • Never concatenate: "Deleted " + n + " orders" breaks in languages with different plural rules and word order. Use a full parameterised template per plural form.
  • Dates: ISO-8601 in logs, APIs and anything machine-read; locale-formatted only in user-facing output. "03/04/2026" is two different days depending on the reader.
  • Numbers: decimal comma versus point, and thousands separators, differ across Europe. Render per locale; store and transmit unambiguously.
  • Names and addresses: do not assume a first name, a last name, a postcode format, or that a state field exists.
  • Allow for text expansion: German runs 30% longer than English, so fixed-width labels will truncate.
  • Timezones: store UTC, display local, and always label which you are showing. "02:00" with no zone has caused more incidents than any single bug class.

Writing with AI Assistance

Language models are excellent at structure, tedious transformation and first drafts, and unreliable at anything requiring knowledge of your specific system. Used well they roughly double writing throughput; used badly they produce fluent, confident, plausible documentation that is wrong in ways reviewers do not catch because it reads so well. The discipline is knowing exactly which parts of the job can be delegated.

What to delegate and what never to

Safe to delegate

Restructuring content you wrote into BLUF order. Generating a first draft from your bullet points. Converting a design doc into an ADR. Producing a table from prose. Tightening sentences. Generating a glossary from a document. Drafting alternative phrasings of a difficult paragraph. Writing the boring parts of an OpenAPI description once the schema exists. Suggesting what a reviewer might object to.

Never delegate

Any factual claim about your system — numbers, field names, endpoints, behaviour, limits. Root-cause analysis in an incident. Security or data-protection statements. Commitments, dates, estimates. The judgement of what to include or omit. Anything that goes to a customer unreviewed. Nuanced interpersonal communication, where generic warmth reads as insincerity.

The review loop

  1. You supply the facts; the model supplies the shape. Never ask for content you have not provided in some form. The generation step should be a transformation, not a retrieval.
  2. Require an explicit gaps list. "List anything you needed and did not have" converts silent invention into a visible to-do.
  3. Verify every specific. Field names against the schema, commands against a shell, numbers against the source, links by clicking them. Fluency is not evidence.
  4. Read it aloud for voice. Model prose has tells: "robust", "seamless", "leverage", "it's important to note that", tricolons everywhere, and a relentless evenness of tone. Edit them out or your documentation stops sounding like a person who knows the system.
  5. Cut 30% after generation. Models over-explain and restate. The editing checklist applies more strictly to generated text, not less.
  6. Never paste customer data into a tool that is not covered by the agreement. This is a contractual and often legal matter, not a preference. Check before the first use, not after.
The specific failure mode to watch forA model will produce a beautifully formatted runbook with an entirely plausible kubectl command that references a namespace which does not exist, a flag that was removed two versions ago, or a metric name it inferred from context. It reads exactly like the nine correct commands around it. Reviewers skim it because the document is well structured. At 3 a.m. the operator runs it and it fails, or worse, succeeds against the wrong thing. Every command in generated operational documentation must be executed once, by you, before it ships.

Docs as a Product

Documentation that nobody owns, nobody reviews and nobody measures decays into a liability — an archive of confidently wrong statements that new joiners trust. Treating docs as a product means they have an owner, a review process, a quality bar enforced by tooling, a release process, and a deletion process. The last of those is the one almost everyone skips.

Killing stale documentation

  1. Put a review date and an owner in the front matter of every page. No owner means no page.
  2. Run a quarterly report of pages past their review date and pages whose code has changed since the doc did. Both are candidates.
  3. Default to deletion, not revision. The question is "would we write this today?", not "is anything in it still true". Most stale pages should go.
  4. Archive rather than agonise. Move it to an archive/ directory with a banner stating the date it was last known accurate and that it is unmaintained. Nothing is lost and nobody is misled.
  5. Make deletion part of the fix. When a failure mode is designed out, deleting its runbook belongs in the definition of done for that ticket.
  6. Never leave a page that is half right. A page with three accurate sections and one that is silently wrong is more dangerous than no page, because the accurate parts earn trust that the wrong part then exploits.

Interview Questions

Writing questions in engineering interviews are usually probing for three things: whether you have an audience model, whether you understand that documentation is a product with maintenance costs, and whether you have actually been responsible for knowledge surviving your departure. Answer with structure, a specific artefact and a measurable test.

How do you decide what to document and what to leave out?
I start from the reader and what they must do afterwards, because that determines everything else. Concretely I ask two questions: who reads this, and what decision or action follows? If I cannot name a reader, I do not write the document. Then I apply a filter — would removing this sentence change what the reader does? If not, it goes. The things I always document regardless of appetite are the ones that cannot be recovered from code: why a decision was made and what was rejected, what the system deliberately does not do, how to operate it when it fails, and what breaks if a specific person is unavailable. The things I deliberately do not document are anything the code expresses better, anything that will drift faster than it will be read — component-level internals are the classic example — and anything I would be writing for completeness rather than for a reader. I also use the six-week test continuously during an engagement: if I were unreachable for six weeks starting tomorrow, what would break and who would be stuck? Whatever I answer is the next thing I write, which means the handover pack assembles itself rather than being improvised in the final week.
Explain the Diátaxis framework and why it matters.
It separates documentation into four types along two axes — whether the reader is learning or working, and whether they need theoretical knowledge or practical steps. That yields tutorials for beginners learning by doing, how-to guides for competent people with a specific goal, reference for people who need a fact while working, and explanation for people who want to understand why. It matters because most bad documentation is not badly written; it is two or three of these blended into one file, which fails all of them. A tutorial that pauses to explain the consensus protocol loses the beginner. A how-to that starts by installing a dev environment wastes the time of someone who already has a running system. Reference with recommendations embedded in it becomes unscannable and ages badly. The practical value is that "where does this paragraph go?" stops being a matter of taste and becomes answerable: ask whether the reader is learning or working and whether they are acquiring or applying knowledge, and you get a quadrant. In my own work the most common correction is pulling explanation out of runbooks — the background about why a failure happens belongs at the very bottom, because nobody reads it at 3 a.m. and everybody reads it at 10 a.m.
What makes a good runbook?
A runbook is written for one specific reader: someone woken at 3 a.m., who did not build the system, who is stressed, and who needs to restore service before understanding anything. Everything follows from that. It opens with the trigger — matching the alert name exactly so it can be linked from the page — then severity and impact in one line each, then preconditions, because discovering at 3 a.m. that you lack a Kubernetes role is fatal. Then branching numbered diagnosis where each check is one command with an expected result and an explicit "if X go to Y", then remediation with exact runnable commands and no placeholders, then verification, rollback, escalation with named people in order, and what to record afterwards. Background goes last, never first. The three sins I watch for are explaining before acting, placeholders like a bracketed namespace that the reader cannot fill in, and a missing verification step — a runbook ending at "restart the service" leaves the operator unable to tell whether they fixed it or moved it. The way I validate one is a game day: cause the failure in a test environment, hand the runbook to someone who has never done the procedure, and sit silently recording every hesitation. Every hesitation is an edit. An untested runbook is a hypothesis about stressed humans, and those hypotheses are usually wrong.
Walk me through how you would write a design document for a significant change.
I lead with a TL;DR that states the recommendation, the effort and the single decision I need, because the sponsor may read nothing else. Then the problem with measured numbers — current versus target in a table, and specifically why it matters now, which is usually a forecast, a date or a breach of something they committed to. Then goals and, importantly, non-goals, which prevent the document being read as a platform migration when it is one integration. Then the proposed design, including a failure-behaviour table: for each failure, what happens, and what it is observable as. Then alternatives considered with the reason each was rejected, which is the section future readers value most. Then risks with mitigations, a security and data-protection section, a staged rollout with the rollback for each stage, and an explicit "how we will know it worked" with the metric and measurement window. Finally open questions with owners and dates. The thing I do that most changes outcomes is asking each gatekeeper up front what their review criteria are and then answering those criteria item by item, in their wording, in an appendix. Reviewers approve documents that are easy to review, and almost nobody does this — it converts multi-round rejections into conditional approvals.
How do you write for an executive versus for an engineer?
Same facts, different documents, and I write them separately rather than trying to serve both. The executive has about ninety seconds and needs to know whether to act and whether they are exposed, so the first paragraph is the conclusion, the number and the decision required, with no mechanism at all. For an incident I would write "revenue figures were overstated by €412,000, corrected, nothing external used them, root cause fixed, no decision needed from you — flagging in case you want to brief the CFO." The engineering version of the same incident is a timeline with UTC timestamps, the three compounding causes, the fixes with owners and dates, and the generalised lesson, because that audience needs to prevent the class of bug rather than assess exposure. The operator version is different again: an alert-triggered runbook with imperative numbered steps, exact queries, a verification check and a branch for "what if month-end has already run". Attempting one document for all three produces something useless to each. The counter-intuitive part is that writing three documents is faster than writing one merged one, because each is shorter and the structure tells you what belongs where.
How do you stop documentation going stale?
Four mechanisms, and the last one is the one teams skip. First, docs live in the repository next to the code, reviewed in pull requests, so that changing behaviour and changing its description is one commit and one review. Second, every page has front matter with an owner and a review date, which makes staleness measurable — I run a quarterly report of pages past their review date and pages whose code has changed since the doc did. Third, automation in CI: link checking, a prose linter, building with strict mode so broken references fail, and executing tagged code samples so that a command that stopped working fails the build rather than failing an operator. Fourth, and most importantly, deletion. The default question for a stale page is "would we write this today?", not "is anything still true", and most stale pages should simply go. When a failure mode is designed out, deleting its runbook belongs in the definition of done for that ticket. I archive rather than agonise — move it with a banner stating the date it was last verified and that it is unmaintained — because a page that is three-quarters right is more dangerous than no page: the accurate parts earn trust that the wrong part then exploits.
What goes in an ADR, and why bother?
One page: context including the constraints that applied at the time, the decision, the options considered with the reason each was rejected, the consequences including the negative ones, and a section on what would change this decision. The reason to bother is that the most expensive missing information in any inherited system is why — why this database, why this boundary, why this odd constraint everyone is now afraid to remove. Without a record, every new senior engineer re-opens the same three debates. With one, the conversation takes ninety seconds. The rejected options are usually more valuable than the chosen one, because they encode the constraints: "we rejected Kafka because we needed work-queue semantics, not a log, and neither ordering nor replay is a requirement" saves a future team two weeks. The most valuable ADRs are the ones that record a no — a deliberate decision not to build something, with the evidence — because the default assumption about any missing capability is that nobody thought of it. Operationally: store them numbered in the repo, never edit an accepted one, write a superseding one instead, keep them to a page, and reference them from the code with a comment above the construct they explain.
How do you get people to actually read what you write?
Mostly by accepting that they will not read it and designing for that. The conclusion goes in the first two sentences, so the ninety-second reader gets the most important thing. Headings are informative rather than categorical — "Recommendation: RabbitMQ, 6 days, no new vendor" rather than "Conclusion" — so that the table of contents alone is a summary. I apply the skim test: read only the title, headings and first sentence of each paragraph, and if that is not a coherent complete summary, the structure is wrong. I put the ask above the fold with a name and a date on it, because a document with no clear ask gets filed. I keep paragraphs to three to six lines and put more than three numbers in a table rather than a sentence. For email specifically, the subject line states topic, status and whether action is needed, because people triage on a phone. And I give readers an exit — "if you only need the decision, stop after section 2" — which sounds like it reduces readership and actually increases it, because people trust a document that respects their time and come back to it.
Tell me about documentation you wrote that failed.
I wrote a handover pack for a data pipeline that was, by any document-quality measure, good: architecture, environments, runbooks, data lineage, support model. Three weeks after I left, the team called me because an overnight job had failed and nobody could restart it. The runbook said to re-run the job, which was correct, but it assumed a Kubernetes role that only two people had and that I had not listed anywhere, because I had that access and never thought about it. The failure was that I had tested the document by reading it rather than by watching someone use it. What I changed permanently is the acceptance test: I now pick an operator who was not on the project, pick three realistic scenarios including at least one failure, and sit in the room silently while they work through them using only the written material, with a second person recording every hesitation. Every hesitation is a documentation defect and I fix it in the reader's own words — if they said "I didn't know where the vault path was", the fix is the vault path in that step, not a general improvement to the credentials section. I also now put that behavioural test into the statement of work as the acceptance criterion, phrased as their operator succeeding rather than my document existing, which makes the deliverable their capability rather than my prose.
How do you use AI tools for writing without losing accuracy?
I treat it as a transformation tool, not a retrieval tool: I supply the facts and it supplies the shape. So I will give it my OpenAPI spec and three real captured request-response pairs and ask for a getting-started page in a specified structure, with an explicit instruction not to invent any field, status code or behaviour that is not in the material, and to list anything it needed and did not have under a MISSING heading. That last instruction is the important one, because it converts silent invention into a visible to-do list. What I never delegate is any factual claim about the system, root-cause analysis during an incident, security or data-protection statements, commitments and dates, and the judgement of what to include or omit. The specific failure mode I guard against is a beautifully formatted runbook containing a plausible command with a namespace that does not exist or a flag removed two versions ago — it reads exactly like the correct commands around it, reviewers skim it because the document is well structured, and it fails at 3 a.m. So every command in generated operational documentation gets executed once by me before it ships. I also edit for voice, because model prose has tells — "robust", "seamless", "it's important to note that", every paragraph the same length — and documentation that does not sound like a person who knows the system is trusted less, correctly.
What is the most valuable thing you can leave behind at the end of an engagement?
The handover pack, and specifically the parts of it that are uncomfortable to write. Architecture and runbooks are table stakes. The sections that actually determine whether the customer succeeds without me are the credentials and access inventory — which answers "my account is disabled tomorrow, what stops working?" explicitly — the known-issues list that distinguishes "we decided this is fine" from "we ran out of time", the deferred-work register with honest estimates including the items that embarrass me, the "what is not monitored" subsection, and a section I call "how to make the three most likely changes", where I predict what they will need to change first and write the how-to before they know they need it. That last one is what most distinguishes a good handover, because I know what they will hit and they do not yet. Underneath all of it, the deferred-work register is the artefact that converts "the consultants left a mess" into "here is a costed backlog we agreed together", which is a completely different conversation and the one that gets you invited back. And the test of the whole thing is behavioural, not documentary: their own team demonstrating the system to another part of the business, correctly, without me present.
How would you improve the documentation of a system you have just joined?
I would start by measuring rather than rewriting, because the instinct to immediately restructure everything produces a beautiful set of documents that solve the previous author's problems rather than the readers'. First, the newcomer test on myself: I follow the README from a clean machine, time it, and write down every point where I had to ask someone or open another file. That list is the first backlog and it is free. Second, I start a question log — every question anyone asks me about the system, pasted verbatim with a date, before I answer it — and review it monthly; repeated questions are documentation defects with the reader's own wording attached, which tells me both where the answer belongs and what to call it. Third, a staleness audit: compare the last-modified date of each doc against the code it describes, and default to deleting rather than revising anything that would not be written today. Fourth, I check whether the four document types are blended — usually the README is doing the job of reference, tutorial and explanation at once — and split rather than polish. Only then would I write anything new, and the first new thing would be whatever the six-week test says: if the person who currently holds the context were unavailable for six weeks, what breaks? That is always a more valuable page than the one I would have written from instinct.

Writing Drills and a 30-Day Improvement Plan

Writing improves through repetition with feedback, not through reading about writing. The drills below take between ten and sixty minutes, use work you already have, and each produces an artefact you can show someone. The feedback loop matters more than the volume: a page rewritten after watching someone misread it teaches more than ten pages written alone.

Weekly drills

  1. Three audiences. Take one thing that happened this week and write it three ways: for an executive in 90 seconds, for an engineer who must prevent recurrence, for an operator who must handle it next time. Compare what survived into each.
  2. The skim test. Take your longest recent document. Read only the title, headings and first sentences. If it is not a coherent summary, rewrite the headings to be informative and reorder.
  3. Write one ADR for a decision already made in your current work, including the options you rejected and what would change the decision. Twenty minutes. Show it to someone who was in the room and ask what you got wrong.
  4. Run the newcomer test. Hand your repo to someone who has not seen it, say nothing, and record every question. Each question is a README defect; fix them in their words.
  5. Game-day a runbook. Cause a failure in a test environment and have someone else follow the runbook while you stay silent. Edit every hesitation.
  6. Rewrite a sentence pair. Find the worst sentence you wrote this week and produce a before/after. Keep a file of these; after a month the pattern in your own failures becomes obvious.
  7. Diagram one thing as code. Convert a whiteboard photo or a drawing-tool export into Mermaid, commit it, and label every arrow.

  • My headings pass the table-of-contents test: reading them alone is a coherent summary.

The habit that compounds fastestIf you adopt only one thing from this guide, make it the question log: before answering any question about your system, paste the question verbatim into a file with the date. It costs ten seconds, requires no tooling, and within a month it will have told you exactly which pages to write, what to call things in the words your readers actually use, and which of your existing documents are failing. Every other improvement in this guide gets easier once you are working from evidence about real readers instead of from your own assumptions about them.
