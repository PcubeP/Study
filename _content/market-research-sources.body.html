The Signal-to-Noise Problem

There has never been more financial information available to an Indian retail investor, and there has never been a worse ratio of useful information to confident noise. The constraint is no longer access. It is attention, and almost every commercial incentive in the market media chain is aligned against yours.

None of this requires anyone to be dishonestMost people in financial media are competent and well-intentioned. The problem is structural: a channel that said "no action required" every day would have no viewers, and so no channel says it, and so the aggregate message reaching you is permanently biased toward action. You do not need to assume bad faith to conclude that the default output of the system is more trading than is good for you. The correct response is not cynicism — it is a deliberately constructed information diet.

What a good source looks like

It is primary, or it tells you where the primary is

A report that links to the filing, the circular or the dataset lets you check. One that says "sources said" or "according to reports" does not. The presence of a citation is the cheapest quality signal available.

It is boring

Regulatory filings, bhavcopy files, RBI bulletins and annual reports are boring because they are not trying to hold your attention. That is exactly why they are reliable. Excitement is a product feature, not an information feature.

It publishes its mistakes

A source that corrects itself, keeps old predictions visible and occasionally says "I was wrong about this" is doing something structurally rare. A source whose archive contains only successes has curated it.

Its incentives are visible

You know how it makes money, and that method does not depend on you transacting. A fee-only adviser, a subscription publication with no sponsorships, or a regulator all pass this test. A free tip channel does not.

It distinguishes fact from opinion

"Revenue grew 14% year on year" and "the stock looks attractive" are different statements. A source that blends them without signalling which is which is doing it deliberately.

It is slow

Almost nothing that matters to a long-horizon investor needs to be known within an hour. A weekly or monthly cadence filters out most of what turns out to be nothing, at no cost to you.

The Hierarchy of Sources

Every piece of market information sits somewhere on a chain that runs from the original document to the headline you eventually see. Each step down the chain adds interpretation, loses precision, and increases the chance of error. Knowing where a claim sits on that chain tells you how much weight it deserves — and more importantly, tells you where to go to check it.

The bar length is reliability, not volume — volume runs in the opposite direction. The lower you go, the more of it there is and the less of it matters.

Verifying an intermediary — the exact procedure

  1. Get the claimed registration numberRegistered entities must display it. If someone cannot produce one immediately, stop there.
  2. Search the SEBI intermediary database by nameGo to sebi.gov.in and find the intermediaries section. Search the exact entity name — not the brand name used in marketing, which is often different from the registered name.
  3. Match the number, the name and the categoryAll three. A number that exists but belongs to a different entity is the oldest trick in this space.
  4. Check the category is the right one for what they are doingA research analyst registration permits issuing research; it does not permit personalised investment advice. An investment adviser registration does. A distributor (AMFI ARN) can sell mutual funds on commission but is not an adviser. These are different permissions and are routinely conflated in marketing.
  5. Search the entity name in SEBI's orders and press releasesEnforcement history is public. So are caution notices.
  6. Check how they want to be paidA registered investment adviser is subject to rules on fee structures and on what they may and may not do. Verify the current fee rules. Payment into a personal account, profit-sharing arrangements, or a request for your trading credentials are all disqualifying.
  7. Be suspicious of any assured returnGuaranteed returns in securities are a regulatory red flag in themselves, and an entity promising them is either unregistered or non-compliant.
If they are not registered, you have no recourseThe investor protection fund, SCORES, SMART ODR and exchange arbitration all exist for registered intermediaries. An unregistered "advisory service" operating through a Telegram channel sits entirely outside that perimeter. If the money goes, the route is a police complaint and a civil suit, not a regulatory remedy. Verification takes four minutes and is the single highest-value habit in this entire guide.

Company Sources: Reading an Annual Report in 90 Minutes

The annual report is the single most information-dense document a listed company produces, and it is free, filed with both exchanges, and posted on the company's own website. It is also three hundred pages long, which is why almost nobody reads it. There is a reading order that extracts most of the value in about ninety minutes.

Only worth doing for a direct holdingIf your equity exposure is through index funds, reading annual reports is intellectually interesting and operationally pointless — you own the index, not the company, and you cannot act on what you find. This section is for the capped direct-equity satellite sleeve described in the portfolio construction guide, used alongside the methods in the stock analysis guide.

The 90-minute reading order

  1. Auditor's report — 10 minutes. Read this first.Look for the opinion type (unmodified, qualified, adverse, disclaimer), the Key Audit Matters, any emphasis of matter, and the CARO annexure. A qualified opinion, a going-concern emphasis, or an auditor who resigned mid-year ends the exercise. Also check: who is the auditor, and how long have they been there? A recent auditor change with no clear reason is worth understanding.
  2. Cash flow statement — 10 minutesOperating cash flow versus reported profit, over five years if the report shows them. A company reporting growing profit with flat or negative operating cash flow is telling you something the income statement is not. Then look at investing cash flow (is capital expenditure consistent with the growth story?) and financing cash flow (is growth funded by debt or equity issuance?).
  3. Notes to accounts — 25 minutes, selectivelyNot all of them. Go to: related party transactions (who else is being paid, and how much); contingent liabilities (the obligations not on the balance sheet); borrowings (amounts, maturities, rates, covenants, security); revenue recognition policy; receivables ageing; inventory; and any note the Key Audit Matters pointed to.
  4. Management discussion and analysis — 15 minutesRead it for what it omits. If a segment that was discussed extensively last year is barely mentioned this year, find out why. Compare the language on outlook with last year's — year-on-year comparison of the MD&A is more informative than reading one in isolation.
  5. Corporate governance report and director details — 10 minutesBoard composition, independent director tenure and attendance, audit committee composition, related party approval processes, and remuneration of directors and key management relative to profit.
  6. Shareholding and promoter pledge — 5 minutesIn the report and, more currently, on the exchange site. Pledge percentage and its trend.
  7. Segment reporting — 10 minutesWhere the revenue and profit actually come from. Many companies are one profitable segment carrying several loss-making ones, which the consolidated numbers hide.
  8. Chairman's and CEO letters — 5 minutesRead last year's first, then this year's. Did they do what they said they would? A management that sets out specific commitments and then reports against them honestly is rare and valuable.
The highest-value trick: read two years at onceDownload this year's and last year's annual report. Compare the same sections side by side — the accounting policies, the MD&A, the segment disclosure, the related party list, the contingent liabilities, the risk factors. Changes in language are more informative than the language itself. A risk factor that moves up the list, a policy that changes, a related party that appears or disappears, a segment that stops being broken out — these are the things that do not make it into any news article.

How to read a transcript in 15 minutes

  1. Skip the opening remarks entirely on the first passThey are a scripted reading of the press release. Come back if you have time.
  2. Go straight to the Q&AThis is where the information is. The transcript will mark it clearly.
  3. Read the last three questions firstCall time is finite and operators take questions in order of queue position, but the most pointed questions — often from analysts who are sceptical — frequently come late, when the comfortable ones have been exhausted.
  4. Search for specific words"Guidance", "outlook", "margin", "pricing", "competition", "working capital", "debt", "capex", "one-off", "exceptional". Each search takes seconds and lands you on the substantive exchanges.
  5. Note the questions that were not answered"We will come back to you offline", "we do not break that out", "directionally it is in line" — these are non-answers. One is normal. A pattern of them on the same topic across consecutive quarters is information.
  6. Compare with the previous two quarters' transcriptsDid management say something last quarter that this quarter's numbers contradict? Has the explanation for a weak segment changed three times in three quarters? This is the single highest-value comparison available and nobody does it.
  7. Note who is askingThe same analysts appear repeatedly. Over a few quarters you will identify the ones who ask substantive questions, and you can read their questions first.

Exchange Filings and the Material Event Rules

Listed companies in India are required to disclose material events to the exchanges within specified timelines. Understanding what must be disclosed, and how fast, tells you what you can expect to find in the filings — and tells you when a "news report" about an undisclosed development should be treated with suspicion.

ElementPosition (verify at sebi.gov.in)
Governing frameworkSEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 — "LODR" — particularly Regulation 30 and Schedule III
Deemed material eventsA list of events that are material by their nature and must always be disclosed — including acquisitions, scheme of arrangement, winding up, changes in key managerial personnel, auditor resignation, fraud by or against the company, and others

TimelinesPrescribed timelines from the occurrence of the event or from the board decision, which were tightened in recent amendments. Verify the current timelines.

The practical consequenceIf a genuinely material development has occurred, there should be a filing. A story circulating on social media about a large order, an acquisition or a regulatory action, with no corresponding exchange filing and no clarification, is either not material, not true, or both. The rumour-verification framework means that a sustained unexplained price move accompanied by media reports should produce a company response. Checking the exchange filings before acting on a story is a thirty-second test that defeats most market rumours.

How to use a screener without being misled

  1. Check the definition of every ratio you filter on"ROCE" can be computed with or without cash, with average or closing capital employed, with or without other income. Two providers will give different numbers for the same company and both will be defensible. Read the provider's definition before filtering on it.
  2. Decide consolidated or standalone deliberatelyFor a company with significant subsidiaries these are completely different businesses. Screening on standalone numbers for a holding company produces nonsense.
  3. Beware survivorshipA screener shows companies that are currently listed. Companies that were delisted, suspended or went to insolvency have left the universe. Any "this screen would have returned X%" claim built on current constituents is overstated by an unknown margin.
  4. Do not over-fit the filterA screen with nine conditions returning four companies has not found four good companies; it has found four companies that happen to satisfy nine arbitrary thresholds. Three or four conditions with a clear economic rationale is usually the limit of what means anything.
  5. Treat the output as a reading listThe screen's job is to produce ten names to read about, not ten names to buy. The work starts after the screen.
  6. Verify the final candidates against the filingBefore any position, check the key numbers in the annual report. Data errors in aggregators are not rare, particularly after corporate actions, restatements and accounting standard changes.
  7. Exclude the surveillance listCross-check candidates against the exchanges' graded surveillance and trade-to-trade lists before going further.

What to actually look at when evaluating a scheme

  • Category — the SEBI-mandated category determines what the fund may hold. Check this before anything else.
  • Benchmark — and whether it is a Total Return Index. Comparison against a price index flatters the fund by roughly 1–1.5 percentage points a year.
  • Expense ratio, direct plan — and the difference versus the regular plan, which is the commission you would otherwise pay.
  • Rolling returns, not point-to-point returns. A fund's "5-year return" depends heavily on the start date chosen; rolling returns over many overlapping windows do not.
  • Portfolio holdings and overlap with what you already own.
  • Portfolio turnover — high turnover means costs inside the fund that do not appear in the expense ratio.
  • Fund size relative to the strategy. A small-cap strategy at very large scale cannot execute the same way it did when small.
  • Manager tenure — a ten-year track record under three different managers is three track records.
  • For debt funds: modified duration, credit quality breakdown, and the proportion in instruments below the top rating. See the debt section of the portfolio construction guide.
  • Risk-o-meter and the scheme's own stated risk level — and whether it has changed.

Newsletters, Podcasts and Video — How to Evaluate a Source

Independent commentary is where the best and the worst financial content both live. There is no institutional filter, which means genuinely expert individuals publish alongside people whose entire business model is your trading activity. The evaluation questions below are worth applying to every source before it enters your routine, including the long-standing ones.

The conflict-of-interest questions

  1. How does this person make money?If the answer is unclear after five minutes of looking, that is the answer. Advertising, sponsorship, courses, affiliate links to brokers, subscription fees, and holding the securities discussed are all different incentive structures with different distortions.
  2. Are they SEBI-registered, and as what?Providing personalised investment advice requires registration as an investment adviser. Publishing research requires registration as a research analyst. Neither registration is required for general education. Someone recommending specific securities to a general audience without registration is operating outside the framework — verify the current requirements at sebi.gov.in.
  3. Do they disclose positions?A credible source discloses whether they hold what they are discussing, and says when that changes. A research analyst is subject to specific disclosure requirements.
  4. Is there an affiliate relationship with a broker or platform?A referral link means they are paid when you open an account. That is not disqualifying if disclosed; it is disqualifying if hidden.
  5. Do they take sponsorship from the entities they cover?And is sponsored content clearly labelled every time, or only sometimes?
  6. What happens to their old calls?Is the archive complete and searchable, or has it been curated? Do they revisit a thesis that failed, and in the same format and prominence as the original?
  7. Do they ever say "I do not know"?Markets produce genuine uncertainty constantly. A source that is confident about everything has replaced analysis with performance.
  8. Do they distinguish between education and recommendation?"Here is how a cash flow statement works" and "here is what to buy" are different activities with different regulatory consequences. A source that blurs them deliberately is telling you something.
  9. What is the implied time horizon?Content optimised for weekly publication cannot be about decade-horizon investing, because nothing changes weekly at that horizon. The cadence constrains the content.
  10. Would you still value this if the market fell 40%?The honest test. Most high-frequency commentary becomes obviously useless in a crisis, which is when you most need judgement.

Detecting Pumps, Paid Promotion and Fabrication

Market manipulation aimed at retail investors is not hypothetical in India; SEBI's enforcement orders describe the mechanics in detail, repeatedly. The schemes are not sophisticated. They work because they are fast, emotionally compelling, and arrive through channels that feel personal.

Schematic only, not real price data. The key structural feature is the right-hand side: a security that rose on thin volume falls through successive lower circuits with no buyers, so the paper loss cannot be realised at any price near it.

Pump-and-dump — the signatures

  • A small, illiquid company with a low free float and low average traded volume — the only kind where a campaign can move the price.
  • A sudden, unexplained price rise on volume far above the recent average, often with repeated upper circuits.
  • No corresponding exchange filing that explains the move, and no clarification from the company.
  • Coordinated messaging appearing across several channels at once, with similar phrasing and a specific target price.
  • A narrative involving a transformative order, a name change, a new business line, or an association with a currently fashionable theme.
  • The security appears on an exchange's graded surveillance or trade-to-trade list — check this before anything else.
  • Promoter pledge or promoter holding changes in the recent shareholding pattern.
  • Urgency: "buy before Monday", "last chance below X".
  • The channel that promoted it goes quiet, or deletes messages, once the price turns.
Why the loss is usually total rather than partialThe mechanism that makes these schemes profitable for the operator is the same one that traps you. The security is illiquid by design. On the way up, thin volume means small buying moves the price a lot. On the way down, there is no natural buyer at any price, so the security hits successive lower circuits with no trades executed. You cannot sell at −20% or −40%, because there is nobody on the other side. Investors in these situations routinely find that the first price at which they can actually exit is 70–90% below where they bought. Read a few of SEBI's enforcement orders in this area at sebi.gov.in; the mechanics are described plainly and the deterrent effect of reading three of them is considerable.

Detecting a fabricated screenshot or track record

  • Profit-and-loss screenshots are editable in a browser's developer tools in under a minute. They carry no evidential weight whatsoever, from anyone.
  • Look for internal inconsistency: does the percentage match the absolute amount? Does the date match a trading day? Does the quantity match a valid lot size for that instrument?
  • A screenshot of a single winning position says nothing about the account. Ask for the full account statement for a full year, including losses, and note how often that request is refused.
  • Verified track records in this space are rare. A genuine one would be a broker-issued statement or an audited record covering a complete period, not an app screenshot.
  • Be alert to survivorship within one person's own output: ten "calls" are made, three work, three are screenshotted, seven are never mentioned again.
  • Check whether the claimed return is even plausible. Sustained returns far above what large, well-resourced institutions achieve, by an individual, posted publicly for free, is not a likely configuration.
  • Impersonation is common: fake accounts using the name and photograph of a genuine, well-known investor or of a registered intermediary. Check SEBI's caution notices and press releases, and verify the entity in the intermediary database rather than trusting a profile.

Building Your Own System: The Reading Routine

An information system is not a list of sources. It is a schedule, a set of exclusions, and a place for what you learn to go. Without a schedule, consumption expands to fill available time. Without exclusions, the worst sources crowd out the best, because they are designed to. Without a destination, nothing you read compounds.

The schedule is the system. Sources without a schedule become a feed, and a feed is optimised for someone else's objectives.

Daily — 15 minutes, at a fixed time

  • One business daily. Headlines only, open at most two articles. Fifteen minutes on a timer.
  • Exchange filing alerts for your direct holdings, if you have any. Read the filing, not the coverage of it.
  • Do not look at prices. Price checking is the single most behaviourally corrosive daily habit; the more often you see a loss, the more risk-averse you become, which is precisely backwards for a long-horizon investor.
  • Do not open a trading app unless you are executing a pre-decided transaction.
  • When the timer ends, stop, whether or not you finished. There is always more; that is the nature of the supply.

Weekly — 60 minutes, same slot each week

  • One long-form piece read properly — an annual report section, a rating rationale, a transcript, a regulator publication, or a book chapter. Depth once beats breadth seven times.
  • Update the portfolio tracker: transactions, charges, current values. Fifteen minutes.
  • Check the 10-year G-Sec yield and note it. Thirty seconds.
  • Write three sentences in your notes: what you learned, what you are uncertain about, what you are doing (usually nothing).
  • Clear the "read later" queue ruthlessly. Anything older than two weeks was not important.
  • No transactions during this session. Reviewing and acting are separate activities and must be separated in time, or the review becomes a justification exercise.

Monthly — 20 minutes

  • The six-line macro dashboard from the earlier section.
  • AMFI monthly category flow data, as a contrarian temperature check only.
  • RBI Bulletin's "State of the Economy" article, skimmed.
  • Check that every standing instruction executed.

Quarterly — 3 hours

  • Results and earnings call transcripts for every direct holding, using the 15-minute template.
  • Shareholding pattern and promoter pledge for every direct holding.
  • Allocation band check per the portfolio construction guide; act only if a band is breached.
  • Thesis review: for each direct holding, has a pre-written "what would make me wrong" condition triggered?
  • Source audit: which sources did you actually use this quarter, and which did you merely consume? Remove two.

Annually — half a day

  • Annual reports for direct holdings, using the 90-minute order, compared against the prior year.
  • The current SPIVA India scorecard, read with its methodology.
  • The Economic Survey.
  • Your own decision log for the year, read start to finish. This is the only honest calibration available.
  • The full annual review per the portfolio construction guide — life facts, insurance, savings rate, goals, allocation, XIRR versus benchmark, costs, tax, nominations.

APIs and Light Automation

A small amount of automation removes the tedious parts of tracking — fetching NAVs, computing allocation drift, computing XIRR — without creating a system you have to maintain. The ambition should be deliberately low. A script that updates a spreadsheet is useful; a script that places orders is a different activity with different risks.

Read the terms before you automate against anyone's siteExchange and data provider websites have terms of use, and many apply rate limiting, require session headers, or prohibit automated access outright. Scraping a site that forbids it is a terms violation and may be blocked or actioned. Prefer official downloadable files and documented APIs over scraping. Broker APIs are typically a paid, authenticated service with their own terms and their own security obligations on you. Verify the current terms of any source before automating against it.

A simple portfolio tracker in Python

"""
A minimal Indian portfolio tracker.

Reads two CSV files you maintain by hand, fetches
current mutual fund NAVs from AMFI's published daily
NAV file, and prints:
  - current value by asset class
  - drift against your policy weights
  - portfolio XIRR

No trading. No credentials. No broker API.
Read AMFI's terms of use before scheduling this.

holdings.csv
    scheme_code,asset_class,units
    119551,IN-EQ-BROAD,1243.871
    120503,INTL-EQ,884.220
    118989,GOLD,610.405
    103504,DEBT-SHORT,2204.118

flows.csv   (negative = money you put in)
    date,amount
    2021-04-05,-200000
    2021-05-05,-25000
    2026-03-31,0        # leave 0; value is added
"""

import csv
import urllib.request
from datetime import date, datetime

AMFI_NAV_URL = "https://www.amfiindia.com/spages/NAVAll.txt"

POLICY = {
    "IN-EQ-BROAD": 0.45,
    "IN-EQ-MIDSMALL": 0.10,
    "INTL-EQ": 0.12,
    "GOLD": 0.10,
    "DEBT-SHORT": 0.23,
}
BAND = 0.05  # absolute percentage points


def fetch_navs():
    """AMFI publishes a semicolon-delimited daily file.

    Columns are, in order:
      Scheme Code; ISIN Div Payout/Growth; ISIN Div
      Reinvestment; Scheme Name; Net Asset Value; Date

    Header and category lines do not have 6 fields, so
    they are skipped naturally.
    """
    navs = {}
    req = urllib.request.Request(
        AMFI_NAV_URL,
        headers={"User-Agent": "personal-portfolio-tracker"},
    )
    with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=30) as resp:
        text = resp.read().decode("utf-8", errors="replace")

    for line in text.splitlines():
        parts = line.split(";")
        if len(parts) != 6:
            continue
        code, _, _, name, nav, nav_date = parts
        code = code.strip()
        try:
            navs[code] = {
                "nav": float(nav.strip()),
                "name": name.strip(),
                "date": nav_date.strip(),
            }
        except ValueError:
            continue  # header row, or "N.A." for a stale NAV
    return navs


def read_holdings(path="holdings.csv"):
    rows = []
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        for row in csv.DictReader(f):
            rows.append(
                {
                    "scheme_code": row["scheme_code"].strip(),
                    "asset_class": row["asset_class"].strip(),
                    "units": float(row["units"]),
                }
            )
    return rows


def read_flows(path="flows.csv"):
    flows = []
    with open(path, newline="", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        for row in csv.DictReader(f):
            d = datetime.strptime(row["date"].strip(), "%Y-%m-%d").date()
            amt = float(row["amount"])
            if amt != 0.0:
                flows.append((d, amt))
    flows.sort(key=lambda x: x[0])
    return flows


def xnpv(rate, flows):
    t0 = flows[0][0]
    return sum(
        cf / (1.0 + rate) ** ((d - t0).days / 365.0)
        for d, cf in flows
    )


def xirr(flows, lo=-0.9999, hi=10.0, tol=1e-7, iters=400):
    """Bisection. Robust enough for a normal investor's
    flow pattern (many negatives, one positive at the end).
    Returns None if it cannot bracket a root."""
    if xnpv(lo, flows) * xnpv(hi, flows) > 0:
        return None
    for _ in range(iters):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2.0
        v = xnpv(mid, flows)
        if abs(v) < tol:
            return mid
        if v > 0:
            lo = mid
        else:
            hi = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2.0


def inr(x):
    """Format in the Indian grouping convention."""
    neg = x < 0
    s = "{:.0f}".format(abs(x))
    if len(s) <= 3:
        out = s
    else:
        head, tail = s[:-3], s[-3:]
        groups = []
        while len(head) > 2:
            groups.insert(0, head[-2:])
            head = head[:-2]
        if head:
            groups.insert(0, head)
        out = ",".join(groups) + "," + tail
    return ("-" if neg else "") + "Rs " + out


def main():
    navs = fetch_navs()
    holdings = read_holdings()

    by_class = {}
    total = 0.0
    stale = []

    for h in holdings:
        info = navs.get(h["scheme_code"])
        if info is None:
            stale.append(h["scheme_code"])
            continue
        value = info["nav"] * h["units"]
        by_class[h["asset_class"]] = (
            by_class.get(h["asset_class"], 0.0) + value
        )
        total += value

    if stale:
        print("WARNING - scheme codes not found in the AMFI file:")
        for code in stale:
            print("   ", code)
        print("   Check the code, or the scheme may have merged.\n")

    print("PORTFOLIO AS AT", date.today().isoformat())
    print("=" * 56)
    print("{:<18}{:>16}{:>9}{:>9}".format(
        "ASSET CLASS", "VALUE", "ACTUAL", "TARGET"))
    print("-" * 56)

    breaches = []
    for cls in POLICY:
        value = by_class.get(cls, 0.0)
        actual = value / total if total else 0.0
        target = POLICY[cls]
        drift = actual - target
        flag = ""
        if abs(drift) > BAND:
            flag = "  BAND BREACH"
            breaches.append((cls, drift, (target - actual) * total))
        print("{:<18}{:>16}{:>8.1f}%{:>8.1f}%{}".format(
            cls, inr(value), actual * 100, target * 100, flag))

    unmapped = set(by_class) - set(POLICY)
    for cls in sorted(unmapped):
        print("{:<18}{:>16}{:>9}{:>9}  NOT IN POLICY".format(
            cls, inr(by_class[cls]), "-", "-"))

    print("-" * 56)
    print("{:<18}{:>16}".format("TOTAL", inr(total)))

    if breaches:
        print("\nREBALANCING REQUIRED")
        for cls, drift, rupees in breaches:
            action = "BUY " if rupees > 0 else "SELL"
            print("  {} {:<16} {} (drift {:+.1f}pp)".format(
                action, cls, inr(abs(rupees)), drift * 100))
        print("\n  Before selling anything, try routing the next")
        print("  few months of contributions to the underweight")
        print("  asset first. See the portfolio construction guide.")
    else:
        print("\nAll asset classes within bands. No action required.")

    flows = read_flows()
    flows.append((date.today(), total))
    rate = xirr(flows)
    if rate is None:
        print("\nXIRR: could not converge. Check the flow signs -")
        print("the first flow must be negative and the final")
        print("portfolio value must be a positive flow.")
    else:
        invested = -sum(a for _, a in flows if a < 0)
        print("\nTotal invested     ", inr(invested))
        print("Current value      ", inr(total))
        print("Portfolio XIRR      {:.2f}% p.a.".format(rate * 100))
        print("\nCompare this against your BLENDED benchmark's")
        print("time-weighted return, not against an equity index.")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Frequently Asked Questions

Questions that recur from Indian investors trying to build an information system. These are general explanations, not recommendations, and no source mentioned is endorsed.

What is the single most useful free source, if I only use one?
The exchange corporate announcements page for any company you hold, at nseindia.com or bseindia.com. Everything you read in a news article about a listed company originated there, hours earlier, in more detail and without interpretation. If you hold only index funds and have no direct equity, the equivalent answer is amfiindia.com for NAVs and category flow data, plus your registrar's consolidated statement.
Is Screener.in enough, or do I need a paid tool?
For a part-time investor with a capped direct-equity sleeve, a good free screener plus the exchange filings covers almost everything. Paid tools buy you convenience — aggregated estimates, segment-level operating data, pre-built alerts — not better judgement. The honest test: write down the specific decision the subscription would change. If you cannot name one, the subscription is buying the feeling of being well-equipped. Note also that several tools are affiliated with brokers and platforms, which is a commercial relationship worth knowing about. Nothing here endorses any of them.
How do I know if a YouTube channel or newsletter is trustworthy?
Run the conflict-of-interest questions: how do they make money; are they SEBI-registered and as what; do they disclose positions and affiliate relationships; is their archive complete and uncurated; do they revisit failed calls with the same prominence as successes; do they ever say "I do not know". Then check the registration claim in SEBI's intermediary database at sebi.gov.in, and search the name in SEBI's orders and caution notices. Four minutes. Do it before you take a single idea from anyone.
Someone in a Telegram group gave a tip that worked. Should I keep following them?
No, and the fact that it worked is not evidence. In any group of a thousand people making calls, some will be right by chance, and you only see the ones being promoted. The structural questions are unanswered: are they registered; do they hold the security; who is selling to you as the price rises; what is their record including losses; and why are they giving this away free rather than acting on it themselves. One profitable tip followed by a position size that reflects new-found confidence is the standard path to a large loss. Run the four-minute due diligence routine and note how often it terminates at step 1.
How much time should I actually spend on this each week?
Fifteen minutes a day and an hour a week is ample for a diversified portfolio of index funds, debt and gold — roughly 100 hours a year against a portfolio needing perhaps two to seven decisions. If you run a capped direct-equity sleeve, add about three hours a quarter for results and transcripts, and half a day a year for annual reports. More time spent does not correlate with better outcomes beyond that point, and past a certain level it correlates negatively, because additional inputs produce additional trades.
Should I watch business television?
For scheduled events — the Union Budget, an RBI policy announcement, a major result — it is a reasonable way to follow live developments. As a daily habit it is the worst available format for an investor: continuous commentary on price movement, guests whose positions are often undisclosed, a format requiring a confident view on everything within thirty seconds, and an explicit commercial objective of keeping you watching. None of that is a criticism of the people involved; it is a description of the incentive structure.
Where do I find earnings call transcripts?
The company's own investor relations page is the primary location, and transcripts are also commonly filed with the exchanges under corporate announcements. Several data platforms aggregate them, which is more convenient. Verify the current disclosure requirement for recordings and transcripts under the SEBI LODR Regulations at sebi.gov.in. They are the highest-value free document on a listed company — the only place management answers questions they did not choose.
Do FII and DII flow numbers predict the market?
There is no reliable evidence that daily institutional flow data gives a retail investor an actionable edge. The numbers describe what already happened, are as much a result of price as a cause, cover only part of foreign activity, and report cash and derivative activity separately. They are reported daily because they are available daily and sound specific, not because they forecast anything. For a long-horizon investor following a policy allocation, daily flow data has no legitimate use.
What is SPIVA and why does it matter more than star ratings?
The SPIVA India scorecard, published at spglobal.com/spdji, reports what percentage of actively managed funds in each category underperformed their benchmark over 1, 3, 5 and 10 years, corrected for funds that were merged or closed. A star rating tells you which funds did well in the past, which is weakly predictive. SPIVA tells you a structural fact about the category, which is the right input to the active versus passive decision — a decision that comes before any fund selection. Read the current scorecard with its methodology rather than any summary; results vary by category and period, and summaries cherry-pick in both directions.
How do I verify that someone is actually SEBI-registered?
Go to sebi.gov.in, find the intermediaries section, and search the exact registered entity name — not the brand name used in marketing, which is often different. Match the name, the registration number and the category. The category matters: a research analyst registration does not permit personalised investment advice; an investment adviser registration does; a mutual fund distributor (AMFI ARN) is neither. Then search the name in SEBI's enforcement orders and caution notices. If any of this fails, there is no investor protection fund, no SCORES route and no arbitration available to you.
Can I just use ChatGPT or another AI tool for market research?
For explaining concepts, summarising a document you supply, and writing analysis code — yes, usefully. For facts about a specific company, a current price, a tax rate, a regulation or a recent event — no. Language models produce confident, plausible, and sometimes entirely fabricated specifics, including invented figures and citations, and their knowledge has a cutoff. Any factual claim must be traced to a primary source before you act on it, which is the same rule that applies to every level-4-to-6 source in the hierarchy. And no AI tool is a SEBI-registered investment adviser.
How do I spot a pump-and-dump before I am in it?
Five signatures together: a small, illiquid company with a low free float; a sharp price rise on volume far above its own recent average, often with repeated upper circuits; no exchange filing that explains the move; coordinated messaging across channels with a specific target price and urgency; and presence on an exchange surveillance measure list. Check the surveillance list and the filings before anything else — it takes two minutes. The reason this matters more than an ordinary bad investment is liquidity: on the way down there is no buyer, successive lower circuits execute no trades, and the first price at which you can actually exit is frequently 70–90% below where you bought.
What is worth paying for?
In rough order of defensibility: a one-off engagement with a fee-only SEBI-registered investment adviser to sanity-check your plan; a chartered accountant if you have derivatives activity, foreign assets, business income or a loss to carry forward — the fee is small against a forfeited carry-forward; one data tool if it genuinely changes a decision you can name; and one subscription publication whose beat matches your holdings. Not worth paying for: tips, "calls", signal services, courses promising a system, anything with a guaranteed return, and anything requiring your trading credentials.
I feel like I am always behind on market news. Is that a problem?
No — it is the correct state. The volume of market content is unbounded and designed to be unfinishable, so "caught up" is not an achievable condition and chasing it is a treadmill. The relevant question is not whether you have read everything, but whether you have read the small number of things that touch an actual decision: the filings for what you hold, your allocation drift, and anything that changes the rules. A deliberately small information diet is not a compromise; it is the design.
My portfolio is entirely index funds. Do I need any of this?
Much less of it, which is one of the underrated advantages of indexing. You need: the AMFI NAV source and your registrar's statements; the amfiindia.com category flow data as an occasional temperature check; the SPIVA scorecard annually to confirm the decision still holds; the index methodology document for what you actually own; the regulators for rule changes; and the tax calendar. That is five or six sources and perhaps thirty hours a year. Everything else in this guide becomes optional reading rather than a routine.

The twelve rules, condensed

  1. Know the level of every claimPrimary filing, regulator, curated data, analyst, news, social. Never act on levels 4 to 6 without tracing to 1 or 2.
  2. Go to the filingEverything you read about a listed company originated there, earlier and in more detail.
  3. Verify registration before paying anyoneFour minutes on the SEBI intermediary database. Match name, number and category.
  4. Prefer boring sourcesRegulatory filings and statistical releases are reliable precisely because they are not competing for your attention.
  5. Ask how each source is paidAnd whether that method depends on you transacting.
  6. No alert without a pre-written actionIf you cannot complete "when this fires I will do X", the alert should not exist.
  7. Turn off every push notificationThere is no market event that needs to interrupt you.
  8. Keep the diet small on purposeTen sources, a fixed schedule, and a willingness to be permanently behind.
  9. Write three sentences a weekReading without recording produces the impression of knowledge and none of the benefit.
  10. Separate reviewing from actingDifferent sessions, different days. A review that can end in a trade becomes a justification exercise.
  11. Assume a screenshot is fabricatedAnd assume an accuracy percentage is meaningless. Ask for a full-year statement including losses, and note the refusal.
  12. Default to no actionA portfolio built on the principles in the portfolio construction guide needs between zero and seven decisions a year. Everything else you read is, for your purposes, noise — and noise that provokes a trade is worse than noise you ignored.
