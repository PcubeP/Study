Why a Fund, Not a Stock, Is the Right First Instrument

The question is not whether picking stocks can work. It is whether it is the right use of your hours, given that a pooled vehicle gives you instant diversification, professional execution and a regulated structure for a cost measured in basis points. For almost every Indian software engineer with a full-time job, the honest answer is that funds should be the core and direct equity, if it appears at all, should be a deliberately capped sleeve.

The arithmetic of diversification

WHY ONE STOCK IS NOT LIKE TWENTY

Suppose each stock in a universe has a 4% chance of
losing 90%+ of its value over ten years (business
failure, fraud, obsolescence, a terminal regulatory
change). This is not pessimistic; across any broad
market over a decade, a meaningful tail of companies
does exactly this.

  Hold 1 stock:
    probability of a catastrophic hit = 4%
    if it happens, your equity portfolio is gone

  Hold 5 stocks, equally weighted:
    probability at least one is hit
      = 1 - 0.96^5 = 18.5%
    if one is hit, you lose 90% of 20% = 18% of
    the portfolio - painful, survivable

  Hold 20 stocks:
    probability at least one is hit
      = 1 - 0.96^20 = 55.8%
    expected number hit = 0.8
    if one is hit, you lose 90% of 5% = 4.5%
    - a bad quarter, not a disaster

  Hold an index fund of 250 companies:
    expected number hit = 10
    expected drag = 10 x 90% x 0.4% = 3.6% of the
    portfolio spread across a decade, and it is
    already embedded in the index's historical
    return

THE POINT: diversification does not raise your
expected return. It removes the LEFT TAIL - the
outcomes from which you cannot recover. Expected
return is set by the asset class and the price you
pay; survival is set by position count and sizing.

How a Mutual Fund Actually Works

An Indian mutual fund is not a company and the AMC does not own your money. The structure is a trust, deliberately designed so that the entity managing the money, the entity holding the money and the entity keeping the records are all different. Understanding this structure is what tells you what actually happens if an AMC fails — and the answer is reassuring in a way most investors do not realise.

The Indian mutual fund trust structure. The AMC manages the money; it never owns or holds it.

What you actually own

You own units in a scheme. A unit is a proportionate beneficial interest in a specific pool of securities held by the trustees through a custodian. You do not own shares in the AMC, you are not a creditor of the AMC, and the AMC cannot pledge your units or use scheme assets for its own purposes.

What happens if the AMC fails

  • Scheme assets are not available to the AMC's creditors. They belong to the trust, held for unitholders, and are custodied separately. This is the entire point of the structure.
  • The trustees can terminate the AMC's appointment and appoint another, subject to SEBI and unitholder approval as required under the regulations.
  • In practice, Indian AMCs that have been sold, merged or wound down have had their schemes transferred to another AMC or merged into similar schemes, with unitholders given an exit option at the prevailing NAV without exit load.
  • What the structure does not protect you from is the performance of the underlying securities. If a debt scheme holds bonds that default, you lose money, and no amount of trustee oversight changes that. There is no deposit insurance equivalent for mutual funds, and there should not be — you are taking market risk by design.
The side-pocket and the wind-up mechanismTwo mechanisms you should know exist. A segregated portfolio (side pocket) allows an AMC, on a credit event, to separate the affected security into a separate set of units so that the rest of the scheme remains liquid and new investors are not harmed. A wind-up under Regulation 39 allows a scheme to be closed; following events in the Indian debt fund market in 2020 and the subsequent litigation, SEBI clarified that unitholder consent by simple majority is required and provided detailed disclosure and timeline rules. Both are real, both have been used, and both mean a debt fund is not a bank deposit. Read the SID section on segregated portfolios before investing in any credit-risk-taking scheme.

NAV Properly Understood

Net asset value is the one number every fund investor sees daily and the one most frequently misunderstood. It is not a price in the sense a share price is a price. It is an arithmetic consequence of the portfolio's value divided by the units outstanding, and a ₹12 NAV is in no sense cheaper than a ₹480 NAV.

Why a low NAV is not "cheap" — the arithmetic

THE MOST COMMON BEGINNER ERROR, SETTLED

You have Rs 1,00,000 to invest. Two funds hold
IDENTICAL portfolios; one is simply older.

  FUND A: NAV Rs 15      FUND B: NAV Rs 450
  Units you get          Units you get
  = 100000/15            = 100000/450
  = 6,666.67             = 222.22

Now both portfolios rise 20%.

  FUND A: NAV -> Rs 18   FUND B: NAV -> Rs 540
  Value = 6,666.67 x 18  Value = 222.22 x 540
        = Rs 1,20,000          = Rs 1,20,000

IDENTICAL. The number of units is irrelevant. What
matters is the PERCENTAGE change in NAV, which is
determined entirely by the underlying portfolio.

WHY THIS CONFUSION EXISTS
With a SHARE, price per share relates to earnings
per share, so a low price can genuinely be cheap
relative to earnings. With a FUND UNIT there is no
such relationship: the NAV is just total value
divided by unit count, and the unit count is
arbitrary - it depends only on how long the scheme
has existed and how much money has flowed in.

A new fund offer at Rs 10 is NOT a discount. It is
a brand-new portfolio bought at today's market
prices, with no track record, launched because the
category is currently easy to sell.

THE "DIVIDEND MAKES NAV FALL" POINT
If a scheme with NAV Rs 50 declares an IDCW payout
of Rs 4 per unit, the NAV falls to Rs 46 on the
record date. You received Rs 4 in cash and your NAV
fell by Rs 4. NOTHING WAS CREATED. Your total value
is unchanged, except that you now owe tax on the
Rs 4 at your slab rate. This single mechanism has
cost Indian retail investors a great deal of money.

If you care about which NAV you get — and for a long-horizon SIP you should not — transact well before cut-off. Verify the current rules at sebi.gov.in.

Costs: TER, Direct Versus Regular, and the Lifetime Price of a Distributor

Costs are the only variable in investing that is known in advance, guaranteed to apply, and entirely within your control. Returns are uncertain; a 1.1 percentage point annual cost difference is certain. Over a 25-year accumulation, that certain difference can consume an amount roughly equal to everything you contributed.

TER is the annual charge, expressed as a percentage of a scheme's daily net assets, covering the investment management fee, registrar and custodian charges, audit, marketing, distributor commission (in regular plans), and GST on the management fee. A fair word for distributors and for adviceNone of the above says advice is worthless. Good advice — the kind that stops you redeeming in a crash, gets your asset allocation right, ensures you are insured, and stops you buying a thematic fund at the top of a cycle — is worth far more than 1.1% a year. The combination of an RIA fee plus direct plans is almost always cheaper than regular plans alone, and the incentives are aligned rather than conflicted.

Debt Funds in Depth

Debt funds are widely sold as "safe" and widely misunderstood. They are not deposits, they carry two distinct and separable risks, their net asset value genuinely falls, and the tax treatment of most of them changed fundamentally in 2023. Used correctly they are excellent instruments. Used as a fixed deposit substitute without understanding duration, they produce unpleasant surprises.

Where the return comes from

A DEBT FUND'S RETURN HAS THREE COMPONENTS

  1. ACCRUAL (coupon income)
     The interest the bonds pay. Predictable,
     always positive, accrues daily.

  2. MARK-TO-MARKET (price change)
     Bond prices move INVERSELY to yields. This
     component can be strongly negative.

  3. CREDIT EVENTS
     Downgrades reduce the valuation; defaults
     cause write-downs. Always negative when
     they occur.

  Total return = accrual + MTM + credit effects
                 - TER

THE FD COMPARISON, STATED CORRECTLY
  Fixed deposit: the bank promises a rate. The
  value never falls. Deposit insurance covers a
  prescribed amount per depositor per bank (verify
  the current limit at dicgc.org.in). Premature
  withdrawal attracts a penalty. Interest is taxed
  at slab, every year, on accrual, with TDS.

  Debt fund: no promise. NAV marked to market
  daily, and it can fall. No insurance. Usually no
  exit load after a short period. Tax deferred
  until you redeem - which for a long holding is
  a real and underrated advantage even after the
  2023 change removed indexation.

Interest-rate risk and duration, worked

DURATION - THE NUMBER THAT MATTERS MOST

MACAULAY DURATION is the weighted average time to
receive a bond's cash flows, in years. SEBI defines
every debt fund category by it.

MODIFIED DURATION estimates price sensitivity:
  Modified duration = Macaulay duration / (1 + y)
  where y is the yield per period

  Approximate price change
    = - modified duration x change in yield

WORKED: A SHORT DURATION FUND
  Macaulay duration                  2.40 years
  Portfolio yield to maturity (YTM)      7.20%
  Modified duration = 2.40 / 1.072  =  2.239
  TER                                    0.35%

  SCENARIO A: yields rise 50 bps
    Price effect = -2.239 x 0.50%   = -1.12%
    Accrual over 1 year             = +7.20%
    Less TER                        = -0.35%
    APPROXIMATE 1-YEAR RETURN       = +5.73%

  SCENARIO B: yields rise 150 bps
    Price effect = -2.239 x 1.50%   = -3.36%
    Accrual + TER effect            = +6.85%
    APPROXIMATE 1-YEAR RETURN       = +3.49%

  SCENARIO C: yields FALL 100 bps
    Price effect = +2.239 x 1.00%   = +2.24%
    Accrual + TER effect            = +6.85%
    APPROXIMATE 1-YEAR RETURN       = +9.09%

WORKED: A LONG DURATION / GILT FUND
  Macaulay duration                  9.10 years
  Portfolio YTM                          7.05%
  Modified duration = 9.10 / 1.0705 =  8.501
  TER                                    0.45%

  SCENARIO A: yields rise 50 bps
    Price effect = -8.501 x 0.50%   = -4.25%
    Accrual - TER                   = +6.60%
    APPROXIMATE 1-YEAR RETURN       = +2.35%

  SCENARIO B: yields rise 150 bps
    Price effect = -8.501 x 1.50%   = -12.75%
    Accrual - TER                   =  +6.60%
    APPROXIMATE 1-YEAR RETURN       =  -6.15%
    *** A NEGATIVE YEAR IN A FUND WITH ZERO
        CREDIT RISK, HOLDING ONLY GOVERNMENT
        SECURITIES. ***

  SCENARIO C: yields FALL 100 bps
    Price effect = +8.501 x 1.00%   = +8.50%
    Accrual - TER                   = +6.60%
    APPROXIMATE 1-YEAR RETURN       = +15.10%

THE BREAKEVEN YIELD RISE
  How far can yields rise before a year's return
  turns negative?
    breakeven = (YTM - TER) / modified duration

  Short duration fund: 6.85 / 2.239 = 306 bps
  Long duration fund:  6.60 / 8.501 =  78 bps

  The long fund turns negative on a 78 basis point
  rise. The short fund needs a 306 basis point
  rise. THIS is the practical meaning of duration,
  and it is the single calculation to do before
  buying any debt fund.

CONVEXITY - the small correction
  The relationship is not perfectly linear: price
  rises slightly more than duration predicts when
  yields fall, and falls slightly less when yields
  rise. For retail purposes the linear estimate is
  close enough; just know the error is in your
  favour.

MATCH DURATION TO HORIZON
  The rule of thumb: choose a fund whose Macaulay
  duration is roughly equal to or below your
  investment horizon. Held for approximately the
  duration, interim price losses are offset by
  reinvestment at the new higher yields.
    6 months  -> liquid / ultra short
    1 year    -> low duration / money market
    2-3 years -> short duration
    3-5 years -> corporate bond / medium duration
    5+ years and willing to accept volatility
              -> gilt or long duration

Why credit risk in a fund is worse than credit risk in a single bondIf you hold a bond directly and it defaults, you lose on that bond. If a fund you hold suffers a default, three things happen simultaneously: the NAV is written down; other investors redeem, forcing the manager to sell the liquid holdings first, which concentrates the remaining portfolio in the illiquid and impaired paper; and the investors who stay are left holding a worse portfolio than they started with. This reflexive dynamic — not the default itself — is what made India's 2018–2020 debt fund episodes so damaging. SEBI's responses (minimum liquid asset requirements, segregated portfolios, swing pricing for stressed conditions, potential risk class disclosure) reduce but do not eliminate it. Before buying any debt fund, open the monthly portfolio disclosure and read every holding. If you are not willing to do that, restrict yourself to gilt, overnight, liquid and corporate bond categories.

When a debt fund beats a fixed deposit, and when it does not

POST-2023: THE HONEST COMPARISON

The Finance Act 2023 removed indexation and the
concessional long-term rate for units of "specified
mutual funds" acquired on or after 1 April 2023.
Gains are treated as short-term and taxed at SLAB
rates regardless of holding period. The Finance
(No. 2) Act 2024 further revised the definition.
*** Verify the current position at
incometax.gov.in - this area has changed twice in
three years. ***

This removed the single biggest historical advantage
of debt funds over fixed deposits.

WORKED: Rs 10,00,000 for 5 years, 30% slab + cess
(effective 31.2%)

FIXED DEPOSIT at 7.00%, interest taxed yearly
  Post-tax rate = 7.00% x (1 - 0.312) = 4.816%
  Value after 5 years
    = 10,00,000 x 1.04816^5 = Rs 12,65,100

DEBT FUND returning 7.30% gross, TER 0.40%
  Net return 6.90%, NO tax until redemption
  Pre-tax value = 10,00,000 x 1.069^5
                = Rs 13,95,900
  Gain          = Rs  3,95,900
  Tax at 31.2% on redemption = Rs 1,23,500
  NET VALUE     = Rs 12,72,400

  ADVANTAGE TO THE DEBT FUND: Rs 7,300 over five
  years, or about 0.12% a year.

WHAT REMAINS OF THE DEBT FUND'S ADVANTAGE
  1. TAX DEFERRAL. An FD is taxed every year on
     accrual with TDS; a debt fund is taxed only
     on redemption. Over long horizons this
     compounding-on-untaxed-money effect is worth
     a few tenths of a percent a year, as above.
  2. LIQUIDITY without penalty. Most debt funds
     have no exit load after a short period; FDs
     charge a premature withdrawal penalty.
  3. CONTROL OF THE TAX YEAR. You choose when to
     realise, so you can redeem in a year when
     your income is lower.
  4. PARTIAL WITHDRAWAL. Redeem exactly what you
     need; an FD usually has to be broken in full
     or in defined units.
  5. Potential for higher return if rates fall.

WHAT THE FD HAS
  1. CERTAINTY. The rate is contractual.
  2. DEPOSIT INSURANCE up to the DICGC limit.
  3. NO MARK-TO-MARKET. It never shows a loss.
  4. Simplicity.

HONEST CONCLUSION
  For a 30% taxpayer with a 3-5 year horizon, the
  two are now close, and the choice should turn on
  whether you value certainty or flexibility more.
  For a taxpayer in the lowest slab the FD is often
  simpler and perfectly adequate. The old
  "debt funds always beat FDs on tax" argument is
  obsolete and you should be suspicious of anyone
  still making it.

WHERE DEBT FUNDS REMAIN CLEARLY BETTER
  - Short-term parking (liquid/overnight) where an
    FD is impractical
  - The debt leg of an asset allocation that needs
    to be rebalanced, since switching within a
    fund house is operationally trivial
  - Arbitrage and equity savings funds, which get
    EQUITY taxation while behaving like short-term
    debt - see section 15. For a high-bracket
    taxpayer this is now often the better
    "debt-like" holding, with the caveat that
    arbitrage returns depend on market spreads.

A passive fund does not try to beat an index; it tries to be one, as cheaply and accurately as possible. The idea is simple, the implementation is not, and the choice between an index fund and an exchange-traded fund has practical consequences in India that are often glossed over.

TWO DIFFERENT NUMBERS, ROUTINELY CONFUSED

TRACKING DIFFERENCE
  = fund return - index return, over a period
  This is what actually HAPPENS TO YOUR MONEY.
  It is normally NEGATIVE, and should be roughly
  equal to the TER plus transaction costs.

TRACKING ERROR
  = the standard deviation of the daily (or
    periodic) return differences, annualised
  This measures how CONSISTENTLY the fund tracks,
  not how much it lags. A fund could have zero
  tracking error and still lag by 1% a year.

    -> cash drag, poor rebalance execution,
       securities lending not passed through,

WHAT TO ACTUALLY COMPARE

     tracking error. AMCs quote tracking error
     because it looks small.
  2. Against the TOTAL RETURN INDEX (TRI), which
     includes dividends. SEBI requires benchmarking
     against TRI. Comparing a fund against a price
     index flatters it by roughly the dividend
     yield.
  3. TER, which is the floor on the lag.

  - TER                          (always)

The iNAV premium and discount problem on Indian exchangesAn ETF's market price is set by supply and demand on the exchange and should track the indicative NAV closely, because authorised participants arbitrage any gap by creating or redeeming units. In India this arbitrage works well for the largest, most liquid ETFs and poorly for the rest. Thinly traded ETFs have repeatedly traded at meaningful premiums and discounts to iNAV, and the problem is worst exactly when you most want to transact — in a sharp market move, when spreads widen and market makers step back. There have been episodes of Indian ETFs trading several percent away from fair value intraday. Practical rules: always check the iNAV published on the AMC and exchange websites before trading; always use limit orders, never market orders; never place an ETF order in the first or last fifteen minutes of the session; and check the average daily traded value before choosing an ETF at all. An index fund avoids every one of these problems because you transact at NAV with the AMC.

Active Versus Passive — What the Evidence Actually Shows

This is the most argued-about question in fund investing and one of the few where real, systematic, independently produced evidence exists for India specifically. The honest conclusion is neither "active management is pointless" nor "India is different because it is an inefficient market". It is more nuanced than either slogan, and it varies sharply by category.

The arithmetic that comes before any data

THE ARITHMETIC OF ACTIVE MANAGEMENT
(this part is not an empirical claim; it is
 definitionally true)

All investors in a market collectively hold the
market. Therefore:

  Average return of all investors, BEFORE costs
    = market return

  Average return of all investors, AFTER costs
    = market return - average costs

Passive investors hold the market at low cost.
Therefore the AVERAGE actively managed rupee must,
by construction, earn:

  market return - (active costs - passive costs)

Active equity funds in India charge roughly 0.5% to
1.2% in direct plans and 1.5% to 2.1% in regular
plans. A broad index fund charges roughly 0.1% to
0.3%. So the average active rupee starts each year
roughly 0.4% to 1.8% behind.

THIS IS NOT A CRITICISM OF FUND MANAGERS. It is
arithmetic. Some managers will beat the index; by
the same arithmetic, the shortfall of the losers
must exceed the excess of the winners by the cost
difference. The questions that remain empirical
are:
  (a) HOW MANY beat the index, and by how much?
  (b) Is outperformance PERSISTENT, so that past
      winners can be identified in advance?
  (c) Does the answer differ by category?

Does outperformance persist?

THE PERSISTENCE QUESTION

Even if some funds beat the index, that is only
useful if you can identify them IN ADVANCE. The
standard test: take the funds in the top quartile
in one period, and see where they rank in the next.

  If performance were purely random, you would
  expect 25% of top-quartile funds to remain in
  the top quartile.

  S&P's Persistence Scorecards for India, and the
  equivalent studies for other markets, have
  repeatedly found that the share of top-quartile
  funds remaining top-quartile over successive
  multi-year periods is LOW - frequently at or
  BELOW what chance alone would produce.

  Over longer horizons, the share of funds that
  stay in the top quartile across three or four
  consecutive periods typically falls to a very
  small number.

WHAT THIS MEANS IN PRACTICE
  Selecting a fund because it was a top performer
  over the last three years has, on the evidence,
  close to no predictive value for the next three.
  This is why section 10 ranks past returns NEAR
  THE BOTTOM of the fund selection criteria, which
  is the opposite of how funds are marketed and
  how star ratings are constructed.

WHY PERSISTENCE IS SO WEAK - three mechanisms
  1. MEAN REVERSION OF STYLE. A fund that
     outperformed did so partly because its style
     (value, momentum, small-cap tilt, a sector
     overweight) was in favour. Styles revert.
  2. ASSET GROWTH. Success attracts inflows.
     A strategy that worked at Rs 2,000 crore may
     not work at Rs 40,000 crore, because the
     manager can no longer take meaningful
     positions in smaller companies without moving
     prices. Many successful Indian small- and
     mid-cap funds have restricted or suspended
     lump-sum inflows for exactly this reason.
  3. COSTS AND COMPETITION. Skill is common and
     the market is competitive; the excess return
     available is small relative to the fee.

Why the large-cap passive case is stronger than the small-cap one

Large caps

Around 100 companies, covered by dozens of analysts each, with high liquidity, constant news flow and institutional dominance. Information advantages are nearly impossible to find, and a 1% fee is a large hurdle against a market where mispricing is small. The passive case is strong and the evidence supports it.

Small caps

Hundreds of companies, many with little or no analyst coverage, far less institutional participation, and genuine information gaps. There is more scope for skill. But there is also more scope for disaster, impact costs are high, capacity constrains the winners, and the dispersion of outcomes between managers is enormous. Even here, most funds have lagged over long horizons.

Debt

Gross returns are low, so costs are a large fraction of them. A 0.8% TER against a 7% gross return is 11% of the return. Active debt managers who add value do so largely by taking credit or duration risk, which you could choose to take directly and more cheaply. The passive and low-cost case is strong.

International

The most developed markets are the most efficient and the most heavily indexed. Access costs for an Indian investor are higher, and RBI limits can suspend inflows. A low-cost index-tracking feeder or fund of funds is the straightforward route.

The balanced conclusion

  • The arithmetic is not in dispute. The average active rupee must underperform the market by the cost difference. This is definitional, not empirical.
  • The Indian evidence is consistent with the arithmetic and is strongest against active management in large-cap equity and in debt.
  • Persistence of outperformance is weak, which means past returns are a poor basis for selection — and past returns are exactly what most investors and most star-rating systems select on.
  • Some managers do add value and some have done so over long periods. The problem is identifying them in advance, not whether they exist.
  • Costs are the one controllable variable. Using direct plans removes 0.5–1.3% a year with certainty and no trade-off whatsoever. This is a larger and more reliable improvement than most fund selection decisions.
  • A defensible position: a low-cost passive core for large-cap and broad-market exposure, with any active exposure deliberately limited to categories where the evidence is least unfavourable, chosen on process and cost rather than on recent returns, and reviewed against the category benchmark rather than against other funds.
  • An equally defensible position: an entirely passive portfolio of two or three broad index funds. It is simpler, cheaper, requires no manager monitoring, and removes an entire category of decision from your life. The evidence does not say you are giving up anything you could reliably have captured.
  • What is not defensible is paying 2% in a regular plan for a fund chosen because it had the highest three-year return, held for eighteen months, and then replaced with the new top performer. That combines the highest costs with the weakest selection criterion and the worst behaviour.

SIP, STP, SWP and Lump Sum

A Systematic Investment Plan is an instruction to a bank, not an investment strategy. It is extremely valuable, but for behavioural reasons rather than the mathematical ones usually claimed. This section sets out the mechanics precisely and then examines rupee cost averaging honestly, including the cases where it costs you money.

The four mechanisms

MechanismWhat it doesTypical use
SIP — Systematic Investment PlanA fixed amount invested into a scheme at a fixed interval from your bank accountAccumulating from monthly income
STP — Systematic Transfer PlanA fixed amount moved periodically from one scheme to another within the same AMCDeploying a lump sum gradually. Park in a liquid or arbitrage fund, transfer to equity over 6–24 months.
SWP — Systematic Withdrawal PlanA fixed amount redeemed periodically and credited to your bank accountGenerating regular income in retirement, far more tax-efficiently than IDCW
Lump sumA single purchaseDeploying a bonus, a maturity, or a windfall
An STP is a redemptionEvery STP instalment is a redemption from the source scheme and a purchase in the target. That means it is a taxable event and may attract an exit load in the source scheme. Transferring from a liquid fund is usually immaterial because gains are small; transferring out of an equity fund that has appreciated is not. Check both tax and exit load before setting up an STP, and note that STP is only available between schemes of the same AMC.

Mandates and the mechanics of an SIP

  1. Register the mandateAn e-mandate (NACH) or UPI AutoPay authorisation permitting the AMC or RTA to debit your account up to a stated maximum amount. Registration takes a few business days; the first instalment may be debited manually.
  2. Choose the date and frequencyMonthly is standard; weekly, fortnightly, quarterly and daily options exist. The date does not matter — studies of SIP date selection across long periods show no meaningful difference, and anyone selling you an "optimal SIP date" is selling noise. Pick a date two to three days after your salary credit so the debit never fails.
  3. Understand the NAV dateUnits are allotted at the NAV of the day on which funds are realised in the scheme account, subject to the cut-off. For a monthly SIP this means the allotment date may be a day or two after the debit date. Over 300 instalments this is irrelevant.
  4. Set the tenureMany platforms default to a fixed number of years. Choose perpetual or the longest available tenure and stop it deliberately when you want to, rather than having it lapse silently at a point you have forgotten about. A lapsed SIP is a surprisingly common and entirely avoidable problem.
  5. Consider a step-upAn annual increase of 5–10% in the instalment, matched to salary growth. The compounding effect is large — see the worked example below.
  6. Keep the bank balance fundedA failed debit may attract a bank charge and, after repeated failures, the SIP registration may be cancelled.

Rupee cost averaging, examined honestly

WHAT RUPEE COST AVERAGING ACTUALLY DOES

By investing a FIXED RUPEE amount, you buy more
units when the NAV is low and fewer when it is
high. Your average cost per unit is therefore the
HARMONIC mean of the NAVs, which is always less
than or equal to the ARITHMETIC mean.

That is a real mathematical property. It is also a
SMALL one, and it is routinely oversold.

THE HONEST CONCLUSION
  Markets rise more often than they fall. Over long
  periods, equity markets trend upward. Therefore
  staggering a lump sum over time will, MORE OFTEN
  THAN NOT, produce a lower return than investing
  it immediately. The research on this - conducted
  across many markets and long periods - is
  consistent: immediate investment beats
  cost-averaging a lump sum roughly two-thirds of
  the time, with the margin growing with the
  staggering period.

SO WHY DO AN SIP AT ALL? Four genuinely good
reasons, NONE of which is rupee cost averaging:

  1. YOU DO NOT HAVE A LUMP SUM. If you earn
     monthly and invest monthly, the lump-sum
     comparison is irrelevant. This describes
     almost every salaried investor, and it is
     the real reason SIPs dominate in India.
  2. IT REMOVES THE DECISION. The single largest
     destroyer of investor returns is the decision
     not to invest, repeatedly, while waiting for
     a better entry. An automated mandate defeats
     procrastination and market timing in one
     instruction.
  3. IT REDUCES REGRET AND THEREFORE ABANDONMENT.
     Deploying a large lump sum a week before a
     20% fall is psychologically devastating and
     frequently causes people to exit at the
     bottom. Staggering produces a lower expected
     return and a higher probability that you are
     still invested in three years. For most
     people that trade is worth making.
  4. IT ENFORCES SAVING. The money leaves before
     you can spend it.

HOW TO DEPLOY AN ACTUAL LUMP SUM
  The evidence favours investing it at once. If
  that is psychologically impossible - and for
  most people it is - use an STP over 6 to 12
  months and accept that you are paying a small
  expected return for a large reduction in regret
  risk. Beyond about 12 months the cost starts to
  outweigh the benefit. Do NOT stagger over three
  years waiting for a correction.

The SIP mistake that actually costs moneyNot the date you choose, not the fund you choose — stopping during a drawdown. An SIP running through a market fall is buying units cheaply, which is precisely when the mechanism works. Stopping at the bottom and restarting after the recovery converts the one genuine advantage of a monthly investment into its opposite: you bought expensively and skipped the cheap period. AMFI data on SIP discontinuation shows closures rising during market stress, which means a large number of Indian investors do exactly this. Write down, now, while markets are calm, that you will not stop your SIP in a falling market. That single pre-commitment is worth more than everything else in this guide.

STANDARD DEVIATION

  roughly two years out of three, the annual return
  fell within 14.20% +/- 16.80%, i.e. between
  -2.6% and +31.0%. That is the RANGE OF EXPERIENCE

  moved about 1.04%. Slightly more volatile than
  the index. A beta materially below 1 in an equity

  = (fund return - risk-free) / standard deviation
  = (14.20 - 6.80) / 16.80

  = 7.40 / 11.20  =  0.66
  Uses only DOWNSIDE volatility, on the sensible

  informative than Sharpe for an asymmetric

  rupees, before you invest: will I continue the

  you are in the wrong category, and no amount of
  fund selection will fix that.

CAPTURE RATIOS
  Upside capture   = fund return in up months
                     / benchmark return in up months
  Downside capture = same, for down months

  Example: upside 97%, downside 88%
  -> the fund captured 97% of the market's gains
     and only 88% of its falls. That combination is
     what good active management actually looks
     like, and it is more informative than the
     headline return.

  Example: upside 112%, downside 118%
  -> the fund is simply a leveraged version of the
     index. Its outperformance in a bull market
     tells you nothing about skill.

ALPHA
  The return not explained by market exposure.
  Quoted widely, unstable statistically, and
  heavily dependent on the benchmark chosen and
  the period measured. Treat with scepticism.

Rolling returns, not point-to-point

WHY POINT-TO-POINT RETURNS MISLEAD

"5-year return: 18.4%" means the return from one
specific start date to one specific end date. Change
either date by three months and the number can move
by several percentage points, because both endpoints
sit somewhere in a market cycle.

ROLLING RETURNS fix this. Compute the 5-year return
starting from EVERY day (or month) in the period,
then look at the distribution.

  HYPOTHETICAL FUND - 5-year rolling returns
  measured over a 10-year history:

    Number of 5-year windows            1,240
    Average                            13.80%
    Median                             13.20%
    Minimum                             4.10%
    Maximum                            24.60%
    % of windows beating the benchmark    58%
    % of windows with a negative return    0%
    % of windows below 8%                 14%

  THIS tells you something. "58% of five-year
  windows beat the benchmark" is a far more honest
  statement than "the fund returned 18.4% over five
  years", and the minimum of 4.1% tells you what a
  bad five-year stretch looked like.

  Most Indian fund platforms now offer rolling
  return charts. Use them. If a platform only shows
  point-to-point returns, it is showing you the
  least informative version of the data.

Portfolio Overlap and Over-Diversification

The most common structural flaw in an Indian retail fund portfolio is owning six to ten equity funds in the belief that this is diversification. In practice it usually reproduces the index, at four to eight times the cost, with the added disadvantage that you now have eight things to monitor and no idea what you actually own.

ELSS and the Tax-Saving Landscape

For two decades an Equity Linked Savings Scheme was the obvious answer to a salaried Indian's tax-saving question: equity exposure, the shortest lock-in of any Section 80C instrument, and a deduction. The default new tax regime removed the deduction, which has changed the case for ELSS fundamentally. Most of the material written about ELSS predates this and is now misleading.

The single most important fact in this sectionSection 80C — and therefore the ELSS deduction, along with PPF, tax-saving FDs, life insurance premiums and ELSS alike — is not available under the new tax regime, which is the default regime. If you have not deliberately opted for the old regime, you are getting no tax benefit from an ELSS investment at all, and you are accepting a three-year lock-in for nothing. Check which regime you are actually in before buying an ELSS. Verify the current position at incometax.gov.in; regime rules and slab structures have been revised repeatedly and are expected to change again with the Income-tax Act, 2025 taking effect from 1 April 2026.

The honest assessment

In favour

Among the lowest charges of any managed retirement product anywhere. The 80CCD(1B) additional deduction has no equivalent. 80CCD(2) survives in the new regime, which is now its most compelling feature. A disciplined glide path. A tax-free 60% lump sum.

Against

Illiquid until 60 with narrowly defined partial withdrawals. Compulsory annuitisation of at least 40% at whatever rate prevails, with the income taxed at slab and usually not inflation-indexed. Equity capped at 75%. Switching pension fund managers is possible but adds friction.

Compared with an equity fund

A direct-plan index fund has no lock-in, no annuity requirement, full equity exposure if you want it, and concessional LTCG taxation. It has no deduction. For someone in the new regime with no employer NPS, the mutual fund route is generally cleaner.

A reasonable conclusion

Use NPS for the 80CCD(1B) amount if you are in the old regime, and for the employer 80CCD(2) contribution in either regime if your employer offers it. Build the rest of your retirement corpus in ordinary funds where you keep control of liquidity and the exit. Treat the annuity requirement as a known cost, not a feature.

The administrative items that actually matter

  1. KYC, and its statusMutual fund KYC is PAN-based and shared across the industry through KYC Registration Agencies. Following SEBI's move towards validated KYC, records that were completed with non-Aadhaar documents may show as "registered" or "on hold" rather than "validated", which can block transactions with a new AMC. Check your KYC status on a KRA website and re-validate if needed — many investors discovered this only when a transaction failed. Verify the current requirements at sebi.gov.in.
  2. NominationSEBI has made nomination (or an explicit opt-out declaration) mandatory for mutual fund folios. Do this for every folio. Without it, your family faces a transmission process requiring succession documentation, which can take months. Up to a prescribed number of nominees with percentage allocations is permitted. Review it after marriage, a birth, or a death in the family.
  3. Consolidated Account StatementCAMS and KFintech jointly issue a CAS covering all your mutual fund folios across every AMC, emailed periodically and available on demand. A separate CAS from the depositories covers demat holdings including stocks, bonds and ETFs. Request both at least annually. The CAS shows the ARN against each folio, which is how you confirm whether you are in direct or regular plans.
  4. Bank mandate hygieneKeep the registered bank account live and funded. If you change banks, update it in every folio — a redemption is credited only to a registered account. MF Central allows a bank change across folios in one request.
  5. Email and mobileThese are how the AMC communicates every material change. Do not register a work email address you will lose when you change employer.
  6. GrievancesComplain to the AMC first. If unresolved, escalate through SEBI SCORES, and thereafter through the SEBI-administered Online Dispute Resolution mechanism. These are free and they work.

Why thematic funds arrive at the worst time — the structural explanation

This is not a conspiracy; it is an incentive structure operating as designed.

  • A sector performs well for two to three years, so its recent return figures become attractive.
  • Attractive recent returns make a product easy to sell, which makes it cheap for an AMC to gather assets in that category.
  • Sectoral and thematic funds are the only equity category exempt from the one-scheme-per-AMC rule, so an AMC may launch as many as it likes.
  • Marketing and distributor effort concentrate on the product that sells, which is the one with the best recent numbers.
  • Investors therefore receive the heaviest promotion for a sector after it has run, and flows peak near the top.
  • Sector returns mean-revert. The investor who arrived at the peak experiences the full drawdown and frequently exits near the bottom, crystallising it.

The defence is simple and entirely within your control: do not buy a fund because it is being promoted, and treat any fund launched into a hot category with the scepticism you would apply to an IPO in a hot sector. If you want sector exposure, you already have it — a broad index fund holds every sector in proportion to its market value, which is a defensible default.

Reviewing, and When to Actually Exit a Fund

Most portfolio reviews are exercises in finding a reason to act. The purpose of a review is the opposite: to confirm that nothing requires action, and to catch the small number of situations that genuinely do. Reviewing more often than annually reliably makes outcomes worse.

Illegitimate reasons that feel legitimate

  • "The market has fallen." You are selling the asset class, not the fund, and you are doing it at the worst time. If the allocation was right at the top it is more right now.
  • "The market is at an all-time high." Markets spend a large share of their time near all-time highs; that is what an upward-trending series does. This is market timing with extra steps.
  • "Another fund did better last year." The persistence evidence in section 8 says that is close to uninformative. Switching on this basis guarantees costs and tax in exchange for a coin flip.
  • "It has been flat for two years." Two flat years inside a seven-year holding period is ordinary. Judge against the benchmark, not against zero.
  • "I want to book profits." Booking profits in an accumulation portfolio means paying tax to convert an appreciating asset into cash you will then have to redeploy, usually at a higher price.
  • "The rating was downgraded from five stars to three." Ratings are backward-looking by construction; the downgrade follows the underperformance you have already experienced.
  • "My relationship manager suggested a better scheme." Ask how they are paid. Then ask for the comparison against the TRI benchmark over rolling five-year windows, in writing.

Frequently Asked Questions

The questions Indian fund investors actually ask, answered directly. None of this is advice; every rate and rule is as at FY 2025-26 / late 2026 and must be verified at sebi.gov.in, amfiindia.com, incometax.gov.in and pfrda.org.in.

Is a fund with a ₹12 NAV cheaper than one with a ₹450 NAV?
No, and the two are not comparable in any way. A unit's NAV is total portfolio value divided by units outstanding, and the unit count depends only on how long the scheme has existed and how much money has flowed in. ₹1,00,000 into a ₹12 NAV fund and ₹1,00,000 into a ₹450 NAV fund with an identical portfolio produce identical outcomes — the worked proof is in section 3. The same point disposes of the idea that a new fund offer at ₹10 is a discount: it is a brand-new portfolio bought at today's prices with no track record.
Direct or regular plan — does it really matter that much?
It is the single largest controllable variable in this guide. On a ₹20,000 monthly SIP over 25 years at 12% gross, a 1.1 percentage point TER difference costs approximately ₹59.5 lakh — close to the entire ₹60 lakh you contributed. The portfolio, manager and strategy are identical; the only difference is distributor commission. Check whether you hold direct plans by looking for the word "Direct" in the scheme name and for an ARN tag in your Consolidated Account Statement. Many "free" apps are distributors selling regular plans; that is how they make money.
Does rupee cost averaging actually work?
Partly, and it is oversold. It is mathematically true that a fixed rupee instalment buys you the harmonic mean of the NAVs, which is at or below the arithmetic mean — in the worked example in section 9 that advantage was 0.93%. But markets rise more often than they fall, so staggering a lump sum over time has historically underperformed investing it immediately roughly two-thirds of the time. The real reasons to run an SIP are different and better: most people invest from monthly income and have no lump sum; the mandate removes the decision and defeats procrastination; and staggering reduces the regret that causes people to abandon equity entirely. Those are strong reasons. The averaging arithmetic is a footnote.
Should I stop my SIP when the market crashes?
No, and this is the single most expensive mistake in Indian retail investing. A falling market is the only time a monthly instalment buys meaningfully more units; stopping then and restarting after the recovery means you bought expensively and skipped the cheap period. AMFI data shows SIP discontinuations rising during market stress, which means a large number of investors do exactly this. The defence is a pre-commitment made while markets are calm: write down now that you will not stop, and reduce how often you look at the portfolio. If a 40% fall would genuinely force you to stop, you are in the wrong asset allocation, which is a different problem.
How many mutual funds should I own?
Fewer than you think. One broad-market index fund is a complete equity portfolio. Two to four schemes covers almost every realistic situation: a broad equity core, perhaps one active or mid/small-cap satellite, and a debt or liquid fund for the fixed-income leg and near-term goals. Beyond five or six equity funds you are almost certainly reproducing the index with heavy overlap at several times the cost — compute the overlap by matching the monthly portfolio disclosures on ISIN, as set out in section 12. Two large-cap funds are typically 55–80% identical by weight.
Index fund or active fund?
The arithmetic is not in dispute: the average active rupee must earn the market return minus the extra cost. The Indian evidence from SPIVA scorecards is consistent with that, and is most unfavourable to active management in large-cap equity and in debt; it is least unfavourable in mid- and small-cap, though most funds have lagged there too over long horizons. Persistence of outperformance is weak, so you cannot reliably identify the winners in advance. A defensible answer is a low-cost passive core with any active exposure deliberately limited, chosen on cost and process rather than recent returns. An entirely passive two- or three-fund portfolio is equally defensible and simpler. Download the current scorecard from spglobal.com/spdji rather than relying on anyone's summary, including this one.
Are debt funds safe? Can they lose money?
They can and do. Two separate risks. Interest-rate risk: a gilt fund with a modified duration of 8.5 loses about 12.75% of value on a 150 basis point yield rise, which against a 6.6% net accrual gives a negative year with zero credit risk. The breakeven calculation — net yield divided by modified duration — tells you how far yields can rise before a year turns negative, and takes thirty seconds. Credit risk: a default is written down in the NAV, and redemptions by other investors then leave the remaining holders with a worse portfolio, which is what made India's 2018–2020 debt fund episodes so damaging. Match duration to your horizon, read the credit quality table, and if you will not read the full portfolio, stay in gilt, overnight, liquid and corporate bond categories.
Are debt funds still better than fixed deposits after the 2023 tax change?
Far less clearly than before. The Finance Act 2023 removed indexation and the concessional long-term rate for most debt funds, which was their main structural advantage. The worked comparison in section 6 — ₹10 lakh for five years at a 30% slab — gives the debt fund an edge of roughly ₹7,300 over five years, or about 0.12% a year, arising purely from tax deferral. What remains in a debt fund's favour is deferral, liquidity without a premature-withdrawal penalty, control over which tax year you realise in, and partial withdrawal. What an FD has is contractual certainty, DICGC insurance up to the limit, and no mark-to-market. For a high-bracket taxpayer wanting debt-like exposure, arbitrage and equity savings funds are now often more efficient than either, because they receive equity taxation — with the caveat that arbitrage returns depend on market spreads.
Index fund or ETF?
For a monthly SIP investor in India, generally the index fund, despite its slightly higher expense ratio. An ETF requires a demat and trading account, trades in whole units at a market price that can diverge from the indicative NAV, and attracts brokerage, exchange charges, GST, STT and the bid-ask spread on every purchase. The premium-and-discount problem is real on Indian exchanges outside the most liquid trackers, and it is worst precisely when markets are stressed. An ETF makes sense for a large lump sum where you can check the iNAV and place a limit order. If you do buy an ETF: check iNAV first, always use limit orders, avoid the first and last fifteen minutes of the session, and check average daily traded value before choosing the ETF at all.
Is ELSS still worth it?
Only if you are in the old tax regime and have unused Section 80C headroom. Section 80C is not available in the new regime, which is the default — so if you have not deliberately opted for the old regime, an ELSS gives you a three-year lock-in and no deduction whatsoever, which is strictly worse than an ordinary diversified equity fund. If you are in the old regime with headroom, ELSS is a strong option: an immediate benefit of roughly 31% of the amount invested at the top slab, with the shortest lock-in of any 80C instrument. Note that the lock-in runs three years from each instalment's allotment, so an SIP started in April 2026 is not fully free until March 2030. Decide which regime suits you first, using your actual numbers on the calculator at incometax.gov.in; the ELSS question is downstream of that.
Should I invest in NPS?
For two specific purposes, yes. First, the 80CCD(1B) additional ₹50,000 deduction if you are in the old regime — nothing else offers it. Second, and more importantly now, the employer contribution under 80CCD(2), which is one of the very few deductions surviving in the new regime, at up to 14% of salary. Against that: the money is locked until 60, at least 40% of the corpus must buy an annuity at whatever rate prevails, and that annuity income is taxed at slab and is usually not inflation-indexed — a level annuity loses roughly half its purchasing power over 25 years at 5% inflation. The charges are genuinely excellent. A reasonable position is to use NPS for those two deduction buckets and build the rest of your retirement corpus in ordinary funds where you retain control of the exit.
Growth or IDCW option?
Growth, for almost everyone. An IDCW payout is not income generated by the fund — it is a distribution out of the scheme's own NAV, which falls by exactly the amount distributed, and it may include a return of your own capital. You receive ₹4, the NAV falls ₹4, and you owe tax on the ₹4 at your slab rate. IDCW reinvestment is worse still: slab-rate tax with no cash received. The growth option defers all tax until you choose to redeem and then taxes it as capital gains at concessional rates. If you need periodic income, use an SWP from the growth option: each withdrawal is part capital and part gain, so only the gain is taxed, and after twelve months at the concessional long-term rate with the annual exemption available. The worked comparison in section 9 shows an SWP beating an equivalent IDCW by roughly ₹1.7 lakh a year on a ₹1 crore corpus.
How do I actually choose between two similar funds?
After fixing the category and insisting on the direct plan, compare in this order: TER against the category median; mandate consistency across twelve monthly portfolio disclosures; maximum drawdown and downside capture, because those describe the experience you will have to survive; portfolio overlap with what you already own; AUM appropriateness for the strategy; manager tenure against the period of the track record being advertised; and only last, rolling returns over three- and five-year windows against the Total Return Index. Past returns come last deliberately — the persistence evidence says they are close to uninformative for selection, though a fund lagging its benchmark for five years does need explaining.
My fund has underperformed for two years. Should I switch?
Almost certainly not yet. Two years is within the normal range for any active strategy, and switching costs you capital gains tax, possibly an exit load, stamp duty and time out of the market — the worked example in section 18 puts a typical switch at around 3.3% of the corpus, which needs roughly four years of 1% annual outperformance to recover. The threshold worth acting on is three to five years of underperformance against the TRI benchmark on rolling returns, with no explanation consistent with the fund's stated process, or a clear mandate drift, or a manager departure in a manager-dependent fund. And before switching for performance, consider whether the honest answer is to move the money to a low-cost index fund rather than to another active fund chosen on the same criterion that failed you.
What happens to my money if the AMC goes under?
Your units are not an asset of the AMC. An Indian mutual fund is a trust; the trustees hold the scheme's securities for unitholders, a separate SEBI-registered custodian physically holds them, and an independent RTA maintains the records. Scheme assets are therefore not available to the AMC's creditors. The trustees can terminate the AMC's appointment, and in practice Indian AMCs that have been sold or wound down have had their schemes transferred or merged, with unitholders given a load-free exit option. What this structure does not protect you from is the performance of the securities themselves — if the bonds in a debt fund default, you lose money, and there is no deposit insurance equivalent for mutual funds. That is market risk, and it is the risk you signed up for.

  • Market-cap composition checked against the category label
  • Top-10 concentration computed; cash holding checked for a persistent drag
  • Sector bets against the benchmark noted and consciously accepted
  • For debt funds: credit quality table read, YTM compared against the category, Macaulay and modified duration noted, breakeven yield rise computed
  • For debt funds: the full monthly portfolio read holding by holding, or the category restricted to gilt, overnight, liquid or corporate bond
  • Potential Risk Class matrix (debt) and riskometer level noted

E. Risk and performance (6 items)

  • Maximum drawdown converted into rupees on your intended investment, and the honest question asked: would I continue the SIP at that point?
  • Standard deviation and beta compared against the category
  • Sharpe and Sortino compared against the benchmark, not quoted in isolation
  • Upside and downside capture ratios checked — the combination is more informative than the headline return
  • Rolling three- and five-year returns reviewed, against the Total Return Index
  • Percentage of rolling windows beating the benchmark noted, rather than a single point-to-point figure

F. Fit with what you already own (4 items)

  • Portfolio overlap computed against existing holdings; anything above 50% challenged
  • The specific new exposure this fund provides can be named in one sentence
  • Total scheme count after the addition is five or fewer
  • AUM appropriate for the strategy; stress test disclosure checked for mid- and small-cap funds

G. Manager, AMC and admin (6 items)

  • Fund manager tenure compared against the period of the track record being advertised
  • Performance of the manager's other schemes checked — SEBI requires this disclosure
  • AMC's history on credit events, mandate discipline and whether it closes funds at capacity considered
  • KYC status confirmed as validated with a KRA
  • Nomination registered on the folio
  • Bank mandate, email and mobile number current and not tied to an employer
