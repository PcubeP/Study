What a Share Actually Is

Before any discussion of brokers, order types or settlement, one idea has to be solid: a share is a legal claim on a fraction of a real business. Everything else in this guide — the exchanges, the clearing corporations, the depositories, the circuit filters — is plumbing built to make that claim transferable safely. If the claim is not understood, the plumbing is just noise.

Where the value comes from

THE ONLY THING THAT ULTIMATELY GIVES A SHARE VALUE

A share is worth the present value of all the cash the
business will return to its owners, from now until it
ceases to exist.

        PV  =  sum over t of  [ CF(t) / (1 + r)^t ]

  CF(t) = free cash flow attributable to equity in year t
  r     = the rate of return investors demand, given risk

Two things move a share price, and only two:
  1. A CHANGE IN EXPECTED FUTURE CASH FLOWS
       - better or worse earnings
       - a new contract, a lost customer, a regulatory change
       - margin expansion or compression
  2. A CHANGE IN THE DISCOUNT RATE r
       - interest rates (RBI repo rate, G-Sec yields)
       - perceived risk of the business
       - the general appetite for risk among investors

EVERYTHING ELSE you read about — FII flows, "technical
breakouts", news sentiment, index inclusion, a bonus issue —
operates THROUGH one of those two channels, or is noise.

WORKED: WHY A RATE RISE HURTS A "GROWTH" STOCK MORE

  Company A: steady ₹100 cr free cash flow, flat forever
  Company B: ₹20 cr now, growing 25% a year for 10 years,
             then flat

  At r = 10%:
     A is worth 100/0.10              = ₹1,000 cr
     B's cash flows are back-loaded; most of its value sits
     in years 8-20. Say it is worth   ≈ ₹1,050 cr

  Raise r to 13% (say G-Sec yields rise 300 bps):
     A is worth 100/0.13              = ₹  769 cr   (-23%)
     B, with value further out in time, is discounted
     harder: say                      ≈ ₹  680 cr   (-35%)

  NEITHER COMPANY'S BUSINESS CHANGED AT ALL.
  Only r changed. This is why the whole market can fall
  10% on an inflation print, and why long-duration growth
  stocks fall more than cash-generating ones when rates rise.

The point that surprises most beginnersWhen you buy 50 shares of a listed Indian company on NSE, the company receives nothing. Your money goes to the person who sold. But it explains why "supporting a company by buying its shares" is a misunderstanding.

Why prices move — the microstructure answer

THE MECHANICAL TRUTH ABOUT PRICE

A share price is simply THE PRICE OF THE LAST TRADE.
Nothing more. It is not a valuation, not a consensus,
not an official figure. It is one transaction between
one buyer and one seller.

AT ANY MOMENT THE ORDER BOOK LOOKS LIKE THIS:

   BIDS (buyers)                ASKS (sellers)
   Qty      Price               Price      Qty
   1,240    ₹1,248.50           ₹1,248.75  860
   3,100    ₹1,248.25           ₹1,249.00  2,400
   890      ₹1,248.00           ₹1,249.25  1,150
   5,600    ₹1,247.75           ₹1,249.50  7,300

   Best bid ₹1,248.50   Best ask ₹1,248.75   Spread ₹0.25

The price "moves up" when a buyer is willing to pay the
ask, consuming the sell orders at ₹1,248.75, then
₹1,249.00, and so on. The last traded price becomes
whatever level the buying stopped at.

SO THE PRICE RISES WHEN:
  buyers are more AGGRESSIVE (willing to cross the spread
  and lift offers) than sellers are at that moment.

It does NOT rise because "more people bought than sold."
Every trade has exactly one buyer and one seller, always.
The number of shares bought ALWAYS equals the number sold.
What changes is the URGENCY on each side.

CONSEQUENCE FOR A RETAIL INVESTOR:
  In a thin stock, your own order can move the price.
  Placing a market order for 20,000 shares in a stock that
  trades 40,000 shares a day will walk the book up several
  percent — and you will pay every step of it. See section 6.

Novation — why you never worry about who sold to you

WHAT NOVATION ACTUALLY DOES

BEFORE NOVATION
   You  <--- a contract ---> Some anonymous stranger
   If the stranger does not deliver shares, you have a
   problem with a person you cannot identify.

AFTER NOVATION (automatic, on every exchange trade)
   You  <--- contract ---> CLEARING CORPORATION
   Clearing Corporation <--- contract ---> The seller

   The single contract is legally replaced by two contracts.
   The clearing corporation is now:
       the SELLER to every buyer, and
       the BUYER to every seller.

WHY THIS MATTERS
   If the seller fails to deliver, THE CLEARING CORPORATION
   STILL DELIVERS TO YOU. It then recovers from the defaulter
   through an auction (see section 7) and through the
   defaulter's margins.

HOW IT PROTECTS ITSELF
   - Upfront margin collected from brokers before orders are
     accepted (VaR margin + Extreme Loss Margin + others)
   - Mark-to-market margin collected daily
   - Core Settlement Guarantee Fund (Core SGF) contributed to
     by the clearing corporation, the exchange and members
   - A documented "default waterfall": defaulter's margins ->
     defaulter's SGF contribution -> clearing corp's own
     "skin in the game" -> remaining SGF -> other members

CONSEQUENCE: in the Indian equity cash segment, a retail
buyer has never had to chase a defaulting seller. This is
genuinely world-class market infrastructure, and it is why
you can transact with a stranger for lakhs of rupees
without knowing their name.

WHAT IT DOES NOT PROTECT AGAINST
   - Your BROKER misusing your securities or funds
   - Your own bad decisions
   - A company's business failing
   See section 4 for broker-level protections.

  3. SEBI SCORESThe centralised online complaint system at scores.gov.in. Complaints are routed to the intermediary with deadlines and escalation.

Linking it all together

HOW MONEY AND SHARES ACTUALLY MOVE

BUYING
  1. You transfer funds from your BANK to the broker's
     designated client bank account (UPI, net banking,
     NEFT/RTGS/IMPS). Some brokers charge ₹9-₹20 for
     payment gateway transfers; UPI is usually free.
  2. The funds show as "available margin" in your TRADING
     account. This is NOT your bank account — it is money
     held by the broker in a client account, segregated
     from the broker's own funds.
  3. You place a buy order. On execution, the obligation
     is created.
  4. On settlement, funds move to the clearing corporation
     and shares are credited to your DEMAT account.

SELLING
  1. You place a sell order. SEBI's framework requires
     upfront verification that the shares exist — brokers
     use either an early pay-in or a pledge of the shares
     to the clearing corporation.
  2. On execution, your DEMAT is debited at pay-in.
  3. On settlement, funds are credited to your TRADING
     account, less all charges.
  4. You WITHDRAW from the trading account to your BANK
     account. This is a separate step and typically takes
     one working day.

IMPORTANT: RUNNING ACCOUNT SETTLEMENT
  SEBI requires brokers to settle (transfer back to your
  bank) any unused funds lying in your trading account on
  a periodic basis — monthly or quarterly, per your
  election, commonly on the first Friday of the month or
  quarter. This prevents brokers from holding client money
  indefinitely. You will see an automatic bank credit; it
  is not an error.

  Verify the current running-account settlement norms at
  sebi.gov.in — they have been tightened more than once.

  • What is the all-in cost for my pattern? If you place four delivery buys a year and sell twice, brokerage is nearly irrelevant and DP charges plus AMC dominate. Model your actual usage, not the headline rate.
  • How does the platform behave on a volatile day? Indian brokers have had publicised outages during high-volume sessions. Check whether the broker publishes uptime, whether there is a backup web terminal, and whether call-and-trade is reachable. An outage on a normal day is an annoyance; an outage while you hold a leveraged position is a loss.
  • Is there a genuine escalation path? A named grievance officer, a published escalation matrix, and a realistic response time. Test it with a trivial query before you fund the account heavily.
  • Does the broker push products? Notice whether the app surfaces "trending stocks", F&O prompts, margin offers and streak-style gamification. That design exists to increase your order count, and your order count is their revenue.
  • Direct or regular mutual funds? If you plan to buy funds through the broker, confirm the plans are direct. Some platforms route to regular plans and earn trail commission.
  • How easy is it to leave? Check the closure process and the cost of transferring holdings out (inter-depository or intra-depository transfer charges). SEBI has pushed brokers to simplify this.
  • Does the broker separate client funds properly? SEBI requires segregation of client funds and securities, upstreaming of client funds to clearing corporations, and daily/weekly reporting. Read the broker's disclosures.
The protections that exist, and the one that does notSEBI has progressively hardened client-asset protection: client securities must be held in the client's own demat account (the Margin Pledge / Re-pledge system replaced the old practice of brokers holding client shares in a pool), client funds must be segregated and upstreamed, and there is a daily reporting regime. Exchanges maintain an Investor Protection Fund that compensates investors up to a prescribed limit if a broker is declared a defaulter. What does not exist is deposit-insurance-style protection for the market value of your portfolio: if the stock you own falls 80%, no one compensates you. The IPF protects against broker default, not against market loss. Verify the current IPF limit with the relevant exchange.

Multiple brokers — a practical consideration

Many experienced investors keep two broker accounts: a primary one and a dormant backup. The reason is operational, not financial — if the primary platform is down on a day when you need to act, the backup gives you access. The costs are a second AMC (unless waived) and the loss of BSDA eligibility, which requires a single demat account. Weigh that against the value of redundancy for your situation.

The pre-open call auction, in detail

HOW THE OPENING PRICE IS DISCOVERED

WHY A CALL AUCTION AT ALL?
  Overnight, news accumulates: results, global markets,
  policy announcements. If trading simply started at 09:15
  with continuous matching, the first few trades would be
  chaotic and easily manipulated. The call auction collects
  ALL the overnight intent and resolves it at ONE price.

THE ALGORITHM (simplified)
  1. Collect all limit and market orders during 09:00-09:08.
  2. For every possible price, compute how many shares
     would trade if that price were chosen.
  3. Choose the price with MAXIMUM EXECUTABLE QUANTITY.
  4. Tie-break 1: the price leaving the minimum unmatched
     quantity.
  5. Tie-break 2: the price closest to the previous close.
  6. ALL matched orders execute at that SINGLE price,
     regardless of the limit price they specified
     (provided it was better than the equilibrium price).

WORKED EXAMPLE

  Previous close: ₹500

  Buy orders collected          Sell orders collected
  ----------------------        ------------------------
  2,000 @ ₹512 (limit)          1,500 @ ₹505 (limit)
  3,000 @ market                2,500 @ ₹508 (limit)
  1,000 @ ₹506 (limit)          4,000 @ ₹512 (limit)
    800 @ ₹502 (limit)          1,000 @ market

  Test ₹508:
    Willing buyers at ₹508 or above:
       2,000 (@512) + 3,000 (market) = 5,000
    Willing sellers at ₹508 or below:
       1,500 (@505) + 2,500 (@508) + 1,000 (market) = 5,000
    EXECUTABLE QUANTITY = 5,000

  Test ₹506:
    Buyers: 2,000 + 3,000 + 1,000 = 6,000
    Sellers: 1,500 + 1,000 = 2,500
    EXECUTABLE = 2,500

  Test ₹512:
    Buyers: 2,000 + 3,000 = 5,000
    Sellers: 1,500 + 2,500 + 4,000 + 1,000 = 9,000
    EXECUTABLE = 5,000  (tie with ₹508)
    Tie-break 1: unmatched at ₹512 = 4,000;
                 unmatched at ₹508 = 0
    ₹508 WINS.

  OPENING PRICE = ₹508. All 5,000 shares trade at ₹508.

  The person who bid ₹512 gets filled at ₹508 — BETTER
  than their limit. This is a property of call auctions:
  you never pay worse than your limit, and often better.

KEY PRACTICAL POINTS
  - A MARKET order in the pre-open executes at the
    equilibrium price, whatever it turns out to be.
    In a gap-up or gap-down this can be far from the
    previous close. Use LIMIT orders in the pre-open.
  - Unmatched orders carry forward into the continuous
    session at 09:15 (limit orders retain their price;
    market orders convert to limit at the open price,
    per the current exchange rules).
  - The pre-open applies to the equity cash segment.
    A separate pre-open call auction applies to newly
    listed IPO shares on listing day (see section 12).
Why the first fifteen minutes are the worst time for a beginner to tradeVolatility and spreads are widest at the open, as overnight information is absorbed and institutional algorithms work large orders. For a long-term investor placing a delivery order, there is no advantage whatsoever to transacting at 09:15 rather than 11:30 or 14:00 — and there is a real disadvantage in wider spreads and more erratic prices. If you must trade on a given day, the middle of the session is generally calmer. Better still: place a limit order and let it fill when it fills.

Holidays and special sessions

  • The exchanges publish an annual trading holiday list in advance at nseindia.com and bseindia.com. It is not identical to the bank holiday list, and settlement holidays are a separate list again — which matters for when your money actually arrives.
  • Muhurat trading: a special one-hour (approximately) ceremonial session held on Diwali Laxmi Pujan, treated as the start of the Hindu accounting year Samvat. It is a full trading session — trades settle normally — but volumes are low, spreads are wide, and it is a poor session in which to execute anything meaningful. Treat it as tradition, not as an opportunity.
  • Special live trading sessions: exchanges occasionally conduct live sessions on a Saturday to test disaster-recovery sites. These are real sessions with real settlement; check the circular before participating.
  • Budget day: when the Union Budget falls on a Saturday, exchanges have historically conducted a special live session.
  • If you hold a position across a long weekend, remember that settlement is based on trading days, not calendar days — a Thursday sale may not credit funds until the following Monday or Tuesday if Friday is a holiday.

Impact cost — the metric nobody checks

Impact cost is published, and you can look it upNSE publishes impact cost for securities — the percentage cost of executing a standard order size (historically ₹1 lakh for index eligibility purposes) relative to the ideal mid-price. It is one of the criteria for inclusion in the Nifty 50 and other indices. A stock with an impact cost of 0.02% is deeply liquid; one with 1.5% is not. Before buying anything outside the large-cap universe, check its average daily traded value and its delivery percentage on nseindia.com. If the stock trades ₹80 lakh a day and you want to buy ₹5 lakh, you are a meaningful part of the day's volume — and getting out will be harder than getting in.

The rules that govern matching

  1. Price priorityThe better price always executes first. A bid of ₹500.25 is ahead of a bid of ₹500.20 regardless of when each arrived.
  2. Time priorityAmong orders at the same price, the one entered first executes first. This is why modifying an order's price sends it to the back of the queue at the new price.
  3. Tick sizeThe minimum price increment. SEBI and the exchanges moved to a graded tick size regime where the tick depends on the price band of the stock — ₹0.01 for lower-priced scrips with finer increments for very low-priced ones. Verify the current tick-size circular.
  4. Price bandsAn order outside the applicable circuit band is rejected by the exchange, not held. See section 8.
  5. Order quantity limitsExchanges set a maximum order value and quantity per order for each scrip. Large orders must be split.
  6. AnonymityYou never learn who the counterparty is, and they never learn who you are. This is a deliberate design choice that prevents counterparty discrimination.
The practical rule for a long-term investorUse limit orders, in the CNC/delivery product type, in the middle of the session, in stocks or ETFs with meaningful daily volume. Set the limit at or very slightly above the best ask if you want a near-certain fill, or at the mid-price if you are patient. You give up the thrill of instant execution and you keep the 0.5%–3% that a market order in a thin book would have taken from you. Over a lifetime of transactions, this single habit is worth more than most stock selection.

ETF-specific order hazards

WHY MARKET ORDERS IN ETFs ARE ESPECIALLY DANGEROUS

An ETF has an underlying fair value — the iNAV (indicative
Net Asset Value) — published by the AMC and the exchange
through the trading day. In a liquid ETF the market price
tracks iNAV closely because authorised participants arbitrage
any gap.

In an ILLIQUID Indian ETF, that arbitrage can break down:
  - low on-screen volume
  - wide spreads (sometimes 1-3%)
  - occasional "freak trades" far from fair value
  - in capped international ETFs, SUSTAINED PREMIUMS of
    5-20% above iNAV, because the AMC cannot create new
    units while the overseas investment limit is breached

CONSEQUENCE
  A market order can execute 10% above fair value. You have
  paid ₹110 for ₹100 of assets, and that ₹10 does not come
  back — it erodes as the premium normalises.

WHAT TO DO
  1. Find the iNAV. The AMC publishes it; the exchange
     displays it on the quote page.
  2. Compare the market price to the iNAV. If the premium
     is more than about 0.5%, do not transact.
  3. ALWAYS use a limit order, set near the iNAV.
  4. Check the ETF's average daily traded value. Some listed
     Indian ETFs trade a few lakh rupees a day.
  5. If the ETF is persistently at a large premium, consider
     whether an equivalent INDEX FUND (bought at NAV, no
     premium, no spread) solves the same problem.

Settlement

Settlement is the process by which the trade you executed becomes an actual transfer of shares and money. India runs one of the fastest settlement cycles in the world, and understanding it answers a cluster of beginner questions: why the money is not in my bank, why I cannot sell what I just bought in certain segments, and what "auction" means on my contract note.

Settlement cycle as at late 2026 — verify at sebi.gov.inIndia completed its phased transition to a T+1 rolling settlement cycle for the equity cash segment in January 2023. SEBI subsequently introduced an optional T+0 (same-day) settlement cycle for a specified set of scrips in a beta phase from March 2024, with a stated intention to expand it. The scope, scrip list and broker participation in T+0 have evolved; a move toward optional instantaneous settlement has also been discussed. Check the current circular — the mechanics below describe the framework, not a guaranteed current state.

Short delivery and auction settlement

WHAT HAPPENS WHEN A SELLER FAILS TO DELIVER

THE SITUATION
  Someone sold shares they did not actually have in their
  demat account at pay-in time. Causes:
    - BTST gone wrong (they were themselves short-delivered)
    - Shares were pledged and not released in time
    - Operational error at the DP
    - Deliberate naked short selling (prohibited for
      retail in the Indian cash segment)

THE CLEARING CORPORATION'S RESPONSE
  It does NOT leave the buyer short. It runs an AUCTION
  on T+1 (under T+1 settlement the auction session has
  been moved to align with the shorter cycle — verify the
  current timing with the exchange).

  In the auction, the clearing corporation BUYS the missing
  shares from other market participants who offer them, and
  delivers them to the buyer who was short-delivered.

WHO PAYS
  The DEFAULTING SELLER pays. The cost is:
     auction purchase price  +  penalty
  and the penalty structure is deliberately punitive.

THE CLOSE-OUT FORMULA (indicative — verify current rules)
  If no shares are available in the auction, the trade is
  "closed out" and the defaulting seller pays the buyer
  a price computed as the HIGHER of:
     (a) the highest price of the scrip from the trade day
         to the auction day, PLUS a markup (historically 20%
         for the cash segment close-out), or
     (b) the closing price on the auction day plus the markup

  In practice, failing to deliver can cost the seller
  20% or more above the market price, plus the auction
  penalty levied by the exchange.

WORKED EXAMPLE
  You sell 200 shares on Monday at ₹640 (BTST — you bought
  them Monday morning and had not received delivery).
  Your seller short-delivers to you, so you cannot deliver.

  Auction on T+1. Highest price Monday-Tuesday: ₹672.
  Close-out price = 672 x 1.20 = ₹806.40
  You receive your sale proceeds of ₹1,28,000
  You are debited ₹1,61,280
  NET LOSS: ₹33,280 on a ₹1,28,000 trade,
  with no price movement against you at all.

HOW TO NEVER FACE THIS
  1. Do not sell shares you have not received in demat.
     Wait for the credit. One day of patience.
  2. If you must do BTST, do it only in highly liquid
     largecaps where short delivery is vanishingly rare.
  3. Never sell pledged shares without first unpledging.
  4. Check your demat holdings, not just the broker app's
     "holdings" view, which may show T+1 positions.

SEBI's upfront securities-verification framework (early
pay-in or pledge to the clearing corporation at the time
of sale) has made retail short delivery much rarer than
it once was — but BTST still carries the risk.

The ASBA-like block mechanism for the secondary market

UPI block (ASBA for secondary market trading)SEBI introduced a facility under which a retail investor's funds remain blocked in their own bank account through a UPI mandate rather than being transferred to the broker, with the blocked amount debited only on settlement. This is the same principle that already governs IPO applications (where the money stays in your account until allotment). The intent is to remove the need to park funds with a broker at all, eliminating a category of broker-default risk. Adoption has been phased and participation varies by broker. Check whether your broker supports it and verify the current framework at sebi.gov.in. For an investor who transacts rarely, this materially reduces counterparty exposure.

Can I sell shares the same day I buy them, in the delivery (CNC) product type?

Yes in most scrips, and it is a different thing from an intraday MIS trade. If you buy in CNC and sell the same day, your broker will typically treat the pair as an intraday trade for margin and STT purposes — the positions net off and no delivery occurs, so you pay intraday STT of 0.025% on the sell side instead of 0.1% on both legs. If you buy today and sell tomorrow before the shares have been credited to your demat, that is BTST, and it carries genuine short-delivery risk: if the person who sold to you fails to deliver, you cannot deliver to your buyer, and your trade goes to auction with a close-out that can be the highest price over the period plus a 20% markup. Two important exceptions. First, in trade-for-trade (BE series / T group) scrips, same-day buy-and-sell is not permitted to net off — both legs settle separately and you must deliver shares you do not have, which sends you straight to auction. Second, some brokers restrict same-day CNC selling in specific scrips under their own risk policy. Check the series before you do it, and if you are a long-term investor the question should rarely arise.

The most common SIP confusionYour SIP debit date and your NAV allotment date are different. The money leaves your bank on the mandate date, the AMC receives it a day or two later depending on the payment rail, and units are allotted at the NAV of the day the funds are realised. In a volatile week this can mean a 2% difference from the NAV you expected. It is not an error and it averages out over years. If you want certainty of the allotment date, check your AMC's stated realisation timelines.

Indian exchanges do not allow prices to move without limit. If a stock I hold enters GSM, should I sell immediately?

There is no general answer, and selling into a surveillance-triggered decline is often selling at the worst price. What the GSM entry does tell you is factual and useful: the exchange's financial screens have flagged that the price is not supported by the company's net worth, earnings or book value. That is information about the business, not just about the trading pattern. Practically: read the company's last two annual reports and the latest quarterly filing; check promoter shareholding and pledging; check whether the auditor has issued a qualified opinion or resigned. Then decide on the business, not on the GSM tag. Separately, understand the mechanical constraint you now face — at higher GSM stages the stock may trade only in a periodic call auction once a week, an Additional Surveillance Deposit of up to 100% of trade value may be blocked for months, and the price band may be 2%. If you do decide to exit, you may need many sessions to do it, and you should use limit orders in the permitted window rather than attempting to dump the position. The broader lesson is preventive: the sixty-second pre-purchase check above is what stops you ever being in this position.

Price return versus total return

PRI vs TRI — THE DIFFERENCE IS THE DIVIDEND

  PRICE RETURN INDEX (PRI)
     Tracks only price movement. When a constituent goes
     ex-dividend, its price drops by the dividend amount
     and THE INDEX FALLS. The dividend is simply lost
     from the index.

  TOTAL RETURN INDEX (TRI)
     Assumes every dividend is reinvested into the index
     on the ex-date. Captures the full economic return.

  THE GAP = the dividend yield, compounded.

  Indian large-cap dividend yield has typically run around
  1.0%-1.5%. Over long periods this compounds meaningfully:

     Over 20 years at a 1.3% annual gap:
        (1.013)^20 = 1.294
        TRI ends 29.4% higher than PRI

  WHY THIS MATTERS TO YOU
  1. The "Nifty 50" level you see on TV and in news headlines
     is the PRICE return index. Your index FUND, however,
     receives and reinvests dividends, so its NAV should
     track the TOTAL return index.
  2. SEBI requires mutual funds to benchmark against TRI,
     precisely because comparing a fund (which gets dividends)
     against PRI (which does not) flattered active managers
     by roughly 1-1.5% a year. This rule change in 2018
     materially changed how many funds appeared to "beat
     the index".
  3. When someone claims a fund "beat the Nifty", ask:
     beat the PRI or the TRI? The answer changes the
     conclusion in many cases.

Index rebalancing and its market impact

WHAT HAPPENS WHEN A STOCK ENTERS OR EXITS AN INDEX

THE MECHANISM
  Index funds and ETFs tracking the index MUST hold the
  constituents in index weights to minimise tracking error.
  When the index changes, they must trade — and they must
  trade AT or very near the effective date, because that
  is when the index itself changes.

  This is FORCED, PRICE-INSENSITIVE demand or supply.

THE TYPICAL PATTERN
  1. ANNOUNCEMENT (usually 4 weeks before effective date)
     NSE Indices publishes the inclusions and exclusions.
  2. ANTICIPATION PHASE
     Traders who had positioned in advance, and those who
     react to the announcement, buy the incoming stock and
     sell the outgoing one. The incoming stock often rises
     several percent over the following weeks.
  3. EFFECTIVE DATE
     Index funds execute, usually at or near the closing
     price of the day before the change takes effect
     (to match the index's own calculation). Volume on
     that close can be many multiples of a normal day.
  4. REVERSAL
     Academic work across markets has repeatedly found
     that a significant part of the inclusion gain reverses
     over the following weeks, because the demand was
     technical rather than fundamental.

HOW LARGE IS THE FLOW?
  If total assets tracking the Nifty 50 are, say,
  ₹X crore, and the new entrant has a 0.9% index weight,
  then index funds must collectively buy 0.9% of ₹X crore
  of that stock, within a narrow window. For a mid-sized
  company this can be many days of its normal volume.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU
  - If you hold an index FUND, rebalancing happens inside
    the fund. You do nothing. The cost appears as a small
    amount of tracking difference.
  - Buying a stock purely because it is "entering the
    Nifty" is a crowded, well-known trade with documented
    post-inclusion reversal. It is speculation on a
    technical flow, not investing.
  - Index inclusion does NOT make a company better. The
    business is identical the day before and the day after.

THE SAME LOGIC APPLIES TO:
  - MSCI and FTSE index reviews, which drive FOREIGN
    passive flows into Indian stocks on announced dates
  - Changes in free-float factors (a promoter selling a
    stake raises the free float and therefore the weight)
  - F&O inclusion/exclusion lists
Tracking error and tracking differenceTwo different things, often confused. Tracking difference is the gap between the fund's return and the index's return over a period — driven by the expense ratio, cash drag, rebalancing costs and securities lending income. It is the number that affects your wealth. Tracking error is the volatility of that gap — the standard deviation of the daily return differences. A fund can have low tracking error and still consistently lag by 0.7% a year. When comparing index funds, look at the tracking difference over 3 and 5 years, which several AMCs now disclose, and at the expense ratio. A broad index fund with a 0.10% expense ratio and 0.15% tracking difference is doing its job.

A corporate action is anything a company does that changes the shares you hold or pays you something on account of them. Most are value-neutral on the day — the market adjusts the price mechanically — and understanding that prevents a lot of confused panic when your holding suddenly shows a 50% "loss" the morning after a stock split.

Dividends

DIVIDEND — WHAT ACTUALLY HAPPENS TO YOUR WEALTH

SITUATION
  You hold 400 shares, trading at ₹620.
  Portfolio value: ₹2,48,000
  Company declares a dividend of ₹12 per share.

ON THE EX-DATE, the exchange adjusts the reference price:
  Opening reference = 620 - 12 = ₹608

AFTER THE EX-DATE (assuming no other price movement)
  Shares        400 x ₹608 = ₹2,43,200
  Dividend due  400 x ₹12  = ₹   4,800
                             ----------
  TOTAL                      ₹2,48,000

  YOUR WEALTH IS UNCHANGED. The company moved ₹4,800 from
  its bank account to yours, and the share price fell by
  exactly that amount because the company is now worth
  ₹4,800 less (per your stake).

THEN TAX HAPPENS
  Dividends are taxed in the hands of the investor at
  SLAB RATE (as at FY 2025-26). At a 31.2% effective rate:
    Tax on ₹4,800 = ₹1,498
    NET in hand  = ₹3,302
    TDS u/s 194 is deducted by the company if total
    dividend from that company exceeds the annual
    threshold (₹10,000 as at FY 2025-26 — VERIFY, this
    threshold has been revised).

  POST-DIVIDEND WEALTH: ₹2,43,200 + ₹3,302 = ₹2,46,502
  YOU ARE ₹1,498 POORER THAN BEFORE THE DIVIDEND.

THE IMPLICATION
  For a taxpayer in the 30% slab, a dividend is a FORCED,
  TAXABLE event. A company that retains and reinvests
  profits, or buys back shares, defers your tax liability
  and lets the money compound pre-tax inside the business.

  This is why "dividend yield investing" is less
  attractive for a high-slab Indian investor than it
  appears, and why the pre-2020 dividend distribution
  tax regime (where the company paid DDT and the
  dividend was exempt in your hands) was structurally
  different.

  NOTE: this is a structural observation about taxation,
  NOT a recommendation about any investment style.

    Bought 400 shares at ₹500 in Jan 2024 = ₹2,00,000
    1:1 bonus allotted Mar 2026 -> 400 bonus shares, cost ₹0
    Sell all 800 in June 2026 at ₹330 = ₹2,64,000

    Original 400: sold at ₹330, cost ₹500, held > 12 months
        LTCG = 400 x (330 - 500) = -₹68,000  (a LONG-TERM LOSS)
    Bonus 400: sold at ₹330, cost ₹0, held < 12 months
        STCG = 400 x 330 = ₹1,32,000  (SHORT-TERM GAIN)

    The short-term gain cannot be offset by... actually a
    LONG-TERM LOSS CANNOT be set off against a SHORT-TERM
    GAIN. (Short-term loss CAN offset both.)
    So you pay 20% on ₹1,32,000 = ₹26,400 of tax, while
    carrying forward a ₹68,000 long-term loss.

    ECONOMICALLY you received ₹2,64,000 for something that
    cost ₹2,00,000 — a ₹64,000 gain. You are taxed on
    ₹1,32,000. This "bonus stripping" asymmetry is real;
    s.94(8) of the Income-tax Act contains an anti-avoidance
    provision for deliberate bonus stripping. Consult a CA.

Stock split

STOCK SPLIT — DIVIDING THE FACE VALUE

A split reduces the FACE VALUE of each share and issues
proportionally more shares. Unlike a bonus, reserves are
not capitalised — only the face value changes.

WORKED: a split from ₹10 face value to ₹2 face value (1:5)

  BEFORE
    Shares         400
    Face value     ₹10
    Price          ₹620
    Value          ₹2,48,000

  AFTER
    Shares         2,000     (400 x 5)
    Face value     ₹2
    Price          ₹124      (620 / 5)
    Value          ₹2,48,000  <-- IDENTICAL

TAX TREATMENT — BETTER THAN A BONUS
  Your original cost is SPREAD across the new share count,
  and the HOLDING PERIOD IS PRESERVED from the original
  purchase date.

  Bought 400 at ₹500 = ₹2,00,000
  After a 1:5 split: 2,000 shares, cost ₹100 each
  Holding period runs from the ORIGINAL purchase date.

BONUS vs SPLIT — THE PRACTICAL DIFFERENCE
  +----------------+------------------+-------------------+
  |                | BONUS            | SPLIT             |
  +----------------+------------------+-------------------+
  | Face value     | Unchanged        | Reduced           |
  | Reserves       | Capitalised      | Unchanged         |
  | Cost of new    | ZERO             | Apportioned       |
  |   shares       |                  |                   |
  | Holding period | STARTS FRESH     | PRESERVED         |
  |   of new shares|                  |                   |
  | Tax outcome    | Can create a     | Neutral           |
  |                | large artificial |                   |
  |                | short-term gain  |                   |
  +----------------+------------------+-------------------+

NEITHER CREATES VALUE. A pizza cut into eight slices is
not more pizza than one cut into four. Indian retail
investors frequently bid up stocks ahead of a split or
bonus announcement; there is no economic basis for this.

Rights issue

RIGHTS ISSUE — THE ONE WHERE DOING NOTHING COSTS YOU

A rights issue offers existing shareholders the right (not
the obligation) to buy new shares at a DISCOUNT to the
market price, in proportion to their holding. The company
RAISES REAL MONEY. Section 62, Companies Act, 2013.

WORKED: 1:4 RIGHTS AT ₹400 (market price ₹620)

  You hold 400 shares at ₹620 = ₹2,48,000
  Entitlement: 1 new share for every 4 held = 100 shares
  Rights price: ₹400 per share
  Cash required: 100 x ₹400 = ₹40,000

THE THEORETICAL EX-RIGHTS PRICE (TERP)
  TERP = [(existing shares x cum-rights price)
          + (new shares x rights price)]
         / (existing + new shares)

  TERP = [(400 x 620) + (100 x 400)] / 500
       = [2,48,000 + 40,000] / 500
       = 2,88,000 / 500
       = ₹576

THREE CHOICES

  OPTION A — SUBSCRIBE FULLY
    Pay ₹40,000, receive 100 shares
    Total holding: 500 shares at TERP ₹576 = ₹2,88,000
    You invested ₹2,48,000 + ₹40,000 = ₹2,88,000
    NEUTRAL. Your ownership percentage is preserved.

  OPTION B — SELL THE RIGHTS ENTITLEMENT (RE)
    Rights Entitlements are credited to your demat as a
    separate temporary ISIN and TRADE ON THE EXCHANGE
    during the issue period.
    Theoretical value of one RE = TERP - rights price
                                = 576 - 400 = ₹176
    Sell 100 REs at roughly ₹176 = ₹17,600 (less charges)
    Your 400 shares are now worth 400 x 576 = ₹2,30,400
    Total: ₹2,30,400 + ₹17,600 = ₹2,48,000
    NEUTRAL — but your ownership percentage is DILUTED
    from 400/X to 400/(X + new shares).

    TAX: the sale of a Rights Entitlement is a capital
    gain with a cost of acquisition of NIL (for an RE
    received free), taxed per the holding period rules.
    Verify the current treatment with a CA.

  OPTION C — DO NOTHING
    The REs LAPSE WORTHLESS at the end of the issue period.
    Your 400 shares fall to 400 x 576 = ₹2,30,400
    YOU HAVE LOST ₹17,600 BY INACTION.

  THIS IS THE ONE CORPORATE ACTION WHERE IGNORING YOUR
  EMAIL ACTUALLY COSTS YOU MONEY.

HOW TO ACT
  - Rights issues are applied for through the ASBA
    mechanism via your bank's net banking, or through the
    registrar's R-WAP platform
  - You can usually apply for ADDITIONAL shares beyond
    your entitlement, allotted if the issue is
    undersubscribed
  - Watch for the exchange notice and the registrar's
    communication; set a calendar reminder for the
    issue closing date
  - REs are tradeable only during a specified window,
    usually ending a few days before the issue closes

WHY COMPANIES DO RIGHTS ISSUES
  To raise capital from existing shareholders without the
  cost and dilution of a public issue. BUT: a company that
  repeatedly needs rights issues to fund operations (rather
  than growth) is telling you something about its cash
  generation. Read the letter of offer.

  The COMPANY paid buyback distribution tax at ~23.3%.
  Proceeds were EXEMPT in the shareholder's hands u/s

  for shareholders, especially compared with dividends.

  of acquisition against that amount.

  as a CAPITAL LOSS, which can be set off against capital
  gains and carried forward for 8 assessment years.

  You hold 300 shares, cost ₹480 each = ₹1,44,000
  Buyback at ₹700. Acceptance ratio 100% for small
  shareholders (illustrative).
  Tender 300 shares, receive 300 x 700 = ₹2,10,000

  TAX:
    Deemed dividend income = ₹2,10,000 (the FULL amount)
    Tax at 31.2% slab      = ₹65,520
    Capital LOSS available = ₹1,44,000 (the cost),
      usable only against capital GAINS, now or carried
      forward for 8 years

  Cash received ₹2,10,000, tax paid ₹65,520.

  ECONOMIC GAIN was ₹66,000 (2,10,000 - 1,44,000).
  You paid ₹65,520 of tax on it. The loss is only useful
  if you have offsetting gains.

I received shares of a demerged company. How do I work out my cost of acquisition when I eventually sell?

Sections 49(2C) and 49(2D) of the Income-tax Act, 1961 govern this. Your original cost in the parent company is split between the parent (now smaller) and the resulting company, in the ratio of the net book value of the assets transferred to the net worth of the demerged company immediately before the demerger. The company is required to compute and publish this apportionment ratio, and it is usually issued as a note on the company's investor relations page and circulated by the RTA. Save that note. Worked example: you held 500 shares bought at ₹400, total cost ₹2,00,000. The scheme gives you 1 share of the resulting company for every 2 held, so you receive 250 new shares, and the company publishes an apportionment of 78% to the parent and 22% to the resulting entity. Your cost in the 500 parent shares becomes ₹1,56,000 (₹312 per share) and your cost in the 250 resulting-company shares becomes ₹44,000 (₹176 per share). Crucially, the holding period of the original shares carries over to the new shares under Section 2(42A), so if you had held the parent for three years, the newly received shares are already long-term on the day you receive them. One practical wrinkle: the resulting company often lists several weeks after the record date, during which you hold an untradeable asset and your broker may show it at zero or at a notional value. Verify all of this with a chartered accountant and against the specific scheme document, because demerger tax treatment is fact-specific and has been litigated.

Keep a corporate action fileFor every holding, keep a folder with: the original contract note, every corporate action notice, bonus and split adjustment records, demerger cost-apportionment letters, and rights issue allotment advices. When you eventually sell, your capital gains computation depends entirely on an accurate cost of acquisition and holding period — and after a decade of bonuses, splits and a demerger, reconstructing it from memory is impossible. Your broker's capital gains statement is usually right, but it is your responsibility to verify, and it will not capture shares you transferred in from elsewhere.

How to track corporate actions

  • Exchange websites publish a corporate actions calendar with record dates, ex-dates and purposes — nseindia.com and bseindia.com, searchable by company.
  • Your broker's app usually shows upcoming corporate actions for your holdings. Do not rely on this alone for action-requiring events like rights issues and buybacks.
  • The RTA (KFintech, Link Intime, MUFG Intime) sends communications to your registered email. Keep your email and mobile updated with your DP — most missed rights issues are the result of a stale contact record.
  • Companies file outcome-of-board-meeting disclosures with the exchanges under LODR Regulation 30. These are public and free.
  • Set a calendar reminder for any action requiring you to respond, with the deadline two days before the actual closing date.

IPOs End to End

The initial public offering is where retail enthusiasm in India runs hottest and where the evidence is most sobering. This section covers the full process from draft prospectus to listing day, the application mechanics you will actually use, and the honest historical record on IPO returns.

The process, start to finish

DRHP filed→SEBI observations→RHP + price band→Anchor book→Public issue 3 days→Basis of allotment→Listing T+3
  1. Appointment of intermediariesThe company appoints Book Running Lead Managers (merchant bankers), a registrar, legal counsel, and auditors. Their fees come out of the issue proceeds.
  2. DRHP — Draft Red Herring ProspectusFiled with SEBI and the exchanges, made public. This is the single most important document, running to 400–700 pages. It contains the business description, financials, risk factors, promoter details, litigation, related-party transactions, objects of the issue and the shareholding pattern.
  3. SEBI observationsSEBI reviews and issues observations; the company responds and revises. SEBI's clearance is not an endorsement of the issue or its price — the standard disclaimer says exactly this. SEBI vets disclosure, not value.
  4. RHP — Red Herring ProspectusThe updated document with the price band and issue dates, filed with the Registrar of Companies. Published typically a few days before the issue opens.
  5. Anchor bookOne working day before the issue opens, up to 60% of the QIB portion may be allotted to anchor investors — large institutions — at a price within the band. Anchor allottees face a lock-in (a portion for 30 days and a portion for 90 days). The anchor list is published and is widely read as a signal of institutional interest.
  6. Public issue windowOpen for a minimum of three working days, extendable in specified circumstances. Retail, HNI and QIB apply through their respective categories.
  7. Basis of allotmentFinalised by the registrar in consultation with the exchange, typically within a day or two of closure under the current timelines. Published on the registrar's website.
  8. Refunds / unblockingUnsuccessful applicants have their UPI mandate blocks released.
  9. Credit of sharesAllotted shares are credited to the demat account.
  10. ListingSEBI reduced the listing timeline to T+3 (three working days from issue closure), mandatory for all public issues opening on or after 1 December 2023. Verify the current timeline at sebi.gov.in.

Book building and the price band

HOW THE ISSUE PRICE IS DISCOVERED

FIXED PRICE ISSUE (now rare for mainboard)
  The company states one price. You apply at it.

BOOK BUILDING (the standard route)
  The company states a PRICE BAND, e.g. ₹480 to ₹505,
  with a tick of ₹1 or ₹5. The band's upper end is
  usually not more than 20% above the floor.

  Bidders specify both PRICE and QUANTITY.
  QIBs and HNIs bid at various prices in the band.
  RETAIL investors can:
     - bid at a specific price, OR
     - bid at the CUT-OFF PRICE, which means
       "whatever the final price turns out to be"

  CUT-OFF is available ONLY to retail individual
  investors and to employees in the employee reservation.

  At the close, the registrar builds a demand schedule:

     Price    Cumulative demand (shares)
     ₹505           3.8 crore
     ₹500           6.1 crore
     ₹495           9.4 crore
     ₹490          12.2 crore
     ₹480          15.9 crore

  If the issue is 2.0 crore shares and QIB demand is
  strong at ₹505, the issue price is set at or near the
  top of the band. In practice, in an oversubscribed
  Indian IPO, the price is almost always the UPPER END.

  WHY RETAIL SHOULD USUALLY BID AT CUT-OFF
    If you bid at ₹495 and the issue price is set at
    ₹505, YOUR APPLICATION IS REJECTED — you get nothing,
    not even a partial allotment. Bidding at cut-off
    means you accept the discovered price and remain in
    the running. The extra amount is blocked and the
    difference is released if the price is lower.

Lot size, and the retail allotment lottery

HOW MANY SHARES CAN YOU APPLY FOR?

LOT SIZE
  SEBI requires the minimum application value to fall in
  a prescribed band (historically ₹14,000-₹15,000 at the
  upper price band, with periodic revisions — verify the
  current norm at sebi.gov.in).

  Example: price band ₹480-₹505
     Lot size chosen: 29 shares
     Minimum application at cut-off = 29 x 505 = ₹14,645

  MAXIMUM FOR RETAIL = ₹2,00,000
     ₹2,00,000 / ₹14,645 = 13.65 -> 13 LOTS
     13 lots x 29 shares = 377 shares = ₹1,90,385

THE ALLOTMENT MATHS WHEN OVERSUBSCRIBED

  Retail portion: 35% of a 2 crore share issue = 70 lakh
  shares = 70,00,000 / 29 = 2,41,379 lots available

  Retail applications received: 18,00,000 lots
  Retail subscription: 18,00,000 / 2,41,379 = 7.46 times

  BECAUSE SEBI REQUIRES MINIMUM-ONE-LOT-TO-MAXIMUM-
  APPLICANTS, the registrar allots exactly ONE LOT to
  2,41,379 applicants chosen BY LOTTERY. Everyone else
  gets nothing.

  YOUR PROBABILITY OF ALLOTMENT
     If you applied for 1 lot:  roughly 2,41,379 /
       (number of applicants) — but note that applicants
       applying for 13 lots are ALSO only eligible for
       one lot in this scenario, and each application
       (not each lot) gets one entry in the lottery.

  CRITICAL INSIGHT:
     In a heavily oversubscribed retail book, applying
     for 13 lots gives you the SAME chance as applying
     for 1 lot, while blocking 13x the money.

     The variable that increases your odds is the NUMBER
     OF SEPARATE APPLICATIONS — one per PAN. A family of
     four with four PANs and four demat accounts can make
     four applications. Multiple applications from the
     SAME PAN are rejected outright, for all of them.

  WHEN RETAIL IS UNDER-SUBSCRIBED (under 1x):
     Everyone gets full allotment. Applying for more lots
     then DOES get you more shares. But an undersubscribed
     retail book is itself information about demand.

  THE "SMALL HNI" ALTERNATIVE
     Applying for ₹2-10 lakh puts you in the small NII
     bucket, which is also allotted by lot. The subscription
     multiple in that bucket is often different from retail.
     But you forfeit the cut-off option and must fund a
     larger block.

Applying through UPI — the ASBA mechanism

THE APPLICATION PROCESS, STEP BY STEP

ASBA = Applications Supported by Blocked Amount.
Your money NEVER leaves your bank account. It is BLOCKED
(a lien is marked) and debited only if shares are allotted.
This is why IPO applications have no refund delay.

STEP 1  Open your broker's IPO section, or your bank's
        net banking ASBA facility.

STEP 2  Select the IPO. Enter:
          - Number of LOTS (not shares)
          - Price: select CUT-OFF (recommended for retail)
          - Your UPI ID (must be from a bank and app on
            SEBI's approved list — check the list; many
            popular UPI apps are NOT eligible for IPO
            mandates)

STEP 3  Submit. Within a few minutes to a few hours you
        receive a MANDATE REQUEST notification in your
        UPI app.

STEP 4  OPEN THE UPI APP AND APPROVE THE MANDATE.
        THIS IS THE STEP PEOPLE MISS. An unapproved
        mandate means NO APPLICATION. You must approve
        before 17:00 on the issue closing day (verify
        the current cut-off — it has been as early as
        17:00 on the final day).

STEP 5  The amount is BLOCKED in your account. You can
        see it as a lien; the balance still shows but
        is not usable.

STEP 6  On allotment, the blocked amount is DEBITED for
        allotted shares and the rest is RELEASED.
        If not allotted, the full block is released,
        typically within a day of the basis of allotment.

STEP 7  Shares are credited to your demat before listing.

COMMON FAILURE MODES
  [ ] UPI mandate not approved in time -> application void
  [ ] Insufficient balance at the time of blocking
  [ ] Bank or UPI app not on the SEBI-approved list
  [ ] Multiple applications from the same PAN -> ALL rejected
  [ ] Name mismatch between PAN, demat and bank account
  [ ] Applying at a price below the final issue price
  [ ] Demat account frozen due to incomplete KYC/nomination

CHECK YOUR ALLOTMENT STATUS
  - The REGISTRAR's website (KFintech, Link Intime/MUFG
    Intime, Bigshare) — search by PAN or application number
  - The BSE website's IPO allotment status page
  - Your broker's app

The grey market premium — and why it is unreliable

GMP is an unregulated, unofficial and manipulable numberThe "grey market premium" is a price quoted by an informal, unregulated network of dealers for trading IPO applications and allotments before listing. It has no legal standing, no regulatory oversight, no transparency about volumes, and no settlement guarantee. SEBI has repeatedly cautioned investors against relying on it. Three specific problems: (1) It is trivially manipulable. A handful of trades in an opaque market can set a "premium" that is then reported widely, generating retail demand that benefits those who want the issue to be subscribed. (2) The volumes behind it are tiny relative to the issue, so it is not a meaningful price signal. (3) It has frequently been wrong, including issues that showed a large GMP and listed at a discount. Trading in the grey market also means transacting outside the regulated system with no recourse. If you are choosing whether to apply to an IPO based on GMP, you are speculating on a rumour, not investing in a business.

The evidence on IPO returns

Evidence summary — approximate, as at late 2026The figures below summarise patterns documented in SEBI's own research and in academic work on Indian IPOs. WHAT THE DATA ACTUALLY SHOWS ABOUT INDIAN IPOs

1. LISTING-DAY GAINS ARE REAL ON AVERAGE, BUT HIGHLY
   DISPERSED
   A meaningful share of Indian IPOs list above the issue
   price. The AVERAGE listing gain across a cycle has been
   positive. But the distribution is wide: in the same
   period, many issues listed at a discount, and the
   average is pulled up by a small number of very large
   first-day pops.

2. MOST RETAIL INVESTORS SELL ALMOST IMMEDIATELY
   A SEBI study of IPO investor behaviour found that
   individual investors sold a very large proportion of
   allotted shares within the first week of listing —
   with the selling propensity notably HIGHER when listing
   gains were large. This is the DISPOSITION EFFECT at work:
   booking winners fast, holding losers.

3. ONE-YEAR AND THREE-YEAR RETURNS ARE FAR LESS FLATTERING
   Studies of Indian IPO cohorts have repeatedly found
   that a substantial share of issues trade BELOW their
   issue price one year after listing, and that the
   median long-run excess return versus the index is
   negative or negligible. This is consistent with
   international evidence on long-run IPO underperformance.

4. THE STRUCTURAL REASON
   The company and its existing shareholders choose WHEN
   to sell and at WHAT price. They have:
     - complete information about the business
     - merchant bankers advising on timing
     - an incentive to issue when sentiment and valuations
       are favourable
   You have a 600-page document you probably did not read
   and three days to decide.

   This is an information-asymmetric transaction in which
   the better-informed party chose the timing. That does
   not make every IPO bad. It does mean the base rate is
   not in your favour.

5. OFS vs FRESH ISSUE MATTERS
   Read the RHP's "Objects of the Issue". If the issue is
   largely an OFFER FOR SALE (existing shareholders selling
   out), the company receives NOTHING and the transaction
   is an exit for early investors. If it is a FRESH ISSUE
   to fund capacity, repay debt or finance growth, the
   capital enters the business. Many Indian IPOs are
   predominantly OFS.

SME IPOs — a separate and riskier market

The SME platform is not the mainboardNSE Emerge and BSE SME are separate platforms for small and medium enterprises with substantially relaxed eligibility requirements. Key differences: minimum application size is one lot worth roughly ₹1,00,000 or more (not ₹15,000), there is no separate retail category in the same form, market making is mandatory for a period but liquidity is still extremely thin, financial disclosure requirements are lighter, and quarterly reporting obligations are reduced to half-yearly. SEBI has taken enforcement action in several SME cases involving inflated revenues, circular transactions and diversion of issue proceeds, and has progressively tightened eligibility and allotment norms for the segment, including requirements on operating profit track record and caps on OFS. Some SME issues have been subscribed hundreds of times and then fallen heavily. Verify the current framework at sebi.gov.in. For a beginner, the asymmetry of information, the ₹1 lakh minimum ticket and the liquidity profile combine to make this an unsuitable starting point.

WHAT IT GENUINELY TELLS YOU

    weeks and months

     the market closes, and the price has already moved.

  2. IT EXCLUDES DERIVATIVES. An FII can be net selling
     ₹3,000 crore in cash while being long index futures.
     The cash figure alone is misleading.
  3. IT EXCLUDES PRIMARY MARKET. An FII buying ₹8,000

     secondary cash figure.

     trackers (mechanical), long-only active funds
     (view-driven), hedge funds (short-term) and
     sovereign wealth funds (very long-term). A net
     number sums participants with opposite motivations.
  5. PROVISIONAL vs FINAL. The daily number is
     provisional and gets revised.

Your First Trade, Step by Step

A numbered procedure. The aim is a boring, correct delivery purchase with no surprises. Read it once, then follow it with a small amount the first time — ₹2,000 to ₹5,000 is enough to learn the mechanics without the stakes mattering.

  1. Confirm your accounts are readyKYC status is "validated" at a KRA (check at any KRA website with your PAN). PAN is linked to Aadhaar and operative. Nomination is registered on the demat account, or an opt-out is on record. Bank account is linked and the name matches exactly across PAN, bank and demat. Two-factor authentication is enabled on the broker login.
  2. Fund the trading accountUse UPI if available — it is usually free, while net banking transfers often carry a ₹9–₹20 payment gateway fee. Transfer only from a bank account registered in your own name; third-party transfers are rejected by SEBI rules. Confirm the funds show as available margin before proceeding. Transfer slightly more than the trade value to cover charges.
  3. Find the correct symbolSearch by company name, then verify the ISIN. Indian companies have similar names, and some have multiple listed entities after a demerger. Confirm the exchange (NSE or BSE) — prices differ slightly and liquidity is usually deeper on NSE for most scrips. Check the series: "EQ" is normal rolling settlement; "BE" is trade-for-trade with no intraday permitted.
  4. Run the pre-purchase liquidity and surveillance checkAverage daily traded value, bid-ask spread, circuit band, and whether the scrip appears on today's ASM or GSM list at nseindia.com. If the spread is wider than 0.5% or the daily value is under ₹2 crore, treat the position as hard to exit. This takes sixty seconds and prevents the worst category of mechanical loss.
  5. Open the order window and set the product typeRead the product type aloud. It must say CNC or Delivery or Longterm — not MIS, not MTF, not NRML. This single check prevents the most common beginner error: an accidental intraday trade that is auto-squared-off at 15:15 with fees on both legs.
  6. Choose the order typeSelect Limit, not Market. Look at the market depth and set your limit at the current best ask (for a near-certain fill) or a little below it (for a better price with some risk of not filling). Enter the quantity. Leave validity as DAY. Do not use a disclosed quantity for a small order.
  7. Review the order previewCheck, in order: symbol, exchange, buy/sell, quantity, limit price, product type, validity, and the estimated amount. Compare the estimated amount against your available margin. Then confirm.
  8. Watch for executionThe order appears in the Orders tab as "Open" until matched, then moves to "Executed". A partially filled order shows the filled quantity; the balance stays open until 15:30. If the order does not fill by the end of the session, it is cancelled automatically and no charge is incurred.
  9. Verify in Positions, then in HoldingsOn trade day the purchase appears under Positions (or as a T+1 holding). On T+1 it moves to Holdings and the shares are credited to your demat. Do not panic on day one when "Holdings" is empty — settlement has not completed.
  10. Read the contract noteIt arrives by email within 24 hours, digitally signed and usually password protected (commonly your PAN in a specified format). Open it. Check the quantity, rate, and every charge against section 15. Save it in a folder organised by financial year — you will need it for capital gains computation years later.
  11. Verify at the depositoryOn T+1 or T+2, log in to CDSL easi or NSDL IDeAS and confirm the shares appear in your demat account at the depository, not merely in the broker's app. Do this for your first few trades until you trust the chain.
  12. Record itAdd the purchase to your own spreadsheet: date, symbol, ISIN, quantity, price, total cost including charges, and the reason you bought. The "reason" column is the most valuable one — reading it in two years is how you learn whether your reasoning works.
  13. Set up the ongoing checksEnable SMS and email alerts for every demat debit. Set a calendar reminder for a quarterly portfolio review and an annual reconciliation of CAS against AIS. Do not set a price alert that will make you look at the position daily.
Do the first one small and deliberatelyBuy one share, or ₹2,000 worth of a liquid ETF, purely as a mechanics exercise. Watch the full cycle: order placed, order executed, contract note received, T+1 credit in demat, depository statement updated. Then sell it, and watch the sell cycle including the DP charge and the funds landing in your bank. The whole exercise costs under ₹100 in charges and gives you a complete mental model of the plumbing. Doing this before you invest a meaningful amount is the single most useful hour you can spend.

Frequently Asked Questions

Specific answers to the mechanical questions beginners actually ask. All figures and rules are stated as at FY 2025-26 / late 2026 and must be verified before reliance.

Why do I need both a trading account and a demat account? Can I have one without the other?
They do different jobs. The trading account is your interface to the exchange's order-matching engine — it is how you place orders and where funds sit temporarily in transit. The demat account is where securities are held in electronic form, maintained by NSDL or CDSL through a Depository Participant. You can hold a demat account without a trading account if you only receive shares (an inherited holding, an IPO allotment, or an ESOP credit) and never trade — but you cannot sell them on the exchange without a trading account. Conversely, a trading account alone is enough for segments that do not require delivery, such as F&O, which settle in cash. For anyone buying and holding equity, you need both, and they are almost always opened together by the same broker acting as both trading member and DP. Note one more thing: mutual funds do not require a demat account at all — units can be held in statement-of-account form with the RTA, which is the default when you invest directly with an AMC.
What exactly is T+1 settlement, and why is my money not in my bank account the same day I sell?
T+1 means the trade settles on the next trading day. When you sell on Monday, three things happen in sequence. On Monday the trade executes and the obligation is created; the clearing corporation novates it. On Tuesday morning the securities are debited from your demat account at pay-in, and by Tuesday afternoon funds are credited to your broker at pay-out and reflected in your trading account. Transferring those funds from the trading account to your bank account is a separate step you must initiate, and it typically takes one more working day — so money from a Monday sale commonly lands in the bank on Wednesday. Holidays extend this: a Thursday sale with Friday a holiday settles on Monday and reaches the bank Tuesday. SEBI has also introduced an optional T+0 cycle for a specified set of scrips, where settlement happens the same day; availability depends on the scrip and on whether your broker offers it. Verify the current scope at sebi.gov.in.
What happens if my broker goes bankrupt? Do I lose my shares?
Your shares are held in your own demat account at NSDL or CDSL, not by the broker — the broker is only the Depository Participant maintaining the account. SEBI's reforms, particularly the margin pledge and re-pledge system introduced in 2020, ended the earlier practice of brokers holding client securities in pool accounts, which was the vector for the major broker defaults of 2019–2020. So in principle your securities survive a broker failure and can be transferred to another DP. Your funds lying in the trading account are a different matter: SEBI requires segregation of client funds, upstreaming of client funds to clearing corporations, and periodic running-account settlement back to your bank, all of which limit the exposure — but they do not eliminate it. If an exchange declares a broker a defaulter, the exchange's Investor Protection Fund compensates eligible investors up to a prescribed limit; verify the current limit with NSE or BSE. Practical defences: register directly with CDSL easi or NSDL IDeAS so you can see your holdings independently of your broker, enable debit alerts, do not leave large idle balances in the trading account, use the UPI block facility if your broker supports it, and check the exchange's defaulter member list before choosing a broker.
Why did my stock price suddenly halve overnight? Did something terrible happen?
Before assuming bad news, check the corporate actions for that scrip on nseindia.com or bseindia.com. A 1:1 bonus issue doubles your share count and halves the price. A 1:5 stock split multiplies your count by five and divides the price by five. In both cases your total holding value is unchanged, and your broker's app may take a day to reflect the new share count while already showing the new price — which produces an alarming but entirely fictitious "loss" on the screen. A dividend ex-date also reduces the price by the dividend amount, and a demerger reduces the parent's price by the value of the spun-off entity, which you receive as separate shares that may not list for several weeks. If no corporate action explains it, then check the company's exchange filings under LODR Regulation 30 — results, an auditor resignation, a regulatory order, a promoter pledge invocation, or a credit rating downgrade. If the stock is hitting successive lower circuits, that is a liquidity event and you may not be able to sell at any price until buyers return.
Should I use NSE or BSE? Does it matter which exchange I buy on?
For most purposes it matters very little, but there are real differences. Liquidity: NSE has substantially greater volume in most equity scrips, which means tighter spreads and lower impact cost — so for anything other than the largest names, NSE usually gives a better execution. Price: the two exchanges can differ by a few paise because they are separate order books; arbitrageurs keep them close. Charges: exchange transaction charges differ slightly between NSE and BSE. Settlement: both settle T+1 and both use the same depositories, so shares bought on either exchange land in the same demat account. One genuinely important point: you can buy on one exchange and sell on the other once the shares are in your demat, because the holding is exchange-agnostic — but you cannot do this intraday before delivery, because the obligations are exchange-specific. Some scrips are listed on only one exchange, and SME platforms (NSE Emerge, BSE SME) are entirely separate. Your broker may default to one exchange; check which, and for thinly traded scrips compare the depth on both before placing the order.
What is a circuit filter, and why can I not sell my stock?
A circuit filter is a daily price band — 2%, 5%, 10% or 20% depending on the scrip's surveillance status — beyond which the exchange will not accept orders. Scrips in the F&O segment have no fixed daily band but have a dynamic operating range that flexes after a cooling-off period. When a stock hits its lower circuit, it means the price cannot fall further that day, but — crucially — it also means there are no buyers at the permitted price. The order book shows an enormous pending sell quantity against essentially nothing on the bid. You can place a sell order, but it will not execute. If the stock opens at the lower circuit again the next day and the next, you watch the value fall 5% or 10% a day for a week while being unable to exit. This is the mechanism by which holders of manipulated small caps and companies hit by a fraud disclosure lose most of their money, and it is why liquidity, not volatility, is the real risk in small companies. An upper circuit is the mirror image: you cannot buy. Before buying any unfamiliar scrip, check its circuit band and whether it appears on the ASM or GSM list.
How do I actually make money from shares? Is it only when the price goes up?
There are three sources, and over long periods the first is the one that does the work. (1) Earnings growth. The company earns more, so its claim on cash flows is worth more. This is the economic engine, and it is why equity is positive-sum rather than a zero-sum transfer. (2) Dividends. Cash distributed to you, taxed at your slab rate as at FY 2025-26. Note that a dividend is not free money: the share price is adjusted downward by the dividend amount on the ex-date, so your wealth is unchanged before tax and reduced after tax. (3) Multiple re-rating. The market decides to pay a higher price-to-earnings multiple for the same earnings. This is sentiment, it is mean-reverting over long periods, and relying on it is closer to speculation. There is also buyback, which returns cash and reduces the share count — though its tax treatment in India changed materially from 1 October 2024 and it is now treated as a deemed dividend taxed at slab. The practical implication: over one year, price movement dominates and is mostly noise. Over twenty years, earnings growth dominates and the noise averages out. That asymmetry is the entire argument for long holding periods.
Is it worth applying to every IPO? Everyone says listing gains are free money.
The evidence does not support "free money". SEBI's own research and academic work on Indian IPOs document three patterns. First, average listing-day gains across a cycle have been positive, but the distribution is extremely wide — many issues list at a discount, and the average is driven by a small number of large pops. Second, SEBI found that individual investors sell a very large share of allotted stock within the first week, and sell more readily when gains are large, which is the disposition effect rather than a strategy. Third, one-year and three-year returns are far less flattering: a substantial proportion of issues trade below their issue price a year after listing. The structural reason is simple: the company and its existing shareholders choose the timing and the price band, with merchant bankers advising, and they have complete information while you have three days and a 600-page document. That asymmetry does not make every IPO bad, but it means the base rate is not in your favour. If you do apply, the sensible process is: read the risk factors and the "Objects of the Issue" (fresh issue or offer for sale?), compare the implied valuation against listed peers in the RHP's own peer table, check the lock-in expiry schedule, and decide before listing day whether you are a long-term owner or a day-one seller. Do not apply because of the grey market premium, which is unregulated and manipulable, and never apply with borrowed money.
What are DP charges and why am I being charged when I sell but not when I buy?
DP charges are levied by the Depository Participant on behalf of CDSL or NSDL for debiting securities from your demat account — which happens only when you sell. Buying credits your account, and crediting is free. The charge is a flat amount, typically ₹13 to ₹25 plus 18% GST, levied per scrip, per day, entirely irrespective of quantity or value. This makes it sharply regressive for small transactions: ₹17.70 on a ₹2,000 sale is 0.885% of the trade, while the same ₹17.70 on a ₹2,00,000 sale is 0.009%. Two practical consequences. First, selling eight different small holdings on the same day incurs eight separate charges, which can easily exceed ₹140 on a portfolio worth a few thousand rupees. Second, this is a strong argument against accumulating many tiny positions across many scrips — consolidate into fewer, larger holdings, or use mutual funds held in statement-of-account form, which incur no DP charge at all. The charge often does not appear on the contract note; it is debited separately by the DP and shows on your ledger.
What is the difference between an index fund and an ETF if they track the same index?
The underlying portfolio is identical; the wrapper and the buying mechanics differ. An index fund is a regular mutual fund: you buy and sell units directly with the AMC at the end-of-day NAV, you need no demat account, there is no bid-ask spread and no premium or discount, SIPs work natively, and the minimum investment can be ₹100. An ETF is listed and traded like a share: you need a demat and trading account, you buy in whole units at whatever price the market offers, you pay brokerage, STT and the bid-ask spread, and you may pay a premium to the ETF's iNAV if it is illiquid or if creation of new units is constrained — a real problem with Indian ETFs tracking international indices, where sustained premiums of 5%–20% have occurred because the industry-wide overseas investment limit blocked new unit creation. ETFs typically carry a lower expense ratio and allow intraday trading. For a monthly SIP investor, the index fund is usually the simpler and cheaper instrument all-in. For a large lump sum into a deeply liquid ETF, the ETF can win. In either case, check the tracking difference over 3 and 5 years, not just the expense ratio.
How are my gains taxed, and what records do I need to keep?
As at FY 2025-26, for listed equity shares and equity-oriented mutual funds: gains on holdings of 12 months or less are short-term capital gains taxed at 20% under Section 111A; gains on holdings of more than 12 months are long-term capital gains taxed at 12.5% under Section 112A, with the first ₹1,25,000 of LTCG exempt each financial year. Dividends are taxed at your slab rate, with TDS deducted by the company above the applicable threshold. Debt mutual fund units acquired on or after 1 April 2023 are taxed at slab rate regardless of holding period. Records you need: every contract note (filed by financial year), your broker's and the RTA's capital gains statements, corporate action notices (especially demerger cost-apportionment letters and bonus allotment advices, because they determine your cost of acquisition and holding period), and your Annual Information Statement from incometax.gov.in, which you must reconcile before filing. A salaried person with capital gains generally files ITR-2. File by the due date — missing it forfeits your right to carry forward capital losses for eight assessment years. Note that all of this changes: the Income-tax Act, 2025 takes effect from 1 April 2026 and renumbers sections. Verify at incometax.gov.in and use a chartered accountant for your first filing with capital gains.
Can I buy shares of foreign companies like Apple or Microsoft from India?
Yes, through several routes, each with different friction. (1) Indian mutual funds and fund-of-funds tracking global indices — rupee SIPs through any Indian platform, simplest route, but many such schemes have been closed to fresh inflows since early 2022 because the industry-wide overseas investment limits set by SEBI and the RBI (USD 7 billion for mutual funds, USD 1 billion for ETFs) were breached. Availability varies by AMC; check before assuming. (2) Indian ETFs tracking global indices, listed on NSE/BSE — but these frequently trade at large premiums to iNAV when creation is blocked, so check the iNAV and use limit orders. (3) Direct US brokerage accounts funded under the RBI Liberalised Remittance Scheme, which permits up to USD 250,000 per financial year per person. This route attracts TCS on remittances above the annual threshold (₹10 lakh as at FY 2025-26 — verify), which is creditable against your tax but blocks cash flow, plus a forex conversion spread of 0.5%–2%. Tax: US dividends are withheld at 25% under the India-US DTAA with credit available in India; capital gains are taxed in India with a 24-month long-term threshold for foreign assets; and Schedule FA disclosure in your ITR is mandatory, with penalties under the Black Money Act for non-disclosure. (4) GIFT City IFSC-domiciled structures, a growing but still limited route. Verify all limits and tax positions at rbi.org.in and incometax.gov.in.
What is a lower circuit, upper circuit, and what is ASM/GSM? I keep seeing these on my broker app.
Circuits are price bands and are explained above. ASM (Additional Surveillance Measure) and GSM (Graded Surveillance Measure) are joint SEBI-exchange frameworks that progressively restrict trading in stocks showing concerning patterns. ASM targets excessive speculation and volatility — a stock that has run up sharply on unusual volume with high client concentration. Its stages impose 100% margin requirements and narrow the price band to 5% or 2%. GSM targets stocks whose price is not supported by financials — low net worth, poor earnings, weak book value. Its six escalating stages can move a stock to trade-for-trade, then to a periodic call auction where trading happens only once a week or once a month, and can require an Additional Surveillance Deposit of up to 100% of the trade value blocked by the exchange for months. If a stock you hold enters a high GSM stage, you may be unable to sell for days at a time and any sale may lock up additional capital. The daily ASM and GSM lists are published free on nseindia.com and bseindia.com. Checking them before buying any unfamiliar scrip takes sixty seconds and prevents the single worst category of mechanical loss.
My broker's app shows a different average price than my own calculation. Who is right?
Both can be "right" under different conventions, which is why you should maintain your own record. Brokers differ on whether the displayed average price includes brokerage, STT and other charges; on how they treat bonus shares (some assign zero cost, correctly, while others redistribute cost across the enlarged holding); on how they handle shares transferred in from another DP (often with no cost basis at all, showing a meaningless average); and on how they apply the FIFO convention required for capital gains. For tax purposes, what matters is the cost of acquisition under the Income-tax Act, computed on a FIFO basis for listed equity, with bonus shares at nil cost, split shares at apportioned cost, and demerged shares at the ratio specified in the scheme. Your broker's capital gains statement (as distinct from the holdings screen) is usually computed correctly for shares bought through that broker, and is what most people file from — but it cannot know the cost of shares you transferred in. The remedy is to keep your own spreadsheet with date, quantity, price, charges and corporate actions for every holding, and to reconcile it annually against the broker statement, the Consolidated Account Statement and the AIS.
How do I verify that a broker or adviser is genuine before giving them money?
Four checks, in order, and all of them are free. (1) Get the registration number in writing — by email, not verbally. Formats: stock broker INZ000012345, Investment Adviser INA000012345, Research Analyst INH000012345, Portfolio Manager INP000012345; an AMFI ARN-12345 denotes a mutual fund distributor earning commission, not an adviser. (2) Verify it on SEBI's own site — type sebi.gov.in into the address bar yourself, never follow a link they send, go to the intermediaries section, and search by both name and number. They must match the same entity. (3) Check enforcement — search the name in SEBI's Orders section and in the debarred entities list, and check the exchange's defaulter/expelled member list on nseindia.com and bseindia.com. (4) Payment hygiene — pay only into the entity's registered bank account in the entity's name, never an individual's; never share your trading password, PIN, TPIN or OTP; never install remote-access software such as AnyDesk or TeamViewer at anyone's request, which is the most common vector in Indian financial fraud calls. If something goes wrong: complain to the intermediary in writing first, then the exchange's investor grievance cell, then SEBI SCORES at scores.gov.in, then the SMART ODR portal. For cyber fraud, call 1930 or report at cybercrime.gov.in immediately — within the first few hours there is a real chance of freezing the transfer; after 24 hours, very little.

First-Trade Checklist and Glossary

A pre-flight checklist you can work through before your first transaction, followed by a reference glossary of the terms you will encounter on an Indian broker platform, a contract note and an exchange website.

Before your first trade

  • KYC status is "Validated" at a KRA — check at any KRA website using your PAN. "On-Hold" or "Rejected" blocks transactions.
  • PAN is linked to Aadhaar and is operative — check at incometax.gov.in. An inoperative PAN blocks market transactions and triggers higher TDS.
  • Nomination registered on the demat account, or an explicit opt-out filed. Non-compliance has previously frozen accounts for debits.
  • Name matches exactly across PAN, bank account and demat account. A mismatch causes rejected IPO applications and failed payouts.
  • Broker's SEBI registration (INZ) verified on sebi.gov.in and in the exchange member list; defaulter list checked.
  • Two-factor authentication enabled on the broker login and on the email address registered with the broker.
  • Registered directly with the depository — CDSL easi or NSDL IDeAS — so you can see holdings independently of the broker.
  • SMS and email alerts enabled for every demat debit.
  • Bank account linked and funded via UPI (free) rather than net banking (often ₹9–₹20 per transfer).
  • Contract note password known — usually your PAN in a defined format. Test that you can open one.
  • A record-keeping folder created, organised by financial year, for contract notes and corporate action notices.
  • A spreadsheet started with columns: date, symbol, ISIN, quantity, price, total cost, and reason for purchase.

For the specific order

  • Symbol and ISIN verified — not just the company name, which may be ambiguous after a demerger.
  • Series checked: EQ (normal) versus BE (trade-for-trade, no intraday permitted).
  • Not on today's ASM or GSM list — checked at nseindia.com.
  • Circuit band noted — 2%, 5%, 10%, 20% or dynamic.
  • Average daily traded value checked; your order is a small fraction of it.
  • Bid-ask spread checked in the market depth window; under 0.1% is comfortable.
  • For an ETF: iNAV checked and the market price is within about 0.5% of it.
  • Product type = CNC / Delivery. Read it aloud. Not MIS, not MTF, not NRML.
  • Order type = Limit, with a specific price. Not Market.
  • Validity = DAY.
  • Available margin exceeds the order value plus charges.
  • Executing mid-session, not in the first fifteen minutes.

After the trade

  • Order status confirmed as Executed, with quantity and price as expected.
  • Contract note received within 24 hours, opened, and every charge checked against section 15.
  • On T+1, shares visible in Holdings in the broker app.
  • On T+1 or T+2, shares confirmed in the depository statement at CDSL easi or NSDL IDeAS.
  • Entry added to your own spreadsheet, including the reason for purchase.
  • Contract note filed in the financial-year folder.
  • Quarterly review reminder and annual CAS-versus-AIS reconciliation reminder set in your calendar.
  • No price alert set that will make you check the position daily.

iNAVIndicative Net Asset Value. The real-time fair value of an ETF's underlying holdings. Compare it to the market price before trading an ETF.
InvITInfrastructure Investment Trust. A listed vehicle holding infrastructure assets and distributing at least 90% of cash flows.
IOCImmediate or Cancel. Order validity: execute what can be executed now, cancel the rest.
IPFInvestor Protection Fund. Exchange-maintained fund compensating investors up to a limit if a member is declared a defaulter.
IPOInitial Public Offering. A company's first sale of shares to the public, listing it on an exchange.
ISINInternational Securities Identification Number. The unique 12-character identifier for a security. Verify it before trading.
KRAKYC Registration Agency. Stores your market KYC centrally for reuse across intermediaries.
Limit orderAn order that executes only at your specified price or better. The default for a long-term investor.
LODRSEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015. Governs what listed companies must disclose.
Lot sizeThe minimum tradeable or applicable quantity — relevant in IPOs and in derivatives.
LTCG / STCGLong-term / short-term capital gain. For listed equity the threshold is 12 months.
Market depthThe order book showing the best five bid and ask levels with quantities.
Market orderAn order executed immediately at the best available prices. Dangerous in illiquid scrips.
MISMargin Intraday Square-off. The intraday product type; positions are auto-squared-off before close.
MTFMargin Trading Facility. Buying delivery shares with money borrowed from the broker at 12%–18% annualised.
Muhurat tradingA ceremonial one-hour session on Diwali marking the start of Samvat. Real settlement, thin volumes.
NAVNet Asset Value. The per-unit value of a mutual fund scheme.
NIINon-Institutional Investor. IPO applications above ₹2,00,000, split into small (₹2–10 lakh) and big (above ₹10 lakh) buckets.
NovationThe legal substitution by which the clearing corporation becomes buyer to every seller and seller to every buyer.
NSDLNational Securities Depository Limited. India's first depository. Demat IDs begin with "IN".
OBPPOnline Bond Platform Provider. A SEBI-regulated platform selling listed corporate bonds to retail investors.
OFSOffer For Sale. Existing shareholders selling their stake; the company receives nothing.
Pay-in / pay-outThe settlement steps where funds and securities are collected from, and distributed to, members.
PIT RegulationsSEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015. Governs trading on unpublished price-sensitive information.
Pre-open session09:00–09:15, a call auction that discovers the opening price through a single equilibrium calculation.
Price bandIn IPOs, the range within which bids may be placed. In trading, the circuit limit.
PromoterThe controlling shareholder or founding group, as defined in SEBI regulations. Subject to lock-ins and disclosure obligations.
QIBQualified Institutional Buyer. The institutional category in a public issue.
Record dateThe date on which the register is frozen to determine entitlement to a corporate action.
REITReal Estate Investment Trust. A listed vehicle holding income-producing commercial property and distributing at least 90% of cash flows.
RERights Entitlement. A tradeable temporary instrument representing your right to subscribe to a rights issue.
RHPRed Herring Prospectus. The final IPO document with the price band and dates.
RIASEBI-registered Investment Adviser (INA number). A fiduciary who may not earn commission on products advised on.
Rolling settlementSettlement on a fixed number of days after each trade day, as opposed to a fixed weekly cycle. India uses T+1.
RTARegistrar and Transfer Agent. Maintains shareholder records and processes allotments, dividends and corporate actions.
SCORESSEBI's online investor complaint redressal system at scores.gov.in.
SGBSovereign Gold Bond. RBI-issued bond paying 2.5% interest plus gold price appreciation. No new tranches since February 2024.
Short deliveryA seller failing to deliver shares at pay-in, triggering an auction and a punitive close-out.
SL / SL-MStop-loss limit / stop-loss market. Orders that activate when a trigger price is touched.
SME platformNSE Emerge and BSE SME. Separate listing platforms for small enterprises with relaxed requirements, large minimum lot sizes and thin liquidity.
SplitReducing the face value of a share and issuing proportionally more. Value-neutral; cost and holding period are preserved.
SpreadSee bid-ask spread.
STTSecurities Transaction Tax. 0.1% on both legs of a delivery equity trade; different rates for intraday and derivatives.
T+1The Indian equity settlement cycle: trades settle on the next trading day.
T2TTrade-to-trade. A settlement category requiring every trade to result in delivery.
TERPTheoretical Ex-Rights Price. The computed price of a share after a rights issue.
Tick sizeThe minimum permitted price increment for a scrip.
Tracking differenceThe gap between an index fund's return and its index's return over a period. More important than tracking error.
Tracking errorThe volatility of the return difference between a fund and its index.
TRITotal Return Index. An index that assumes dividends are reinvested. The correct benchmark for a fund.
UCCUnique Client Code. Your identifier with a broker at the exchange.
Upper / lower circuitThe maximum permitted daily rise or fall in a scrip. A lower circuit means you may be unable to sell at all.
VWAPVolume Weighted Average Price. The official closing price is the VWAP of the last 30 minutes of trading.
What good practice looks like, mechanicallyLimit orders, CNC product type, liquid scrips in the EQ series, placed mid-session, with the ASM/GSM and circuit checks done first. Contract notes opened, read and filed. Holdings verified at the depository, not just in the app. Nomination registered. KYC validated. No leverage, no MTF, no accidental intraday, no selling before delivery. A spreadsheet with a "reason for purchase" column. That is the whole mechanical discipline, and it costs nothing but attention.
