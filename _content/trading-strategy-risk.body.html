Read this first: the base rates for retail trading

This guide takes trading seriously as a discipline. That means starting with the outcome data rather than the techniques, because any honest treatment of trading has to begin by establishing how unlikely success is and what distinguishes the small minority who achieve it.

This is financial education, not financial adviceNothing here is a recommendation to buy, sell or trade any security, instrument or strategy. Active trading is high-risk and the evidence is that most individuals who attempt it lose money. Why this guide exists anyway

Given that evidence, there are two defensible reasons to read a serious guide on trading.

You are going to try regardless

Many people will trade whatever the evidence says. If you are one of them, the difference between doing it with a written specification, measured expectancy, controlled position sizing and a journal, versus doing it on intuition and Telegram tips, is the difference between a survivable education and a destroyed balance sheet. This guide is the former.

The discipline transfers

Expectancy, risk of ruin, overfitting, out-of-sample validation, cost modelling and base rates are not trading concepts — they are decision-making concepts. An engineer who internalises them makes better decisions about system design, estimation, hiring and product bets. The maths here is worth knowing even if you never place a trade.

What this guide will not doIt will not give you a strategy, a setup, an indicator combination or a "system". Those are worthless when handed over, because any edge that can be written down and distributed to a million people stops being an edge. what it gives you is the process by which an edge would be specified, tested, sized, executed and reviewed — and the honest standards against which to judge whether you have one.

Trading versus investing: different games, different maths

These two words are used interchangeably in casual conversation and they describe activities with almost nothing in common. Confusing them is the first and most expensive error, because the strategies, the risk management, the tax treatment and the base rates are all different.

A clarifying testAsk: "if the market closed for five years and reopened, would my position still make sense?" If yes, you are investing — you own a claim on earnings. If the question is absurd because your position depends entirely on price action next week, you are trading. Many people believe they are investing while behaving entirely as traders, which is how a "long-term holding" gets sold after a 12% drawdown.

The honest hybrid

If you want to trade, ring-fence itThe defensible structure is a core long-term portfolio that is automated, boring and untouchable, plus a small, separately accounted trading allocation that you can lose entirely without consequence. Separate broker accounts if possible. Never pledge the core to fund the satellite. Never move money from the core to the satellite after a loss. Review the satellite's performance against the core annually and be prepared to shut it down.

Where an edge can come from — and where it cannot

An edge is a repeatable reason why your expected value is positive. There are a limited number of places an edge can come from, and for a retail participant most of them are structurally inaccessible. Knowing which is which saves years.

The six sources of edge

SourceWhat it meansWho has itRealistic for retail in India?
InformationKnowing something material before the market prices itInsiders (illegally), company management, large institutional research operations with channel checks and expert networksNo — and acting on genuine non-public price-sensitive information is insider trading, prohibited under SEBI regulations with serious penalties
AnalysisInterpreting public information better than the consensusSkilled fundamental analysts, specialists in neglected small caps, people with genuine domain expertiseMarginally — possible in under-covered segments, over long horizons, with real work. Not possible in liquid large caps on a weekly timeframe.
SpeedReacting to information or order flow faster than othersHFT firms with co-located servers, microwave links and custom hardwareNo — the relevant timescale is microseconds. You are not in this race and never will be.
StructureCapturing returns available because of market plumbing: market making, arbitrage, index rebalancing, borrow/lend spreadsProprietary desks, market makers, large arbitrage booksLargely no — requires capital, infrastructure, negotiated costs and exchange memberships
BehaviourBeing systematically more disciplined than the average participant: not panicking, not chasing, following a planAnyone willing to do the workYes — this is the one genuinely accessible source, and it is also the hardest to maintain
Risk managementSurviving long enough for a small edge to compound; sizing so no single outcome is fatalAnyone willing to do the workYes — and it is not optional. It is the only thing standing between a small edge and ruin.
The uncomfortable implicationFour of the six sources of edge are closed to you. The two that remain — behaviour and risk management — are defensive. They stop you losing faster than necessary; they do not by themselves generate positive expectancy. A retail participant's realistic best case is a small analytical edge in a neglected corner, protected by excellent risk management and disciplined behaviour. Anyone offering you an edge in information, speed or structure is selling something.

Things frequently mistaken for an edge

Claimed edgeWhy it is not one

"I read the news faster"Algorithms parse news feeds in milliseconds. By the time you have read a headline on an app, the price has moved.
"I use a combination of five indicators"All common indicators are deterministic functions of past price and volume. Combining them adds parameters, not information, and dramatically increases overfitting risk.
"I have a 90% accurate system"Accuracy without loss magnitude is meaningless. Selling far out-of-the-money options gives a 90%+ win rate with negative expectancy. Ask for the maximum loss and the drawdown, not the win rate.
"I follow a guru with a great track record"Unaudited track records, survivorship bias in who you hear about, and the fact that the guru's income comes from subscriptions rather than trading. See the scams section.
"I know the sector because I work in it"Genuine domain knowledge is a real starting point for analysis, but only if applied over long horizons to under-covered companies. It is also the fastest route to inadvertently trading on material non-public information. Be extremely careful.
"I am good at maths / I am an engineer"Helpful for building and testing systems; irrelevant to whether an edge exists. Many quantitatively excellent people have lost money because the edge was never there.
"It worked for the last three months"Three months is noise. With a 55% win rate, a run of 20 wins in 30 trades has a probability of several percent — common enough that someone, somewhere, is always showing you one.

The edge question, asked properly

THE FOUR QUESTIONS THAT TEST A CLAIMED EDGE
===========================================
1. WHO IS ON THE OTHER SIDE, AND WHY ARE THEY WRONG?
   Every trade has a counterparty. If you are buying, someone
   is selling. What do you know or do that they do not?
   Acceptable answers:
     "A forced seller (index rebalance, redemption, margin
      call) is transacting for non-informational reasons."
     "I am providing liquidity when it is scarce and being
      paid the spread for it."
     "I have analysed a neglected company more thoroughly
      than the two analysts who cover it."
   Unacceptable answers:
     "The chart looks bullish."
     "The indicator crossed over."
     "It has been going up."

2. WHY HAS THIS NOT BEEN ARBITRAGED AWAY?
   If an effect is real, persistent and easy to find, capital
   floods in and it disappears. Acceptable reasons for
   persistence:
     - Capacity constraints (the opportunity is too small for
       institutional money to bother with)
     - Structural barriers (regulation, mandates, taxation)
     - Behavioural causes that are deeply human and stable
     - It is compensation for a real risk you are bearing
   If you cannot name one, the effect is probably not real.

3. WHAT IS THE SAMPLE SIZE, AND WHAT IS THE OUT-OF-SAMPLE
   PERFORMANCE?
   Fewer than 100 trades is anecdote. In-sample performance
   is a measure of your curve-fitting ability, not of edge.

4. WHAT IS THE EXPECTANCY AFTER COSTS, AND WHAT IS THE
   MAXIMUM DRAWDOWN?
   If you cannot state both numbers, you have not tested it.

If your claimed edge fails any of these four, you do not have
an edge. You have a hypothesis, which is a fine thing to have
and a terrible thing to bet money on.

The strategy archetypes

Momentum / trend following

Buy what is rising, sell what is falling. Premise: price trends persist over certain horizons. Characteristics: low win rate (30–45%), large average winner, many small losers, long flat periods, severe drawdowns when trends reverse. Requires the discipline to take many small losses and let a few winners run — psychologically the hardest profile.

Mean reversion

Buy what has fallen too far, sell what has risen too far. Premise: prices overshoot and revert. Characteristics: high win rate (60–75%), small average winner, occasional very large loser when "too far" keeps going. Psychologically comfortable and structurally dangerous — it is the same payoff shape as selling options.

Breakout

Enter when price exits a defined range. Premise: consolidation precedes directional expansion. Characteristics: many false breakouts, cost-heavy, highly sensitive to the definition of the range and to slippage at the moment of the break, when liquidity is thinnest.

Pairs / relative value

Long one instrument, short a related one; profit from the spread converging. Premise: the relationship is stable. Characteristics: market-neutral-ish, two sets of costs, requires borrow or derivatives for the short leg, and fails catastrophically when the relationship breaks for a fundamental reason.

Event driven

Position around results, corporate actions, index inclusions, policy decisions. Premise: predictable flows or predictable reactions. Characteristics: binary outcomes, high implied volatility, you are competing with specialists, and the event is already priced.

Volatility

Trade implied versus realised volatility rather than direction. Premise: options are systematically mispriced in one direction. Characteristics: requires option fluency and Greeks management; for retail it almost always degenerates into naked option selling, which is covered as a hazard in the derivatives guide.

Win rate and payoff ratio are not independentAcross almost all strategy archetypes, the two trade off against each other. Momentum gives you a low win rate and a high payoff ratio. Mean reversion gives you a high win rate and a low payoff ratio. There is no archetype offering both, because if there were, it would be arbitraged. When someone advertises "75% accuracy with 1:3 risk-reward", they are describing a combination that would be extraordinarily profitable and is therefore almost certainly a curve-fitted backtest or a fabrication.

Market structure for traders

Before any strategy, you need to understand the mechanics of how your order becomes a trade and what it costs you in ways that never appear on a contract note. Market structure is the terrain; strategy is the route.

  Your "maximum loss" from a stop is a planning figure, not a
  guarantee. Size positions so that a 3x-worse-than-planned
  outcome is survivable, not fatal.

  a 6% loss on this position?" If no, the position is too big.

  This is why single-stock concentration is so much more
  dangerous than index exposure: an index cannot be locked at

Building a strategy: writing it as a specification

A strategy that is not written down is not a strategy — it is a mood. As a software engineer you already know the discipline: a system you cannot specify is a system you cannot test, and a system you cannot test is a system you cannot trust. Trading is the same, with money attached.

The seven components

  1. HypothesisA falsifiable statement about market behaviour, with a reason why it should be true and why it has not been arbitraged away. Written first, before any chart is opened.
  2. UniverseExactly which instruments are eligible, defined by objective, checkable criteria — liquidity, price, market cap, segment membership. No discretion.
  3. TimeframeThe bar interval for signals, the expected holding period, and the review cadence.
  4. Entry ruleThe precise, computable condition that triggers a trade. If two people applying it to the same data would get different answers, it is not a rule.
  5. Exit ruleThe condition for taking profit. Separate from the stop.
  6. Stop ruleThe condition for accepting the trade is wrong. Defined in advance, in price terms, before entry.
  7. Filter and sizing rulesConditions under which you do not trade at all (regime filters), and how position size is determined from account equity and the stop distance.
The two-person testHand your written specification to a competent stranger with the same data. If they cannot reproduce your trade list exactly, your specification has discretion hidden in it, and that discretion is where your backtest results came from. Discretionary trading is legitimate, but it cannot be backtested, which means you can never know whether you have an edge — only whether you have had good luck.

    stated threshold

3. TIMEFRAME
------------------------------------------------------------
  Signal bar        : daily close
  Decision time     : after the close; orders placed for the
                      next session
  Expected hold     : 5-10 sessions
  Maximum hold      : 15 sessions (time stop)
  Review cadence    : weekly log review; monthly metrics;
                      quarterly go/no-go

------------------------------------------------------------
4. ENTRY RULE  (all conditions must be true at the close)
------------------------------------------------------------
  E1. Close is above the 200-period simple moving average
      (regime: medium-term uptrend)
  E2. The 50-period SMA is above the 200-period SMA
  E3. Today's close is below the 20-period SMA
      (short-term pullback has occurred)
  E4. Today's close is above the 50-period SMA
      (the pullback has not broken the medium-term structure)
  E5. Today's close is greater than yesterday's close
      (first sign of resumption - avoids catching a knife)
  E6. India VIX is below 22 (regime filter - do not trade
      this pattern in a high-volatility regime)

  ORDER: buy at the next session's open.
  NO DISCRETION. If all six are true, the trade is taken.
  If any is false, no trade. There is no "but it looks good".

------------------------------------------------------------
5. EXIT RULE (profit)
------------------------------------------------------------
  X1. Close above the 20-period SMA by more than 0.5 x ATR(14)
      -> exit at next open
  OR
  X2. Price reaches entry + 2.5 x ATR(14 at entry)
      -> exit on touch
  OR
  X3. 15 sessions elapsed -> exit at next open (time stop)

------------------------------------------------------------
6. STOP RULE
------------------------------------------------------------
  S1. Initial stop = entry - 1.5 x ATR(14 at entry)
  S2. Stop is never widened. Ever. Under any circumstances.
  S3. After price reaches entry + 1.5 x ATR, the stop moves
      to breakeven (entry price)
  S4. Hard stop: if the index closes below its 200-SMA, exit
      all positions at the next open regardless of P&L
      (the regime that justified the trade has ended)

------------------------------------------------------------
7. FILTERS AND SIZING
------------------------------------------------------------
  F1. No new entries in the 3 sessions before a scheduled
      monetary policy decision or the Union Budget
  F2. Maximum 2 concurrent positions
  F3. Maximum portfolio heat 4% (sum of all open risk)
  F4. No trading for 3 sessions after 3 consecutive losses
      (circuit breaker - see the psychology section)

  SIZING:
    Risk per trade        = 1.0% of account equity
    Stop distance         = 1.5 x ATR(14) in points
    Position size (units) = (equity x 0.01) / stop distance
    Rounded DOWN to the nearest tradeable unit.
    If one unit exceeds the risk budget, NO TRADE.

------------------------------------------------------------
8. COST MODEL (must be applied in every backtest)
------------------------------------------------------------
  Brokerage           : Rs 20 per order + 18% GST
  STT                 : as applicable to the instrument
  Exchange charges    : as published
  SEBI turnover fee   : as published
  Stamp duty          : as applicable, buy side
  SLIPPAGE ASSUMPTION : 0.05% of notional per side, on top of
                        all of the above, on every fill,
                        including stop-loss fills
  GAP ASSUMPTION      : stop fills are modelled at the WORSE
                        of (stop price) and (next open)

------------------------------------------------------------
9. METRICS TO RECORD
------------------------------------------------------------
  Trades, win rate, average win (R), average loss (R),
  expectancy (R), profit factor, maximum drawdown (%),
  maximum drawdown duration (days), longest losing streak,
  exposure (% of time in market), return on risk capital,
  total costs as a % of gross P&L

------------------------------------------------------------
10. KILL CRITERIA
------------------------------------------------------------
  K1. Out-of-sample expectancy below +0.10R over 200 trades
  K2. Live drawdown exceeds 1.5x the worst backtested drawdown
  K3. Live expectancy below zero over 100 live trades
  K4. Any rule has been changed more than twice since the
      last out-of-sample test (overfitting indicator)

  If a kill criterion triggers, the strategy stops. Permanently
  or until a full re-test on new data. NOT "with a small tweak".

THIS IS AN ILLUSTRATIVE SPECIFICATION FORMAT, NOT A
RECOMMENDED STRATEGY. The hypothesis is almost certainly not
profitable after costs. It is written out in full so you can
see what a complete specification looks like.
Notice what section 1 doesThe hypothesis section openly states that the pattern is widely known and probably arbitraged away, and commits to a falsification criterion before any testing. That intellectual honesty at the start is what separates research from rationalisation. The overwhelming temptation in strategy development is to write the hypothesis after seeing the backtest, which converts a scientific process into a storytelling one.

Specification anti-patterns

Anti-patternWhat it looks likeWhy it fails
Hidden discretion"Enter on a breakout if the setup looks clean"Untestable. Your backtest measures your hindsight, not the rule.
Undefined exit"Hold until the trend ends"Exits determine most of your return distribution. An undefined exit is an undefined strategy.
Stop as an afterthought"I'll cut it if it goes too far""Too far" is defined by your emotional state at the moment of pain, which is the worst possible input.

No cost modelBacktest on closing prices with zero frictionCovered in detail in the backtesting section. This single omission invalidates most retail backtests.

Expectancy maths

Expectancy is the single number that determines whether a strategy makes money. Everything else — win rate, accuracy, profit factor, Sharpe ratio — is either derived from it or a distraction from it. If you learn one piece of arithmetic from this guide, learn this one.

R-multiples: the unit that makes everything comparable

THE R-MULTIPLE
==============
R = the amount of money you risked on a trade, defined as
    the distance from entry to stop, times the position size.

Every outcome is then expressed as a multiple of R:
  Lost at the stop        = -1.0R
  Exited at half the stop = -0.5R
  Made twice the risk     = +2.0R
  Gapped through the stop = -2.3R   (this happens)

WHY THIS IS THE RIGHT UNIT
  1. It normalises across position sizes and instruments.
     A Rs 4,000 loss on a small position and a Rs 40,000 loss
     on a large one are both -1.0R if both were sized to risk
     1% of equity.
  2. It separates the STRATEGY'S quality from the SIZING
     decision. A strategy with +0.3R expectancy is good
     whether you risk 0.5% or 2% per trade; the sizing
     decision only changes how fast you compound and how
     likely you are to be ruined.
  3. It makes expectancy directly computable.

WORKED EXAMPLE
  Account                Rs 5,00,000
  Risk per trade         1% = Rs 5,000  -> 1R = Rs 5,000
  Entry                  25,000 (index units)
  Stop                   24,800  -> stop distance 200 points
  Position size          5,000 / 200 = 25 units

  Exit at 25,340 -> profit = 340 x 25 = Rs 8,500
  In R terms: 8,500 / 5,000 = +1.70R

  Exit at stop -> loss = 200 x 25 = Rs 5,000 = -1.00R

  Gap down, filled at 24,610 -> loss = 390 x 25 = Rs 9,750
  In R terms: -1.95R   <- RECORD THIS HONESTLY. Hiding gap
  losses by calling them -1R is how people convince themselves
  their risk control works.

The expectancy formula

EXPECTANCY
==========
  E (in R) = (W x A_win) - (L x A_loss)

  W      = win rate (as a decimal)
  L      = loss rate = 1 - W
  A_win  = average winning trade, in R
  A_loss = average losing trade, in R (positive number)

EQUIVALENTLY, using the PAYOFF RATIO P = A_win / A_loss
  and assuming A_loss = 1.0R:

  E = (W x P) - (1 - W)

PROFITABILITY CONDITION:  E > 0  after costs.

WORKED - THREE STRATEGIES WITH IDENTICAL "QUALITY" CLAIMS
---------------------------------------------------------
STRATEGY A - "high accuracy mean reversion"
  W = 0.75,  A_win = 0.5R,  A_loss = 1.6R
  E = (0.75 x 0.5) - (0.25 x 1.6)
    = 0.375 - 0.400
    = -0.025R   LOSES MONEY despite a 75% win rate

STRATEGY B - "trend following"
  W = 0.35,  A_win = 3.2R,  A_loss = 1.0R
  E = (0.35 x 3.2) - (0.65 x 1.0)
    = 1.120 - 0.650
    = +0.470R   MAKES MONEY despite being wrong 65% of the time

STRATEGY C - "balanced swing"
  W = 0.52,  A_win = 1.4R,  A_loss = 1.0R
  E = (0.52 x 1.4) - (0.48 x 1.0)
    = 0.728 - 0.480
    = +0.248R

TRANSLATE INTO RUPEES
  With 1R = Rs 5,000 and 100 trades a year:
    A: 100 x -0.025 x 5,000 = -Rs   12,500
    B: 100 x +0.470 x 5,000 = +Rs 2,35,000
    C: 100 x +0.248 x 5,000 = +Rs 1,24,000

  Then SUBTRACT COSTS. At Rs 180 per round trip:
    100 trades x Rs 180 = Rs 18,000
    A: -Rs 30,500   B: +Rs 2,17,000   C: +Rs 1,06,000

THE LESSON THAT CANNOT BE OVERSTATED
  WIN RATE ALONE TELLS YOU NOTHING.
  Strategy A wins three times as often as B and loses money.
  Anyone who quotes accuracy without payoff ratio is either
  confused or selling you something.

The win rate / payoff ratio frontier

BREAK-EVEN WIN RATE FOR A GIVEN PAYOFF RATIO
============================================
Setting E = 0 and solving:
     (W x P) - (1 - W) = 0
     W x P + W = 1
     W = 1 / (1 + P)

Payoff ratio P   Break-even win rate   Comment
--------------   -------------------   --------------------
     0.25             80.0%            Selling tails. The
                                       structure of a short
                                       option book.
     0.50             66.7%            Classic mean reversion
     0.75             57.1%
     1.00             50.0%            A coin flip
     1.50             40.0%
     2.00             33.3%
     2.50             28.6%
     3.00             25.0%            Classic trend following
     4.00             20.0%
     5.00             16.7%            Long-tail momentum

NOW ADD COSTS. Suppose costs equal 0.15R per round trip
(a realistic retail figure when 1R is small).

  E = (W x P) - (1 - W) - 0.15 = 0
  W = 1.15 / (1 + P)

Payoff ratio P   BE win rate      BE win rate     Extra win
                 (no costs)       (0.15R costs)   rate needed
--------------   -------------    -------------   -----------
     0.25            80.0%            92.0%         +12.0 pp
     0.50            66.7%            76.7%         +10.0 pp
     1.00            50.0%            57.5%          +7.5 pp
     2.00            33.3%            38.3%          +5.0 pp
     3.00            25.0%            28.8%          +3.8 pp
     5.00            16.7%            19.2%          +2.5 pp

READ THE LEFT-HAND SIDE OF THAT TABLE.
A strategy with a 0.25 payoff ratio needs a 92% win rate
after costs. That is the arithmetic reason why "sell premium
for small consistent gains" fails at retail cost levels. The
costs eat a larger fraction of a small average win.

READ THE RIGHT-HAND SIDE.
High-payoff strategies are far more ROBUST to costs. If your
average winner is 5R, a 0.15R cost is 3% of the winner. If
your average winner is 0.25R, the same cost is 60% of it.

PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE FOR A SMALL ACCOUNT
  Prefer strategies with a high payoff ratio and a low trade
  frequency. The cost structure of retail trading in India
  makes high-frequency, small-edge strategies arithmetically
  unavailable to you, regardless of how good the signal is.
The break-even frontier. Strategies to the left (small wins, high accuracy) require near-impossible win rates once realistic costs are included.

How many trades before expectancy means anything?With a true expectancy of +0.25R and a typical trade-to-trade standard deviation of around 1.5R, the standard error of your measured expectancy after n trades is roughly 1.5/√n. Most retail traders form a strong conviction after 15.

Backtesting properly

A backtest is a simulation of a strategy against historical data. Done well, it is the only way to estimate expectancy before risking money. Done the way most retail traders do it, it is an elaborate machine for generating false confidence. The difference is entirely in the discipline, not in the tooling.

Look-ahead bias: the error you will make

LOOK-AHEAD BIAS - FOUR WAYS IT CREEPS IN
========================================
1. USING THE CLOSE TO DECIDE AND THE CLOSE TO TRADE
   "If today's close is above the 20-SMA, buy at today's
   close." You did not know today's close until it happened.
   FIX: decide on close, execute at the NEXT open, and model
   the gap.

2. USING THE DAY'S HIGH OR LOW IN A SIGNAL
   "Buy if price touches the lower band and closes green."
   Within a single bar you cannot know the order of events.
   Did it touch the band before or after the close formed?
   FIX: use intraday data, or restrict signals to values
   known at bar close.

3. USING RESTATED OR LATE-ARRIVING DATA
   Fundamental data is published weeks after the period it
   describes, and is sometimes restated. Using a financial
   ratio "as at 31 March" on 1 April is look-ahead.
   FIX: lag fundamental data by the actual publication delay.

4. SURVIVORSHIP IN RULE DESIGN
   You chose the 20-period SMA because you have been watching
   this market for five years and 20 "works". That knowledge
   came from the data you are now testing on. This is the
   subtlest and most pervasive form, and it is unfixable by
   code. The only defence is genuinely held-out data.

THE DIAGNOSTIC THAT CATCHES MOST CASES
  If your backtest equity curve is remarkably smooth, or your
  win rate is above 70% with a payoff ratio above 1.5, or your
  maximum drawdown is under 8%, assume look-ahead bias until
  you have proved otherwise. Real strategies are ugly.

A worked backtest skeleton, with the common mistakes annotated

"""
Minimal event-driven backtest skeleton (illustrative).
Deliberately simple so the LOGIC is visible rather than hidden
in a framework. The annotations marked MISTAKE show what most
retail backtests get wrong.

NOT a trading recommendation. Educational scaffolding only.
"""

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np


# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 1. COST MODEL  -- build this FIRST, before any strategy
# ----------------------------------------------------------
# MISTAKE #1: writing the strategy first and "adding costs
# later". Costs change which strategies are even viable, so
# they belong in the design loop from the beginning.

COST_CONFIG = {
    "brokerage_per_order": 20.0,       # flat, discount broker
    "gst_rate": 0.18,                  # on brokerage + fees
    "exchange_charge_rate": 0.0000325, # of turnover (verify)
    "sebi_fee_rate": 0.000001,         # of turnover (verify)
    "stamp_duty_rate": 0.00003,        # buy side only (verify)
    "stt_sell_rate": 0.001,            # verify by instrument
    "slippage_rate": 0.0005,           # 0.05% per side
}
# Every rate above is ILLUSTRATIVE and dated late 2026.
# Verify at nseindia.com / incometax.gov.in before use.


def round_trip_cost(entry_price, exit_price, qty, cfg):
    """Rupee cost of one complete round trip."""
    buy_turnover = entry_price * qty
    sell_turnover = exit_price * qty
    turnover = buy_turnover + sell_turnover

    brokerage = cfg["brokerage_per_order"] * 2
    exch = turnover * cfg["exchange_charge_rate"]
    sebi = turnover * cfg["sebi_fee_rate"]
    gst = (brokerage + exch + sebi) * cfg["gst_rate"]
    stamp = buy_turnover * cfg["stamp_duty_rate"]
    stt = sell_turnover * cfg["stt_sell_rate"]
    slip = turnover * cfg["slippage_rate"]

    return brokerage + exch + sebi + gst + stamp + stt + slip


# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 2. INDICATORS -- computed only from data available at the
#    time of the decision
# ----------------------------------------------------------

def add_indicators(df):
    out = df.copy()
    out["sma20"] = out["close"].rolling(20).mean()
    out["sma50"] = out["close"].rolling(50).mean()
    out["sma200"] = out["close"].rolling(200).mean()

    prev_close = out["close"].shift(1)
    tr = pd.concat([
        out["high"] - out["low"],
        (out["high"] - prev_close).abs(),
        (out["low"] - prev_close).abs(),
    ], axis=1).max(axis=1)
    out["atr14"] = tr.rolling(14).mean()
    return out


# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 3. SIGNAL -- evaluated on bar i, ACTED ON at bar i+1 open
# ----------------------------------------------------------

def entry_signal(row):
    # MISTAKE #2: comparing row["close"] and then filling at
    # row["close"]. You cannot trade at a price you only knew
    # after it was set. Signals here are evaluated on bar i;
    # the engine below fills at bar i+1's OPEN.
    if np.isnan(row["sma200"]) or np.isnan(row["atr14"]):
        return False
    return (
        row["close"] > row["sma200"]
        and row["sma50"] > row["sma200"]
        and row["close"] < row["sma20"]
        and row["close"] > row["sma50"]
    )


# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 4. ENGINE
# ----------------------------------------------------------

def backtest(df, equity_start=500000.0, risk_frac=0.01,
             atr_stop_mult=1.5, atr_target_mult=2.5,
             max_bars=15, cfg=COST_CONFIG):

    df = add_indicators(df).reset_index(drop=True)
    equity = equity_start
    trades = []
    position = None

    for i in range(len(df) - 1):
        bar = df.iloc[i]
        nxt = df.iloc[i + 1]

        # ---- manage an open position -------------------
        if position is not None:
            position["bars_held"] += 1

            # MISTAKE #3: assuming the stop always fills at the
            # stop price. If the bar OPENS below the stop, you
            # fill at the OPEN, which is worse. Model that.
            stop_hit = nxt["low"] <= position["stop"]
            gap_through = nxt["open"] <= position["stop"]

            if stop_hit:
                fill = nxt["open"] if gap_through else position["stop"]
                reason = "gap_stop" if gap_through else "stop"
            elif nxt["high"] >= position["target"]:
                # MISTAKE #4: if BOTH the stop and the target are
                # inside the same bar, a daily-bar backtest cannot
                # know which came first. Resolving it in your
                # favour inflates results. Always resolve it
                # AGAINST yourself (stop first), as done here by
                # checking the stop before the target.
                fill = position["target"]
                reason = "target"
            elif position["bars_held"] >= max_bars:
                fill = nxt["open"]
                reason = "time_stop"
            else:
                fill = None
                reason = None

            if fill is not None:
                gross = (fill - position["entry"]) * position["qty"]
                cost = round_trip_cost(position["entry"], fill,
                                       position["qty"], cfg)
                net = gross - cost
                equity += net
                r_multiple = net / position["risk_rupees"]
                trades.append({
                    "entry_date": position["entry_date"],
                    "exit_date": nxt["date"],
                    "entry": position["entry"],
                    "exit": fill,
                    "qty": position["qty"],
                    "gross": gross,
                    "cost": cost,
                    "net": net,
                    "r": r_multiple,
                    "reason": reason,
                    "equity": equity,
                })
                position = None

        # ---- consider a new entry ----------------------
        if position is None and entry_signal(bar):
            atr = bar["atr14"]
            entry_px = nxt["open"] * (1 + cfg["slippage_rate"])
            stop_px = entry_px - atr_stop_mult * atr
            stop_dist = entry_px - stop_px
            if stop_dist <= 0:
                continue

            risk_rupees = equity * risk_frac
            qty = int(risk_rupees / stop_dist)
            # MISTAKE #5: allowing fractional or zero quantity.
            # If one unit exceeds the risk budget, take NO trade.
            if qty < 1:
                continue

            position = {
                "entry_date": nxt["date"],
                "entry": entry_px,
                "stop": stop_px,
                "target": entry_px + atr_target_mult * atr,
                "qty": qty,
                "risk_rupees": qty * stop_dist,
                "bars_held": 0,
            }

    return pd.DataFrame(trades), equity


# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 5. METRICS
# ----------------------------------------------------------

def metrics(trades, equity_start):
    if trades.empty:
        return {"trades": 0}

    wins = trades[trades["net"] > 0]
    losses = trades[trades["net"] <= 0]

    win_rate = len(wins) / len(trades)
    avg_win_r = wins["r"].mean() if len(wins) else 0.0
    avg_loss_r = abs(losses["r"].mean()) if len(losses) else 0.0
    expectancy_r = (win_rate * avg_win_r) - ((1 - win_rate) * avg_loss_r)

    curve = pd.concat([
        pd.Series([equity_start]), trades["equity"]
    ], ignore_index=True)
    peak = curve.cummax()
    dd = (curve - peak) / peak
    max_dd = dd.min()

    gross_profit = wins["net"].sum() if len(wins) else 0.0
    gross_loss = abs(losses["net"].sum()) if len(losses) else 0.0

    return {
        "trades": len(trades),
        "win_rate": round(win_rate, 3),
        "avg_win_R": round(avg_win_r, 3),
        "avg_loss_R": round(avg_loss_r, 3),
        "expectancy_R": round(expectancy_r, 3),
        "profit_factor": round(gross_profit / gross_loss, 3)
                         if gross_loss else float("inf"),
        "max_drawdown_pct": round(max_dd * 100, 2),
        "total_costs": round(trades["cost"].sum(), 2),
        "cost_as_pct_of_gross": round(
            100 * trades["cost"].sum() / max(trades["gross"].sum(), 1e-9), 2),
        "final_equity": round(trades["equity"].iloc[-1], 2),
    }


# ----------------------------------------------------------
# 6. THE SPLIT THAT MAKES THE RESULT MEAN ANYTHING
# ----------------------------------------------------------
# MISTAKE #6 (the biggest one): tuning parameters on the whole
# dataset and reporting that as "the result". Split the data,
# develop on the in-sample portion ONLY, and touch the
# out-of-sample portion once.

def split_in_out_sample(df, in_sample_frac=0.6):
    cut = int(len(df) * in_sample_frac)
    return df.iloc[:cut].copy(), df.iloc[cut:].copy()
Six mistakes, one consequenceEach annotated mistake above inflates results independently. Combined, they routinely turn a losing strategy into a backtest showing a smooth 40% annual return. The order of magnitude is not an exaggeration — resolving same-bar stop/target ambiguity in your favour alone can swing a mean-reversion strategy from negative to strongly positive expectancy.

In-sample, out-of-sample and walk-forward

THE THREE LEVELS OF VALIDATION
==============================
LEVEL 1 - SIMPLE SPLIT
  |-------- IN SAMPLE (60%) --------|-- OUT OF SAMPLE (40%) --|
  Develop, tune and iterate here      Touch exactly ONCE

  Rule: you get ONE look at the out-of-sample data. If you
  look, adjust and look again, it is now in-sample and you
  have no held-out data left.

LEVEL 2 - WALK-FORWARD
  Train on window 1, test on the period immediately after.
  Roll forward. Repeat.

  |--train 1--|-test 1-|
       |--train 2--|-test 2-|
            |--train 3--|-test 3-|
                 |--train 4--|-test 4-|

  The concatenated test periods form an out-of-sample equity
  curve built from parameters that were only ever fitted on
  PRIOR data. This is much closer to how live trading works.

  Typical configuration: 3-year train, 1-year test, roll
  annually. Record whether the OPTIMAL parameters are stable
  across windows. If the best lookback is 14 in one window
  and 63 in the next, the parameter is fitting noise.

LEVEL 3 - PARAMETER SENSITIVITY SURFACE
  Do not report the best parameter set. Report the SURFACE.

  Lookback:      10     15     20     25     30     35
  Expectancy:  -0.02  +0.08  +0.31  +0.11  +0.04  -0.06

  A sharp spike at 20 surrounded by weak results is a sign
  of overfitting - you found the one setting that happened
  to work on this sample.

  Lookback:      10     15     20     25     30     35
  Expectancy:  +0.18  +0.22  +0.24  +0.23  +0.20  +0.16

  A broad plateau is what a real effect looks like. The
  precise parameter barely matters, which means you are
  capturing something structural rather than a coincidence.

ALWAYS PREFER A BROAD PLATEAU AT +0.20R OVER A SHARP PEAK
AT +0.50R. The plateau will survive contact with live data;
the peak will not.

Degrees of freedom and the overfitting budget

COUNTING YOUR DEGREES OF FREEDOM
================================
Every tunable choice is a degree of freedom (DOF):
  - each indicator parameter (lookback, multiplier, threshold)
  - each rule included or excluded
  - the universe definition
  - the timeframe
  - the stop and target multiples
  - the regime filter thresholds

EXAMPLE COUNT for the specification earlier in this guide:
  SMA lookbacks (20, 50, 200)                     3 DOF
  ATR period (14)                                 1 DOF
  Stop multiple (1.5)                             1 DOF
  Target multiple (2.5)                           1 DOF
  Time stop (15 bars)                             1 DOF
  VIX filter threshold (22)                       1 DOF
  Breakeven-move trigger (1.5 ATR)                1 DOF
  Rule set selection (6 entry conditions, each
    could have been included or not)              6 DOF
                                                 --------
  TOTAL                                          15 DOF

RULE OF THUMB
  You need a LOT of independent observations per degree of
  freedom. A commonly used heuristic is 30-50 trades per DOF
  as a bare minimum for the result to mean anything.

  15 DOF x 40 = 600 trades needed.

  If your backtest produced 85 trades, you have roughly 5.7
  trades per degree of freedom. You have not tested a
  strategy; you have drawn a curve through 85 points using
  15 parameters, which any undergraduate would recognise as
  guaranteed overfitting.

HOW TO REDUCE DOF
  - Fix parameters at conventional values and do not tune them
  - Remove rules rather than adding them; every rule you can
    delete without hurting results is a rule that was fitting
    noise
  - Test the SAME parameters across multiple instruments and
    multiple markets; if the edge is real it should appear,
    weakly, in several places
  - Prefer rules with an economic rationale over rules
    discovered by search

Why most backtests lie

Even a technically correct backtest can produce a result that will not repeat. The reasons are statistical rather than procedural, and they are the main source of the gap between simulated and live performance.

Multiple testing and p-hacking

THE MULTIPLE TESTING PROBLEM, QUANTIFIED
========================================
Suppose you test 1,000 strategy variants on the same data.
Suppose NONE of them has any real edge.

If you use a 5% significance threshold, you expect
  1,000 x 0.05 = 50 variants
to appear "significantly profitable" purely by chance.

You will find them. You will be delighted. You will pick the
best one and trade it. It will fail.

HOW MANY TESTS DO YOU ACTUALLY RUN?
  People dramatically undercount. Every one of these is a test:
    - trying a different lookback                (x8)
    - trying a different stop multiple           (x6)
    - trying a different target multiple         (x6)
    - adding or removing a filter                (x4)
    - a different instrument                     (x5)
    - a different timeframe                      (x3)

  8 x 6 x 6 x 4 x 5 x 3 = 17,280 effective tests.

  Even if you only "looked at" 40 of these explicitly, your
  SEARCH SPACE was 17,280, and your brain pruned the rest.
  The multiple-testing correction applies to the search space,
  not to the number you wrote down.

THE DEFLATED EXPECTATION
  A practical correction: whatever your best backtested
  expectancy is, assume the live figure will be substantially
  lower. Some practitioners use a 50% haircut as a starting
  assumption. A strategy showing +0.40R in a tuned backtest
  should be planned around +0.20R or less live - and if it
  is not viable at +0.20R, do not trade it.

THE HONEST PROTOCOL
  1. Decide the hypothesis and parameters BEFORE testing.
  2. Write down how many variants you intend to test.
  3. Test them. Record ALL results, not just the best.
  4. If the best result is not dramatically better than the
     distribution of all results, you found noise.
  5. Validate the single chosen variant out of sample, once.

Capacity constraints

Pressure-testing a result before risking money

  • Remove the best 5% of trades. If the strategy becomes unprofitable, it depends on a handful of outliers you cannot rely on repeating.
  • Double the cost assumption. If it dies, your edge was within the error bars of your cost model.
  • Shift every entry and exit by one bar. A real edge degrades gracefully; a fitted one collapses.
  • Perturb each parameter by ±20%. Look for a plateau, not a peak.
  • Test on a different but related instrument. A real effect should show up weakly elsewhere.
  • Test on a different period. Split by regime, not just by date.
  • Randomise the trade order and recompute drawdown. Your actual drawdown sequence was one draw from a distribution. Monte Carlo reshuffling shows you the range of drawdowns you could plausibly experience — it is usually much worse than the historical one.
  • Check the trade distribution. Are profits spread across many trades, or concentrated in three days in March 2020?
  • Inspect your ten largest winners individually. Data errors and look-ahead bias hide there.
  • Ask a sceptical friend to find the flaw. Pay them if necessary. It is cheaper than the alternative.

Monte Carlo reshuffle of a trade sequence.

Your historical maximum drawdown is ONE sample from the
distribution of drawdowns your strategy can produce. Reshuffling
the SAME trades in a different order shows the range.
Illustrative code; not a recommendation.

import numpy as np


def monte_carlo_drawdowns(r_multiples, risk_frac=0.01,
                          n_sims=10000, seed=42):
    """r_multiples: array of per-trade results in R units."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    r = np.asarray(r_multiples, dtype=float)
    results = []

    for _ in range(n_sims):
        shuffled = rng.permutation(r)
        equity = 1.0
        curve = [equity]
        for x in shuffled:
            # fixed-fractional compounding: each trade risks
            # risk_frac of CURRENT equity
            equity *= (1.0 + risk_frac * x)
            curve.append(equity)
        curve = np.array(curve)
        peak = np.maximum.accumulate(curve)
        dd = (curve - peak) / peak
        results.append({
            "final": curve[-1],
            "max_dd": dd.min(),
        })

    finals = np.array([x["final"] for x in results])
    dds = np.array([x["max_dd"] for x in results])

    return {
        "median_final_multiple": float(np.median(finals)),
        "p05_final_multiple": float(np.percentile(finals, 5)),
        "p95_final_multiple": float(np.percentile(finals, 95)),
        "median_max_dd_pct": float(np.median(dds) * 100),
        "p95_worst_dd_pct": float(np.percentile(dds, 5) * 100),
        "prob_ruin_50pct": float((dds <= -0.50).mean()),
    }


# Typical interpretation:
#   If your historical max drawdown was -18% but the Monte
#   Carlo 5th percentile is -41%, then -41% is the number you
#   must be psychologically and financially prepared for.
#   The historical -18% was luck in the ORDERING of trades.
The Monte Carlo result is the number that mattersMost traders plan around their backtested drawdown. That figure is one draw from a distribution and is almost always optimistic. Plan around the 5th-percentile reshuffled drawdown instead. If you cannot survive that number — financially and emotionally — the strategy is too aggressively sized, regardless of what the equity curve looks like.

Position sizing and the risk of ruin

Position sizing is where most traders actually fail. A positive-expectancy strategy sized badly will destroy an account; a modest strategy sized well will survive long enough for the edge to compound. Sizing is not a detail of execution — it is the dominant determinant of whether you are still trading in two years.

Portfolio heat

PORTFOLIO HEAT - THE RISK YOU FORGET TO COUNT
=============================================
HEAT = the sum of the risk on all currently open positions,
       as a % of equity.

  5 open positions at 1% risk each = 5% heat.

WHY THIS MATTERS
  If all five positions are long equity in a correlated
  market, they are not five independent bets. They are one
  bet with five tickers. A market-wide 4% down day can hit
  all five stops simultaneously.

  Your "1% risk per trade" becomes a 5% day.
  Three such days in a month = -15%.

CORRELATION-ADJUSTED HEAT
  Crude but useful adjustment:

  Effective heat = Nominal heat x sqrt(1 + (n-1) x rho)/sqrt(n)
                   ... for n equally-sized positions with
                   average pairwise correlation rho

  n=5, rho=0.0  (independent):
    5% x sqrt(1)/sqrt(5) ... effective ~2.2%  (diversified)
  n=5, rho=0.7  (typical for Indian large caps in a selloff):
    effective heat approaches the full 5%

  In a crisis, correlations go to 1. Plan for rho = 1.

PRACTICAL HEAT LIMITS
  Conservative          : 3% maximum heat
  Moderate              : 5% maximum heat
  Aggressive            : 8% maximum heat
  Reckless              : no limit at all (most retail)

  With a 5% heat cap and 1% per trade, you may hold at most
  5 positions. Wanting a sixth means closing one first.
  This constraint alone prevents a large category of blow-ups.

SECTOR AND FACTOR HEAT
  Also cap exposure by sector. Five positions in five
  different banks is one position in "banks".

Kelly and why you should use a fraction of it

THE KELLY CRITERION
===================
Kelly gives the position size that maximises the LONG-RUN
GEOMETRIC GROWTH RATE of capital.

For a bet with win probability W, payoff ratio P (win/loss):

  f* = W - (1 - W) / P

WORKED
  W = 0.45, P = 2.0
  f* = 0.45 - 0.55/2.0 = 0.45 - 0.275 = 0.175

  Kelly says risk 17.5% of capital per trade.

WHY YOU MUST NOT DO THAT
  1. KELLY ASSUMES YOU KNOW W AND P EXACTLY. You do not. You
     have estimates from a finite sample, contaminated by
     overfitting. If your true W is 0.40 rather than 0.45,
     the true Kelly is 0.10 - and betting 0.175 is OVER-BETTING,
     which produces NEGATIVE long-run growth. Kelly is brutally
     asymmetric: under-betting costs you a little growth,
     over-betting destroys you.
  2. KELLY TOLERATES ENORMOUS DRAWDOWNS. At full Kelly the
     expected maximum drawdown approaches 50-60%+. No human
     trades through that calmly.
  3. KELLY ASSUMES THE EDGE IS STABLE. Market edges decay.
  4. KELLY ASSUMES INDEPENDENT BETS. Your positions are
     correlated.

FRACTIONAL KELLY
  Common practice is to use a small fraction of full Kelly:

  Fraction   Position size   Growth vs full Kelly   Max DD
  --------   -------------   --------------------   -------
  Full       17.5%           100%                   ~55%
  1/2 Kelly   8.8%            75%                   ~33%
  1/4 Kelly   4.4%            44%                   ~19%
  1/8 Kelly   2.2%            23%                   ~10%
  1/16 Kelly  1.1%            12%                    ~5%

  Note the shape: half Kelly gives you 75% of the growth for
  60% of the drawdown. Quarter Kelly gives you 44% of the
  growth for a third of the drawdown.

  IN PRACTICE, 1/8 TO 1/4 KELLY IS WHERE SERIOUS PRACTITIONERS
  OPERATE, which lands at roughly 1-4% risk per trade - the
  same place that simple fixed-fractional reasoning arrives at.

  Kelly is therefore most useful not as a sizing rule but as
  a CEILING: if your sizing exceeds half Kelly, you are
  over-betting even under the most favourable assumptions
  about your own edge.

import math


def fixed_fractional_size(equity, risk_frac, entry, stop,
                          lot_size=1):
    """Units to trade, rounded DOWN to whole lots.
    Returns 0 if one lot would exceed the risk budget."""
    stop_distance = abs(entry - stop)
    if stop_distance <= 0:
        raise ValueError("stop must differ from entry")

    risk_rupees = equity * risk_frac
    raw_units = risk_rupees / stop_distance
    lots = math.floor(raw_units / lot_size)
    units = lots * lot_size

    # If a single lot already risks more than the budget,
    # the correct answer is NO TRADE - not "round up".
    if units == 0:
        return 0, 0.0
    return units, units * stop_distance


def atr_stop_size(equity, risk_frac, entry, atr, atr_mult=2.0,
                  lot_size=1):
    stop = entry - atr_mult * atr
    return fixed_fractional_size(equity, risk_frac, entry,
                                 stop, lot_size)


def kelly_fraction(win_rate, payoff_ratio):
    """Full Kelly. Use a FRACTION of this, never the whole."""
    if payoff_ratio <= 0:
        return 0.0
    f = win_rate - (1.0 - win_rate) / payoff_ratio
    return max(f, 0.0)


def sizing_sanity_check(equity, risk_frac, open_positions_risk,
                        max_heat=0.05):
    """Reject a new trade that would breach portfolio heat."""
    proposed_heat = (open_positions_risk + equity * risk_frac) / equity
    return {
        "proposed_heat_pct": round(proposed_heat * 100, 2),
        "max_heat_pct": round(max_heat * 100, 2),
        "allowed": proposed_heat <= max_heat,
    }

    print(units, round(risk, 2))
    # Kelly ceiling check
    k = kelly_fraction(win_rate=0.45, payoff_ratio=2.0)
    print("full kelly:", round(k, 4),

Never size by "how confident I feel". Never let one position exceed 20% of the account's notional regardless of stop distance. If you break these, nothing else in this guide can save you.

Stop losses done properly

A stop loss is a pre-commitment device. Its purpose is to make the decision to exit while you are rational, so that your irrational self cannot override it. Most of what goes wrong with stops is not about placement — it is about honouring them.

Logical versus arbitrary stops

Stop typeDefinitionAssessment
Percentage stopFixed % below entry, e.g. 5%Arbitrary. 5% is noise in a volatile small cap and a major move in a stable large cap. Use only as a crude cap.
Rupee stop"I will not lose more than ₹10,000"Arbitrary and backwards — it places the stop based on your wallet rather than on the market's structure. It does belong in sizing, not in placement.
Volatility (ATR) stopEntry − k × ATRThe sensible default. Adapts to each instrument. A 2×ATR stop has roughly comparable "noise-out" probability across instruments.
Structural stopBelow a swing low, a consolidation base, a prior breakout levelGood when the level is objectively definable. Risk: everyone sees the same level, so it attracts stop-hunting order flow.
Thesis stopExit when the reason for the trade is no longer trueIntellectually the purest. Requires the thesis to have been written down precisely enough to be falsified, which is rare.
Time stopExit after N bars regardless of P&LUnderused and valuable. If the move has not happened in the expected window, the hypothesis is not playing out. Frees capital and caps exposure.
Trailing stopStop follows price up by a fixed amount or ATR multipleGood for trend strategies; converts open profit into realised profit. Trails too tightly and you exit on noise; too loosely and you give back most of the gain.
Mental stop"I will exit if it hits X" — not entered in the systemUsually not a stop at all. See below.
Why a mental stop is usually not a stopA stop exists to remove the decision from your stressed self. A mental stop hands the decision right back at the exact moment you are least capable of making it — when the position is losing, your pulse is up, and every narrative in your head is arguing for one more minute. Experienced discretionary traders with a long track record can run mental stops. Beginners who use mental stops are, with high reliability, running no stop at all. Place the order.

Where not to place a stop

  • At a round number. 1,800.00, 25,000, 500 — clusters of stops sit there and price is drawn through them.
  • Exactly at the obvious swing low. Everyone's stop is there. Place it a volatility-scaled distance beyond, or accept being stopped on noise.
  • Inside the instrument's normal daily range. If ATR is 30 points and your stop is 12 points away, you are not managing risk; you are paying for a coin flip with costs attached.
  • At a distance determined by your position size. This is backwards. Decide the stop from the market, then derive the size from the stop.
  • Nowhere, because "it is a quality company". Quality is a reason to invest, not a reason to carry an unbounded loss on a leveraged trade.

THE TRADE-OFF
  A TIGHT trail captures more of THIS move but would have
  exited many earlier trades prematurely on normal pullbacks.
  A LOOSE trail gives back more at the top but stays in
  genuine trends.

  You cannot evaluate a trail on one trade. You must test it
  across the full trade distribution, where the question
  becomes: does the extra profit captured on the big winners
  exceed the profit lost by being shaken out of medium
  winners?

  For trend strategies the answer is usually YES for a 2.5-3.5
  ATR trail and NO for anything tighter than 1.5 ATR.
  For mean-reversion strategies, trailing stops are usually
  harmful - the strategy's premise is a bounded move, so a
  fixed target is more appropriate.

TEST YOUR TRAIL. Do not adopt one because it sounds prudent.
The move to breakevenMoving the stop to the entry price once a trade is up 1–1.5R feels like free risk reduction. It is not free: it increases the probability of a scratch exit on a normal pullback, which truncates winners. Test it. For many trend strategies it reduces expectancy. For strategies with a high proportion of trades that reverse through entry, it helps. The answer is empirical, not obvious.

Drawdowns: the thing that actually ends trading careers

Almost nobody quits trading because of a single bad trade. They quit during a drawdown — a long, grinding period where nothing works, the equity curve bleeds, and every day's decision is made under the weight of accumulated loss. Understanding drawdown maths and preparing for it in advance is the difference between a bad quarter and the end.

THE COMPOUNDING INSIGHT
  Capital destroyed is also TIME destroyed. If your strategy
  genuinely produces 15% a year:
    recovering -20% takes about 1.6 years
    recovering -40% takes about 3.7 years
    recovering -50% takes about 5.0 years
    recovering -70% takes about 8.6 years

  And during the recovery you earn nothing - you are merely
  returning to where you already were. A -50% drawdown on a

  This is why risk control is not conservatism. It is the
  mathematically optimal behaviour for anyone who intends
  to still be trading in five years.

The hardest judgement in tradingDistinguishing "my edge has decayed" from "I am in a normal bad patch" cannot be done reliably in real time with retail sample sizes. This is why the decision must be pre-committed: you write the kill criteria into the specification before you trade, so that the decision is made by the plan rather than by your state of mind during the drawdown.

A drawdown response protocol

  1. At −5%: nothing changesThis is normal. Do not adjust anything. Do not "tighten up". Continue executing the plan exactly.
  2. At −10%: audit execution, not strategyReview the last 20 trades in your journal. Did you follow every rule? Count the deviations. If there are deviations, the problem is you, not the strategy — fix adherence and continue.
  3. At −15%: halve position sizeRisk per trade goes from 1% to 0.5%. This is not a loss of nerve; it reduces the chance of a bad patch becoming unrecoverable, and it buys you more trades per unit of capital to re-establish whether the edge is intact.
  4. At −20%: stop and do a full reviewTwo weeks away from the market. Re-run the strategy's statistics on the live trades. Compare against the backtest confidence bands. Check the kill criteria. Write a conclusion before returning.
  5. At −25%: stop trading liveBack to paper trading until you can demonstrate 30 trades at the specified expectancy. This is the pre-committed line you wrote down when you were calm.
  6. At any point: if you have deviated from the plan twice in a week, stop immediatelyDeviation under pressure is the leading indicator of the large loss. Pause regardless of the P&L.
What you must never do in a drawdownIncrease position size to recover faster. Widen stops to avoid taking losses. Abandon the strategy for a new one you found last week. Add a correlated position to "average down" the overall book. Move money from savings into the trading account. Trade a new instrument you have not tested. Each of these converts a recoverable drawdown into a terminal one, and each feels entirely reasonable at the time.

The trading journal

The journal is the single highest-value habit available to a trader, and the one most consistently skipped. Without it you have no data about your own behaviour, which means you cannot distinguish a strategy problem from an execution problem, and you cannot improve anything that is not measured.

What to record, and when

Pre-trade (before the order)

Hypothesis, setup tag, entry trigger, planned stop, planned target, position size calculation, R value in rupees, portfolio heat after this trade, conviction level, your emotional state, and anything about the market context. Written before you can be influenced by the outcome.

Post-trade (after the exit)

Actual fills, actual costs, R-multiple, exit reason, whether every rule was followed, what you would do differently, and — critically — a separate assessment of decision quality versus outcome. A good decision can lose money. A bad decision can make money.

Separate decision quality from outcomeGrade every trade twice: once on whether you followed your process (A–F), once on the financial result. Over time, the correlation between these two grades is the most informative statistic you will ever compute about yourself. If your F-graded decisions are profitable, you are being rewarded for bad behaviour, which is the most dangerous situation in trading.

Review cadence

  1. Daily (10 minutes, after the close)Complete post-trade entries for anything closed. Note any deviation. Do not analyse — just record. Analysis after a losing day is contaminated.
  2. Weekly (45 minutes, weekend)Re-read every pre-trade entry from the week and ask whether the hypothesis was honest. Count deviations. Compute week's R total and running expectancy. Note anything to investigate.
  3. Monthly (2 hours)Full metrics: expectancy, win rate, payoff ratio, profit factor, drawdown, cost ratio, expectancy by tag and by process grade. Compare against the backtest confidence bands.
  4. Quarterly (half a day)Go/no-go decision on each strategy against the kill criteria. Re-measure the cost model. Review whether market regime has changed. Decide what to stop doing.
  5. AnnuallyCompare total net after-tax return against simply having bought a broad index fund with the same capital. Be honest about the hours spent. This is the only comparison that matters.

Trading psychology, concretely

Most writing on trading psychology is vague encouragement. This section treats psychological failures as observable behaviours with measurable costs and specific countermeasures, because that is the only version of the topic that is actionable.

The central reframe"Fear" and "greed" are not causes. They are symptoms of a position that is too large. A 0.5%-risk position does not produce fear. A 6%-risk position produces fear in everyone, including professionals. When you notice an emotional problem, the first hypothesis should always be a sizing problem, not a character problem.

WHY LEVERAGE DEGRADES DECISION-MAKING

THE PRACTICAL TEST
  Find the position size at which you can walk away from the
  screen for two hours without anxiety. That is your maximum
  size. It is almost certainly smaller than what you are
  currently trading.

  If you cannot sleep with the position on, the position is
  too big. This is not a metaphor - it is a usable, reliable,
  calibrated instrument that you already own.

The circuit-breaker rule

PRE-COMMITTED CIRCUIT BREAKERS
==============================
Write these down. Put them where you can see them. Automate
them if your broker allows it.

TRADE-LEVEL
  - Stop order placed at the same moment as the entry order.
    Not after. Not "in a minute". The same moment.
  - Stop is never widened. There is no exception clause.

DAILY
  - Maximum 3 losing trades, then stop for the day.
  - Maximum daily loss 3% of equity, then stop for the day.
  - Maximum 5 trades per day regardless of outcome.
  - 60-minute cooling-off after any loss before a new entry.

WEEKLY
  - Maximum weekly loss 6% of equity, then stop for the week.
  - If 2+ rule deviations occurred this week, stop for the
    following week and review.

MONTHLY
  - Maximum monthly loss 10%, then stop for the month and
    conduct a full review before resuming.

ACCOUNT-LEVEL
  - At -15% from peak: halve position size.
  - At -20% from peak: stop and conduct a full review.
  - At -25% from peak: return to paper trading until 30
    trades at the specified expectancy are demonstrated.

PERSONAL-STATE
  Do not trade when:
  - You slept under 6 hours
  - You are ill, angry, grieving, or under acute stress
  - You have had alcohol in the last 12 hours
  - You are in a financial emergency and "need" a win
  - You have a major deadline at work today
  - You just had an argument

THE ONLY RULE ABOUT THESE RULES
  They must be written BEFORE you need them, because the
  moment you need them is the moment you will be least
  capable of writing them. A rule invented during a drawdown
  is a rationalisation, not a rule.
If you break a circuit breaker once, you will break it againTreat the first violation as a serious incident. Log it. Analyse it like a production outage: what was the trigger, what was the state, what control failed, what control would have prevented it. Traders who normalise rule violations do not gradually deteriorate — they continue apparently fine until one violation coincides with a large adverse move, and then the account is gone.

Routine and process

Discretion fails under pressure; routine does not. The purpose of a routine is to move as many decisions as possible out of the live session, where your judgement is worst, into prepared time, where it is best.

The pre-market routine

  1. Personal state check (2 min)Sleep, health, stress, deadlines, emotional state. If any flag is raised, today is a no-trade day. Log it as such.
  2. Account state (3 min)Current equity, open positions, current portfolio heat, distance from the drawdown circuit breakers, available margin, any pending corporate actions or expiries.
  3. Calendar check (3 min)Scheduled events today and in the next three sessions — policy decisions, major data releases, results for anything you hold, expiry dates, and any no-trade windows your specification defines.
  4. Scan for signals (10–15 min)Run the specification over the universe. Produce a list. Do not look at anything outside the list.
  5. Pre-compute every trade (10 min)For each candidate: entry, stop, target, size, rupee risk, resulting heat. Write the pre-trade journal entry now, before the session opens, while you are calm.
  6. Write the no-trade conditions (1 min)"I will not trade if X." Having it written removes the mid-session argument.
  7. Close the newsSocial media, television, forums. You have your list. Nothing you read now will improve it; it can only cause deviation.

In-session rules

  • Execute only trades from the pre-market list. No additions. Ever.
  • Place the stop order at the same moment as the entry order.
  • No position size changes to an open trade unless the specification defines scaling.
  • No chart-watching between signals. Set alerts and close the terminal.
  • No P&L watching. Watching P&L causes exits; watching the plan causes execution.
  • If you feel the urge to deviate, write down the proposed deviation and the reason before acting. Most urges do not survive being written down.
  • Honour the daily circuit breakers immediately, without negotiation.
  • Avoid the first and last 15 minutes unless the strategy explicitly requires them.

When not to trade at all

No signal

The specification produced no candidates. This is the most common correct outcome. A trader who trades every day has a specification with no filter, which is to say no strategy.

Regime filter off

Volatility outside the tested range, or the trend filter inactive. The strategy was never tested in this regime; running it here is live experimentation with real money.

Event windows

Policy decisions, budgets, results for a position you hold. Unless your strategy is specifically an event strategy, these are periods of unmodelled risk.

Personal state

Under-slept, ill, stressed, angry, recently argued, drinking last night, major work deadline. The cost of not trading is zero; the cost of trading badly is not.

Circuit breaker active

Daily, weekly or monthly loss limit hit. The rule exists precisely for the moment when you want to override it.

Needing money

If you need this month's trading to cover an expense, your decisions are already compromised. Trading capital must be genuinely discretionary.

  1. The kill switch must be OUTSIDE the strategy code and
     must work even if the strategy is in a bad state.
  2. Risk limits are enforced at the order layer, not in
     the strategy. The strategy cannot be trusted.
  3. Reconcile against broker state on every restart.
     NEVER assume your in-memory position is correct.
  4. Fail closed. On any uncertainty, place no orders.

"""
Risk gate enforced OUTSIDE strategy code.
Illustrative skeleton. Not production code, not a recommendation.
"""

from dataclasses import dataclass, field
from datetime import date


@dataclass
class RiskLimits:
    max_risk_per_trade_frac: float = 0.01
    max_portfolio_heat_frac: float = 0.05
    max_positions: int = 5
    max_daily_loss_frac: float = 0.03
    max_orders_per_day: int = 20
    max_notional_frac: float = 0.60


@dataclass
class RiskState:
    equity: float
    open_risk_rupees: float = 0.0
    open_positions: int = 0
    realised_pnl_today: float = 0.0
    orders_today: int = 0
    open_notional: float = 0.0
    halted: bool = False
    halt_reasons: list = field(default_factory=list)


class RiskGate:
    """Every order passes through here. The strategy cannot
    bypass it. If any check fails, the order is rejected and
    the reason is logged."""

    def __init__(self, limits: RiskLimits):
        self.limits = limits

    def check(self, state: RiskState, order_risk_rupees: float,
              order_notional: float):
        reasons = []
        L = self.limits

        if state.halted:
            reasons.append("system halted: " + ",".join(state.halt_reasons))

        if order_risk_rupees > state.equity * L.max_risk_per_trade_frac:
            reasons.append("per-trade risk exceeds limit")

        new_heat = (state.open_risk_rupees + order_risk_rupees) / state.equity
        if new_heat > L.max_portfolio_heat_frac:
            reasons.append(f"portfolio heat {new_heat:.3f} exceeds limit")

        if state.open_positions >= L.max_positions:
            reasons.append("max concurrent positions reached")

        daily_loss_frac = -state.realised_pnl_today / state.equity
        if daily_loss_frac >= L.max_daily_loss_frac:
            reasons.append("daily loss circuit breaker tripped")

        if state.orders_today >= L.max_orders_per_day:
            reasons.append("daily order count limit reached")

        new_notional = (state.open_notional + order_notional) / state.equity
        if new_notional > L.max_notional_frac:
            reasons.append("gross notional exposure limit")

        return {"allowed": len(reasons) == 0, "reasons": reasons}


def reconcile(local_positions, broker_positions):
    """NEVER trust in-memory state after a restart.
    Any mismatch halts the system - it does not 'fix itself'."""
    mismatches = []
    keys = set(local_positions) | set(broker_positions)
    for k in keys:
        a = local_positions.get(k, 0)
        b = broker_positions.get(k, 0)
        if a != b:
            mismatches.append({"symbol": k, "local": a, "broker": b})
    return {"ok": not mismatches, "mismatches": mismatches}

Dabba trading — do not

Dabba (bucket) trading is illegalDabba trading is off-exchange betting on the prices of listed securities, operated by unregistered intermediaries. Orders never reach the exchange. It is marketed on lower costs, no STT, higher leverage and no KYC. The reality: you have no exchange protection, no clearing corporation guarantee, no investor grievance mechanism, no legal recourse when the operator refuses to pay, and you are participating in an illegal activity that has attracted enforcement action and tax proceedings. If someone offers you trading with no STT and 50x leverage, you are being offered a crime with a high probability of being defrauded.

Scams, tip services and the "profitable system" industry

A large, profitable industry exists around selling trading signals, courses and "algos" to retail participants. Understanding its business model tells you everything about whether its products work.

How to verify a SEBI registration

  1. Ask for the registration numberA SEBI-registered Research Analyst (RA) or Investment Adviser (RIA) has a registration number in a defined format and is required to disclose it. Refusal to provide it is a complete answer.

The honest conclusion

Everything above assumed you were going to trade. This section asks whether you should, and tries to answer it without flattery.

Who should not trade

  • Anyone without an emergency fund, insurance, and a funded long-term investment plan already running.
  • Anyone trading with borrowed money, or with money that has a purpose.
  • Anyone who cannot give the market consistent, sustainable time during session hours for the style they have chosen.
  • Anyone who cannot write down a falsifiable hypothesis with an economic rationale.
  • Anyone who has not tested a specification over 100+ trades with realistic cost modelling.
  • Anyone who needs trading income to meet expenses.
  • Anyone who has increased size to recover a loss, more than once.
  • Anyone whose trading affects their sleep, their work, or their relationships.
  • Anyone following tips, signals or paid "calls".
  • Anyone who cannot state their expectancy, their maximum drawdown and their cost ratio from their own records.

Who might reasonably trade

  • Someone with a secure income, a fully funded long-term portfolio, and genuinely discretionary risk capital.
  • Someone who treats it as a serious, measured, loss-making-until-proven-otherwise activity rather than an income source.
  • Someone with the engineering discipline to specify, test, instrument and review — and the honesty to kill a strategy that fails its criteria.
  • Someone operating in a low-frequency, capacity-constrained niche where small size is an advantage rather than a handicap.
  • Someone who finds the research genuinely interesting independent of the money, because for a long time there will be no money.
  • Someone who has written down, in advance, the drawdown at which they will stop — and who will honour it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What percentage of retail traders in India actually make money?
SEBI's study published in September 2024, covering FY 2021–22 to FY 2023–24, found roughly 93% of individual F&O traders made losses, with aggregate individual net losses in the region of ₹1.8 lakh crore. A 2025 follow-up covering FY 2024–25 found roughly 91% of individual index-option traders losing. Studies of cash-segment intraday trading show a similar majority losing, with the loss rate rising with trade frequency. Verify the current figures at sebi.gov.in — the methodology and periods change, but the direction has been consistent across every version.
Can I realistically trade while working full time as a software engineer?
Intraday and scalping: no. Not "difficult" — structurally incompatible, because those styles require continuous attention during exactly the hours you are being paid to do something else, and they are also the most cost-sensitive styles. Swing and positional trading on a daily timeframe are compatible: signals are evaluated after the close, orders are placed before the open, and the time demand is 30–60 minutes a day plus a weekly review. If your plan requires watching charts during work hours, the plan is already broken and you will do both things badly.
How much capital do I need to start?
Enough that fixed costs do not dominate and that a single position can be sized at 1–2% risk. Below roughly ₹2 lakh, flat per-order brokerage and minimum lot sizes make responsible position sizing arithmetically impossible — one position becomes 15–30% of the account. More importantly, the capital must be genuinely discretionary: money whose total loss changes nothing material in your life. For most people that number is smaller than their enthusiasm, and the honest answer is to paper trade until the capital exists.
Is technical analysis useful or is it astrology?
Neither extreme is right. Some concepts have genuine content — volatility measures like ATR for stop placement and sizing, liquidity and volume for execution quality, and simple trend filters as regime indicators. Much of the rest — named chart patterns, Fibonacci levels, elaborate indicator combinations — has weak and regime-dependent evidence, is visible to millions of participants simultaneously, and is extremely prone to overfitting when tested. The practical position: use a minimal number of simple, economically motivated indicators, test them honestly out of sample, and be suspicious of anything that requires you to interpret a chart subjectively, because subjectivity cannot be backtested.
What is a realistic return for a good retail trader?
The question is badly posed, because the expected value for an untested retail trader is negative. If you have demonstrated a positive expectancy out of sample over 200+ trades, and you size at 1% risk with a +0.20R expectancy and 120 trades a year, the arithmetic gives roughly 24% a year gross before tax — with drawdowns likely to reach 20–30% along the way, and with a wide distribution around that figure. After the marginal tax rate on business income, that becomes roughly 16–17% net. Compare that honestly to a passive index portfolio requiring two hours a year. The comparison is closer than most traders want it to be, and that is for the successful case.
How do I know if my strategy actually has an edge?
You cannot know with certainty; you can only accumulate evidence. The minimum bar: a written specification with no hidden discretion, 200+ trades including a genuinely held-out out-of-sample period, realistic cost and slippage modelling, parameter sensitivity showing a broad plateau rather than a sharp peak, performance across at least two distinct market regimes, and a Monte Carlo reshuffle showing a drawdown you could survive. Even then, deflate your measured expectancy substantially to account for the multiple testing you did not count. Fewer than 100 trades tells you essentially nothing, which is why most retail conviction is formed on noise.
What is the most common reason retail traders lose?
In order of magnitude: (1) no edge to begin with, compounded by the near-zero-sum structure of derivatives; (2) transaction costs, which scale linearly with frequency and routinely exceed 30–40% of a small account per year; (3) position sizing that is far too large, making normal variance fatal; (4) failure to honour stops, converting −1R losses into −3R losses; and (5) behavioural escalation — revenge trading, averaging down, and size increases after wins. Note that only the first is about market knowledge. Four of the five are about process and discipline, which are fully within your control.
Should I use a stop loss on every trade?
You need a defined exit on every trade — that is non-negotiable. Whether it is a resting stop order depends on the instrument and the strategy. For liquid instruments and intraday or swing strategies, place the order. For positional strategies in volatile instruments, a stop placed inside the normal noise range will take you out repeatedly at a cost; a wider stop with a smaller position is usually better. Understand that no stop protects against an overnight gap or a lower-circuit lock, so your position sizing must survive an outcome roughly three times worse than the stop implies. A "mental stop" is, for most beginners, not a stop at all.
How much should I risk per trade?
One to two percent of trading capital, with a hard cap on total portfolio heat of around 5%. The Monte Carlo evidence is stark: with a genuinely positive-expectancy strategy, risking 2% per trade gives roughly a 14% chance of a 50% drawdown over 500 trades, while risking 5% gives roughly a 68% chance. Risk of ruin is sharply non-linear in position size. Professional practitioners typically operate at an eighth to a quarter of full Kelly, which lands in the same 1–4% range. If your sizing exceeds half Kelly on your own (probably optimistic) estimates, you are over-betting.
What drawdown should I expect?
Larger and longer than your backtest suggests. Take your backtested maximum drawdown, compute the Monte Carlo reshuffled 5th-percentile drawdown, take the larger, and multiply by 1.5 — that is your planning number. For a trend-following strategy with a 35% win rate, expect losing streaks of 14 or more trades over a few hundred trades, which means weeks of consecutive losses. The duration of a flat period is usually harder to endure than the depth of the decline. Plan for a full year with no progress and decide now whether you could tolerate that.
How are trading profits taxed in India?
Broadly: intraday equity is treated as speculative business income; exchange-traded F&O is non-speculative business income; delivery-based equity is capital gains unless the facts indicate a business. Business income is taxed at slab rates, which for a well-paid engineer means the top marginal rate — materially worse than the concessional capital-gains treatment on long-held equity. Business treatment does allow expense deduction (brokerage, data, equipment depreciation, professional fees). Turnover for F&O has a specific definition and a tax audit may be required above certain thresholds. Losses are only carried forward if the return is filed on time. As at FY 2026–27, verify at incometax.gov.in — note the Income-tax Act 2025 took effect from 1 April 2026 — and engage a chartered accountant.
Is algorithmic trading allowed for retail investors in India?
Yes, within a framework. SEBI has issued rules covering broker responsibility for algos routed through their systems, unique identification of algo orders, order-per-second thresholds above which exchange approval is required, the treatment of third-party algo providers via broker empanelment, and a defined path for self-developed algos by individual investors. Advertising assured or indicative returns from an algorithm is not permitted. The framework has been revised and implementation extended more than once — verify the current position at sebi.gov.in and with your broker before automating anything. Separately: be extremely sceptical of "proven algo" products sold to retail with performance claims.
How do I verify whether an advisor or tip service is legitimate?
Ask for their SEBI Research Analyst or Investment Adviser registration number and verify it directly in SEBI's published lists at sebi.gov.in — check the name, the category, and that the registration is current. Check for enforcement orders against them. Confirm the fee structure complies with SEBI's constraints: no guaranteed returns, no profit sharing, no handling of your funds, no access to your trading account. If they refuse to supply a number, show only edited screenshots, use urgency tactics, or offer "no STT, high leverage" arrangements (which is dabba trading, and illegal), stop immediately. The economics are also informative: someone with a genuine edge raises capital and trades it rather than selling signals for ₹3,000 a month.
Should I quit my job to trade full time?
No. The base rates alone make this indefensible, but there is a second reason that is specific and practical: trading with capital you need to live on destroys the decision-making that trading requires. Every position becomes a rent payment. You will take trades you should skip, hold losers you should cut, and size up to meet a monthly target that the market has no obligation to supply. People who successfully trade full time have typically done so for years part time first, with a demonstrated and audited edge, and with living expenses covered from capital or another source for a long runway. If you are asking the question, the answer is no.
What should I do instead, if I conclude trading is not for me?
Direct your effort to the three things with far better expected value: increase your income (skills, specialisation, visible work — a 25% compensation increase on a ₹30 lakh package is ₹7.5 lakh a year, recurring, with no capital at risk); increase your savings rate; and invest the surplus systematically in low-cost diversified index funds with an asset allocation suited to your horizon. Automate it and leave it alone. Then keep the analytical skills from this guide — expectancy thinking, base rates, honest testing, separating decision quality from outcome — and apply them to engineering and career decisions, where the odds are overwhelmingly in your favour.

Trading-readiness checklist and a 90-day paper-trading programme

Two closing tools. The first is an honest gate. The second is a structured programme that costs nothing and will tell you, within a quarter, whether you should proceed.

Readiness checklist

Gate 1 — financial preconditions (every item must be true)

  • Emergency fund of 6–12 months of expenses, fully funded and untouched.
  • Health insurance in place; term life cover if anyone depends on you.
  • No high-interest debt — no credit card balances, no personal loans.
  • A long-term investment plan running on automatic, funded monthly.
  • Trading capital is genuinely surplus; its total loss changes nothing material.
  • No borrowed money in the trading account; no long-term portfolio pledged as margin.
  • A separate account for trading, with its own accounting.

Gate 2 — knowledge preconditions

  • I can compute expectancy in R, and I know why win rate alone is meaningless.
  • I can compute position size from equity, risk percentage and stop distance.
  • I know my round-trip cost as a percentage of capital at risk, for the instruments I trade.
  • I understand gap risk and circuit limits and have sized for a 3R adverse outcome.
  • I understand the difference between in-sample and out-of-sample, and why 85 trades with 15 parameters proves nothing.
  • I know the tax treatment of what I intend to trade and the record-keeping it requires.
  • I can explain, in one sentence, who is on the other side of my trade and why they are wrong.

Gate 3 — process preconditions

  • A written strategy specification with hypothesis, universe, timeframe, entry, exit, stop, filters, sizing, cost model and kill criteria.
  • A backtest over 200+ trades with realistic costs, with a held-out out-of-sample period touched once.
  • Parameter sensitivity shows a plateau, not a peak.
  • A Monte Carlo drawdown estimate I could financially and emotionally survive.
  • A journal template ready, with pre-trade and post-trade sections and a process grade.
  • Written circuit breakers: per-trade, daily, weekly, monthly and account-level.
  • A pre-committed drawdown level at which I stop entirely.
  • A pre-committed date at which I compare results against a passive benchmark and decide whether to continue.
Any unchecked box in Gate 1 is disqualifyingGates 2 and 3 can be completed with study and work. Gate 1 cannot be worked around. If the emergency fund is not there, or the debt is not cleared, or the capital is not genuinely surplus, no amount of skill will make the activity appropriate.

THE 90-DAY GO / NO-GO DECISION RULE
===================================
Write this down in week 1, before you have any results.

GO (proceed to live trading at MINIMUM size) requires ALL of:
  [ ] Out-of-sample backtest expectancy  > +0.10R after costs
  [ ] Paper-trading expectancy           > +0.10R after costs
  [ ] Simulated trades                   >= 60
  [ ] Rule deviations                    <= 2 across 90 days
  [ ] Monte Carlo 5th-pctile drawdown    survivable
  [ ] Cost ratio (costs / gross profit)  < 40%
  [ ] Gate 1 financial preconditions     all true
  [ ] Written circuit breakers           in place and tested
  [ ] A written stop-trading drawdown    committed

IF ALL NINE ARE TRUE
  Go live at HALF your intended risk per trade (0.5%, not 1%)
  for the first 50 live trades. Live results are systematically
  worse than paper results - slippage is real, fills are worse,
  and emotion is present for the first time.
  Re-review after 50 live trades before increasing size.

IF ANY ARE FALSE
  Do not go live. Choose one:
   (a) Fix the specific failure and extend the programme by
       30 days - ONCE. Not repeatedly; repeated extension is
       overfitting your own process.
   (b) Reject the hypothesis and either develop a new one or
       conclude that trading is not for you.

  Option (b) after 90 days of honest work is a SUCCESS, not a
  failure. You will have learned the most valuable lesson
  available in this domain at a cost of zero rupees, which is
  precisely the opposite of how most people learn it.

THE HONEST PRIOR
  Most specifications fail at week 5 (out-of-sample) or at
  week 14 (deviation count). Both failures are informative.
  The out-of-sample failure tells you the edge was never
  there. The deviation failure tells you that even with an
  edge, you would not have executed it.
  Each costs nothing to discover now, and a great deal to
  discover with money on the table.
One last honest note about paper tradingPaper results overstate live results, consistently and materially. There is no slippage you did not choose, no partial fill, no rejected order, no margin call, no broker outage, and above all no fear. Model costs aggressively, assume you get the worse side of every spread, and treat a marginally positive paper expectancy as equivalent to a negative live one. If your paper expectancy is +0.12R, plan as though it is zero.
Closing noteThis guide has given you the maths, the process and the standards of serious trading. It has deliberately not given you a strategy, because any edge that can be written down and distributed stops being an edge. The evidence from SEBI's own participant data is that the overwhelming majority of Indian individuals who trade actively lose money, and that the losses are very large in aggregate. Nothing in this guide changes that base rate; it only changes whether you find out cheaply or expensively. If you work through the 90-day programme honestly and conclude that you do not have an edge, you have achieved the best realistically available outcome.
