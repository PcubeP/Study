How to Use This Guide

This is a working manual on startup equity and fundraising for an Indian software engineer who has — or is about to have — a company. It covers the arithmetic of cap tables, the instruments Indian investors actually use, the clauses in an Indian term sheet and what is market on each, the FEMA filings that will blow up your diligence if you skipped them, ESOPs end to end with the tax worked out, the non-dilutive money most founders never apply for, and the exits. Every number is worked. Every form is named. Every portal is named.

This is educational material, not legal, tax or investment adviceSecurities law, exchange control and tax law in India are statutory, fact-specific and change with every Budget, every RBI Master Direction amendment and every MCA notification. Nothing here is advice on your facts. Before you sign a term sheet, issue a single share, grant an ESOP, accept foreign money, or claim a tax benefit, engage a qualified Company Secretary (CS), Chartered Accountant (CA) and a corporate lawyer, and have them confirm the current position for your situation. Every rate, threshold, fee, form number, due date and scheme limit in this guide is stated as at FY 2025-26 / early 2026 and must be re-verified against the official portal before you act on it.

Should You Raise At All?

Venture capital is not funding. It is the sale of a controlling interest in your future, in instalments, to people who are legally obliged to return 3–5x their fund to their own investors inside ten years. That obligation is the single fact that explains every term in a term sheet and every board conversation you will have for the next decade. If your business cannot plausibly return a fund, taking venture money is not ambitious — it is a category error that will end in a forced sale or a wind-down.

What a venture fund actually is

An Indian VC fund is usually registered with SEBI as a Category I or Category II Alternative Investment Fund (AIF) under the SEBI (Alternative Investment Funds) Regulations, 2012. It raises capital from Limited Partners (LPs) — family offices, HNIs, fund-of-funds including SIDBI's FFS, insurance companies, sovereign funds. It has a fixed life, usually 10 years with two one-year extensions. It charges roughly 2% a year in management fees and takes 20% of profits (carry) above a hurdle. The economics are brutal and they are not negotiable by you.

What raising commits you to — the explicit list

  • A board of directors that meets at least quarterly, with a pack circulated in advance and minutes recorded under Section 118 of the Companies Act, 2013.
  • Monthly or quarterly MIS to investors, on a deadline, forever.
  • Reserved matters: a defined list of decisions you cannot take without investor consent — new debt, new hires above a salary band, budget approval, any new share issue, any acquisition, any change in business.
  • An annual statutory audit plus, usually, an investor-mandated audit firm from an approved list (often a Big 4 or a next-tier firm), which costs multiples of your current audit.
  • Founder vesting that restarts or re-cuts at the round — you will re-earn equity you thought you owned.
  • Lock-in: you cannot sell your own shares without consent, often until a liquidity event.
  • An exit obligation: a clause requiring the company and founders to use best efforts to provide an exit to the investor within 5–7 years, sometimes with a buyback or drag-along fallback.
  • A permanent asymmetry: the investor has downside protection (liquidation preference) and you do not.
  • Growth expectations set by the round, not by the market — a ₹8 crore seed at ₹40 crore post means the Series A needs ₹150–250 crore of value, which means roughly 3x revenue growth in 18 months.

Businesses that should almost never raise institutional VC

Services and consulting firms

Revenue scales linearly with headcount. Gross margins cap at 35–55%. There is no multiple expansion. A ₹20 crore (₹200,000,000) revenue dev shop at 15% net margin is a superb life and an unfundable asset. Fund it from retained earnings and a working capital line.

Single-market niche tools

A ₹3–15 crore ARR ceiling is a great outcome for you and a rounding error for a fund. Taking ₹10 crore at a ₹60 crore valuation means you must now reach ₹300 crore+ or deliver nothing.

Lifestyle-optimised businesses

If "I want to work four days a week and take August off" is a non-negotiable, do not sell 20% of your company to people whose entire model assumes you will not.

Low-defensibility arbitrage

Reselling someone else's API with a UI on top. Capital accelerates the copying, not the moat.

Businesses already profitable and growing 30–50%

If you are compounding at 40% on retained earnings, equity is the most expensive capital available to you. Venture debt or revenue-based financing at 16–20% is cheaper than selling equity that will be worth 10x in four years.

Pre-product, pre-customer, pre-insight

Raising before you have evidence sets a valuation you will spend two years trying to grow into. A ₹30 crore pre-seed with no revenue is a trap, not a trophy.

The honest self-testWrite down your realistic best case: revenue in year 7, gross margin, and the exit multiple paid for comparable Indian companies in your category. Multiply. If the answer is under ₹500 crore (₹5,000,000,000), institutional VC is a poor fit regardless of how good the business is. Angels and micro-VCs can still work — their fund math tolerates ₹100–300 crore outcomes. Match the capital to the ceiling.

Equity 101 — The Vocabulary You Must Get Right

Almost every cap-table disaster in an Indian startup traces back to confusing two terms in this section. Authorised capital is not issued capital. Paid-up capital is not valuation. A share's face value has nothing to do with its price. Get these right and the arithmetic in the next four sections becomes mechanical.

The single most common round-blocking errorYou agree a ₹5 crore (₹50,000,000) round, sign the SSA, receive the money — and then discover your authorised capital is ₹1 lakh (₹100,000) and you cannot allot the shares. Increasing authorised capital requires a shareholders' meeting, SH-7, and MCA fees, which adds 7–15 days and can breach your 60-day allotment deadline under Section 42(6) of the Companies Act, 2013. Check authorised capital the day you sign the term sheet, not the day you allot. Verify the current SH-7 fee schedule at mca.gov.in.

Face value, premium and why the price is not the face value

Every equity share has a face value (par value) — typically ₹10 or ₹1 in India, sometimes ₹100. When an investor pays more than face value, the excess is securities premium, credited to the Securities Premium Account under Section 52 of the Companies Act, 2013. Securities premium is not free cash to the P&L; its uses are restricted by Section 52(2) (issuing bonus shares, writing off preliminary expenses, buyback, etc.).

Cap Tables From Scratch

A cap table is a spreadsheet that answers one question: who owns what, on what basis, right now and after the next event. It is also a legal record that must reconcile to your Register of Members, your PAS-3 filings and your FC-GPR filings. Investors will ask for it in the first meeting and diligence will test it line by line. Build it properly on day one; retrofitting one after three ad-hoc issuances is a week of pain and a re-pricing risk.

The structure of a usable cap table

and reissue of share certificates. Avoid it by planning at Day 0.
Why ₹1 face value is the practical defaultNothing in the Companies Act, 2013 prescribes a minimum face value for equity shares of a private company. ₹10 is traditional; ₹1 is operationally better because it gives you 10× the granularity for ESOP grants and small advisor allotments without sub-dividing later. Confirm with your CS, because a few forms and some stamp duty schedules assume ₹10 and your state's stamp duty on share certificates may be computed differently. Verify at mca.gov.in and with your state stamp act.

Founder Equity Split and Vesting

More Indian startups die of founder conflict than of competition. The equity split and the vesting schedule are the two documents that determine whether a co-founder leaving in month 14 is an inconvenience or an extinction event. Both must be decided while everyone still likes each other, and both must be in a signed Founders' Agreement and reflected in the Articles of Association.

The 50:50 trap and the dynamic split

Equal splits are chosen because they feel fair and because the conversation is uncomfortable. They are frequently wrong, because contribution is rarely equal and because a 50:50 split with no tie-breaker creates deadlock in exactly the situations where a decision is most urgent.

SplitWhen it is rightFailure mode
50:50 (two founders)Genuinely equal commitment, capital, idea origination and risk; both full-time from day oneDeadlock. No casting vote. One founder coasts and still owns half.
60:40 or 55:45One founder originated the idea, worked on it longer, or took a bigger pay cutMinimal. This is the healthiest common outcome.
70:30 or more skewedOne founder is clearly the driver; the other joined later or part-timeThe 30% founder loses motivation if the gap is not justified by facts.
Equal thirds (three founders)Rarely rightThree-way deadlock; and three founders at 33% each means a seed round leaves each under 22%
Anything under 10% for a full-time co-founderAlmost neverInvestors will flag it as a retention risk and may insist on a top-up from the pool

Founder vesting — non-negotiable, even if nobody is asking for it

Founder vesting means your own shares are subject to a repurchase right in favour of the company (or the continuing founders) if you leave before the schedule completes. Without it, a co-founder who quits in month 9 keeps 40% of your company forever, contributes nothing, and blocks your Series A because no investor will fund a cap table with a 40% dead shareholder.

Buyback of founder shares is legally constrained in IndiaA company buying back its own shares must comply with Section 68 of the Companies Act, 2013: buyback is limited to 25% of paid-up capital and free reserves in a financial year, requires free reserves/securities premium/proceeds of a fresh issue, a board or special resolution, a solvency declaration, and Forms SH-8/SH-9/SH-11. A startup with accumulated losses often has no free reserves and therefore cannot legally buy back. The practical workaround used by Indian lawyers is a compulsory transfer of unvested shares to the continuing founders or a nominee at a nominal price, enforced through the Articles of Association and the Founders' Agreement — not a Section 68 buyback. Have your corporate lawyer draft it that way from the start. Verify Section 68 and the Rules at mca.gov.in.

Why single trigger can cost you moneyIf 100% of founder equity accelerates on signing, the acquirer has nothing holding the founders. They respond by reducing the headline price and re-granting a large retention package in acquirer stock with its own vesting. You end up with the same economics, delivered later, with more risk. Double trigger with a 12-month protection window is the right ask.

The Founders' Agreement — clauses that actually matter

  1. Equity split and share numbersExact share counts, not just percentages. Percentages move; share counts do not.
  2. Roles, titles and decision rightsWho is CEO. Who breaks a tie. What decisions need both signatures. Write it down even if it feels unnecessary.
  3. Vesting and cliffCommencement date, schedule, credit for prior work, and the exact mechanism (transfer to continuing founders at nominal value, enforced via the AOA).
  4. Good leaver / bad leaver definitionsBad leaver should be narrow: fraud, wilful misconduct, criminal conviction, material breach after notice. Everything else is good leaver. Resignation is usually a neutral event — vested is kept, unvested is forfeited.
  5. Full-time commitment and exclusivityNo other employment, no competing venture, with a defined exception list if someone is winding down a consultancy.
  6. IP assignmentAll IP created before and after incorporation is assigned to the company, with a specific schedule listing pre-existing IP. This single clause is checked in every technical diligence.
  7. ConfidentialitySurvives termination indefinitely for trade secrets.
  8. Non-compete and non-solicitNon-compete during the term is enforceable; post-termination non-compete is largely unenforceable in India under Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 (restraint of trade is void). Non-solicitation of employees and customers is more defensible. Draft accordingly and do not rely on a US-style non-compete.
  9. Transfer restrictionsROFR in favour of the other founders; absolute prohibition on transfer to a competitor; tag-along if one founder sells.
  10. Deadlock resolutionEscalation to a named advisor, then mediation, then a buy-sell ("shotgun") clause as a last resort.
  11. CompensationAgreed salaries, when they start, and the rule for changing them.
  12. Dispute resolution and governing lawArbitration under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, seat specified (Bengaluru/Mumbai/Delhi), sole arbitrator, English language.
  13. Mirror into the ArticlesTransfer restrictions and buyback mechanics must be in the Articles of Association to bind the company and be enforceable against a transferee. A Founders' Agreement alone binds only the signatories.
Section 27, Indian Contract Act 1872 — verify with counselAgreements in restraint of trade are void in India, with narrow exceptions (sale of goodwill, and restraints operating during the term of employment). Courts have repeatedly struck down post-employment non-competes. Do not build your founder-departure strategy on a non-compete. Build it on vesting, IP assignment, confidentiality and non-solicitation. Confirm the current case law position with a litigator before drafting.

Why Indian VCs use CCPS and not plain equity

Downside protection

A liquidation preference attaches to a preference share, not to an equity share. If the company sells for less than the post-money, the CCPS holder gets their money back first. You cannot replicate this with ordinary equity.

Anti-dilution via conversion ratio

CCPS converts at a ratio. If a down round triggers broad-based weighted average protection, the ratio adjusts and the investor gets more equity shares — without a fresh issue, fresh pricing or a fresh FC-GPR for the adjustment itself.

FEMA-compliant

Compulsorily convertible = equity instrument under the NDI Rules 2019. Foreign money can come in under the automatic route with ordinary pricing-guideline compliance.

Separate class for separate rights

Series Seed CCPS and Series A CCPS are different classes with different preferences and different protective provisions, all cleanly documented in the Articles.

Dividend preference

Usually a nominal 0.0001% to 0.01% preferential dividend, present purely so the instrument qualifies as a preference share under Section 55 without creating a real cash obligation.

Clean conversion at IPO

SEBI ICDR requires all convertible instruments to convert before listing. CCPS converts automatically; optional instruments create a mess.

The Section 55 nuance on redeemable preference sharesSection 55(1) of the Companies Act, 2013 prohibits the issue of irredeemable preference shares, and Section 55(2) caps redeemable preference shares at a 20-year tenor. CCPS sidestep this because they convert rather than being redeemed. A term sheet that asks for a redeemable preference share with an assured return is asking for something that collides with both Section 55 and (for foreign investors) the NDI Rules' prohibition on assured returns. Flag it immediately. Verify at mca.gov.in and rbi.org.in.

Valuation: How the Number Is Actually Set

Early-stage valuation is not a calculation; it is a negotiation with a spreadsheet attached for decorum. The number is set by the amount being raised, the dilution the investor wants, comparable rounds in the same sector that quarter, and how many other investors are at the table. The formal methods below matter for two reasons: they give you the vocabulary to argue, and Indian law requires a formal valuation report for certain issuances regardless of what you and the investor agreed over coffee.

How the number is really determined

THE ACTUAL ALGORITHM USED BY AN EARLY-STAGE INVESTOR

  1. "How much do you need to hit the next milestone?"
         Founder: "Rs 6 crore for 18 months of runway"

  2. "I want 20% for a seed."
         -> Post-money = 6 / 0.20 = Rs 30 crore
         -> Pre-money  = Rs 24 crore

  3. Sanity-check against comparables:
         "Indian B2B SaaS seeds with Rs 60L-1Cr ARR priced at
          Rs 20-35 crore pre this quarter. Rs 24 crore is fine."

  4. Adjust for:
         + Repeat founder, strong team         up to +50%
         + Competitive process, 3 term sheets  up to +40%
         + Hot sector (AI, fintech infra)      up to +60%
         - Single founder                      -10 to -25%
         - Pre-revenue                         -30 to -50%
         - Down market                         -20 to -40%
         - Messy cap table / 6 angels at 2%    -10 to -20%

  5. The DCF, if one exists, is produced AFTER step 4 and reverse-
     engineered to support it. This is not cynicism; it is how
     early-stage pricing works everywhere.

THE ONLY TWO LEVERS YOU CONTROL:
  (a) The quality of the evidence you present (revenue, retention,
      growth rate, pipeline)
  (b) COMPETITIVE TENSION - a second interested fund moves the
      number more than any model ever will.

The gap between the number and reality

  • A headline valuation with a 2x participating liquidation preference is worth far less to founders than a lower valuation with 1x non-participating. Always compare offers on the exit waterfall, not the headline.
  • A high valuation sets the bar for the next round. A ₹120 crore post seed means the Series A must price above ₹120 crore or you are in a down round with anti-dilution triggering.
  • Paper valuation is not wealth. It becomes wealth only in a secondary sale, an acquisition, or an IPO — and preference stacks eat the first slice of all three.
  • A 409A-style valuation for ESOP strike price in India (the merchant banker FMV) will be materially lower than the preferred-share price, because ordinary shares lack preference rights. A 2–4x gap is normal and is good for employees.

Clause 3 — Anti-dilution protection

If you later issue shares below the price the investor paid, anti-dilution adjusts their conversion ratio so they end up with more equity shares, compensating them for the down round. The adjustment is paid for by the founders and the option pool.

VariantFormulaSeverityMarket in India
Broad-based weighted averageNCP = OCP × (CSO + CSP) ÷ (CSO + CSAP), where the "outstanding" base includes all equity, options and convertiblesModerate — shares the painStandard — ask for this
Narrow-based weighted averageSame formula, but "outstanding" counts only issued equity (excludes the option pool)Harsher — smaller denominator means a bigger adjustmentNegotiable
Full ratchetConversion price resets to the new, lower price, regardless of how few shares were issuedBrutal — a tiny down-priced issue can massively re-cut the cap tablePush back hard; sometimes accepted with a sunset (applies only for 12–18 months) or a cap
Pay-to-playAnti-dilution applies only if the investor participates pro-rata in the down round; otherwise their preferred converts to ordinaryFounder-friendly additionAsk for it — it aligns everyone
ANTI-DILUTION - WORKED

SETUP: Seed fund holds 29,575 CCPS bought at Rs 2,028.73 each
       (Rs 6 crore). Conversion ratio currently 1:1.
       Pre-down-round FD = 211,045 shares.

DOWN ROUND: company raises Rs 10 crore at Rs 40 crore pre-money
       New price per share = 40,00,00,000 / 211,045 = Rs 1,895.32
       New shares issued   = 10,00,00,000 / 1,895.32 = 52,762

       This is BELOW the seed price of Rs 2,028.73 -> anti-dilution
       triggers for the Seed Fund. (Series A bought at Rs 5,970.30,
       so they are massively triggered too - shown after.)

--- BROAD-BASED WEIGHTED AVERAGE ---
  NCP = OCP x  (CSO + CSP) / (CSO + CSAP)

  OCP  = Old conversion price                = Rs 2,028.73
  CSO  = Common shares outstanding (broad,
         incl. options + as-converted)       = 211,045
  CSP  = Shares that WOULD have been issued
         at the old price = 10,00,00,000 / 2,028.73 = 49,292
  CSAP = Shares ACTUALLY issued              = 52,762

  NCP = 2,028.73 x (211,045 + 49,292) / (211,045 + 52,762)
      = 2,028.73 x 260,337 / 263,807
      = 2,028.73 x 0.98685
      = Rs 2,002.06

  New conversion ratio for Seed Fund:
     Shares on conversion = (29,575 x 2,028.73) / 2,002.06
                          = 6,00,00,089 / 2,002.06
                          = 29,969 equity shares  (was 29,575)
     GAIN: 394 extra shares.  Modest.

--- FULL RATCHET (same facts) ---
  NCP = new price = Rs 1,895.32
     Shares on conversion = 6,00,00,089 / 1,895.32
                          = 31,657 equity shares
     GAIN: 2,082 extra shares.  5x the broad-based adjustment.

--- NOW DO IT FOR SERIES A (Rs 30 Cr at Rs 5,970.30) ---
  FULL RATCHET:
     Shares = 30,00,02,100 / 1,895.32 = 158,286 shares
              (was 50,249)
     The Series A fund's stake explodes from 23.81% to roughly
     158,286 / (211,045 - 50,249 + 158,286 + 52,762 + 394)
            = 158,286 / 372,238 = 42.5%

     FOUNDERS: 47.38% -> 100,000 / 372,238 = 26.9%
     A single down round with a full ratchet nearly HALVED the
     founders. With broad-based weighted average, the same down
     round would have cost them roughly 5 percentage points.

*** THIS IS WHY YOU FIGHT FULL RATCHET AT THE TERM SHEET. ***
Carve-outs you must insist onAnti-dilution must not trigger on: shares issued under an approved ESOP scheme; shares issued on conversion of existing convertibles; shares issued in a bona fide strategic partnership, acquisition or joint venture approved by the board; shares issued to banks, lessors or venture debt providers as warrants; bonus issues, splits and reclassifications; and any issue approved in writing by the holders of a majority of that class. Without carve-outs, granting a normal ESOP can technically trigger anti-dilution.

Clause 9 — The India-specific exit obligation

TYPICAL INDIAN EXIT CLAUSE (paraphrased)

"The Company and the Founders shall use best efforts to provide an
 exit to the Investor within 60 (sixty) months of the Closing Date,
 by way of (i) an initial public offering, (ii) a strategic sale or
 merger, (iii) a buy-back of the Investor's shares by the Company,
 or (iv) a sale to a third party. If no exit has been provided
 within the said period, the Investor shall be entitled to require
 the Company and/or the Founders to purchase the Investor's shares
 at Fair Market Value."

WHY IT EXISTS: a SEBI-registered AIF has a finite fund life and its
LPs expect distributions. The fund cannot hold your shares forever.

WHY IT IS DANGEROUS:
 - A PUT OPTION at an assured price against a NON-RESIDENT investor
   is NOT PERMITTED under FEMA. The NDI Rules permit optionality
   clauses only with a minimum one-year lock-in and an EXIT AT
   MARKET PRICE / FAIR VALUE - no assured return. Verify at
   rbi.org.in (Master Direction - Foreign Investment in India).
 - A BUYBACK by the company requires Sec. 68 compliance and free
   reserves which a loss-making startup does not have.
 - A personal obligation on FOUNDERS to buy the shares is an
   unlimited personal liability. RESIST THIS ABSOLUTELY.

WHAT TO NEGOTIATE:
 1. "best efforts" not "shall ensure"
 2. Obligation on the COMPANY, never personally on founders
 3. Exit at FAIR MARKET VALUE determined by an independent valuer,
    never at an assured IRR
 4. 7 years, not 5
 5. Explicit carve-out: "subject to applicable law, including the
    Companies Act 2013 and FEMA"
 6. Drag-along as the agreed fallback mechanism rather than a put

A term sheet red-flag checklist

  • Liquidation preference above 1x, or participating without a cap.
  • Full ratchet anti-dilution with no sunset and no carve-outs.
  • Option pool of 15%+ created entirely pre-money with no hiring plan.
  • Reserved matters requiring each investor's consent rather than a class majority.
  • Drag-along exercisable by investors alone with no price floor.
  • Personal founder obligation to buy back investor shares.
  • Founder vesting restarting fully at 0% with no credit for time served.
  • Uncapped founder indemnity, or indemnity with no basket and no survival limit.
  • Exclusivity longer than 90 days, or with no lapse mechanism.
  • Uncapped investor legal costs payable regardless of whether the deal closes.
  • "Due diligence satisfactory to the Investor in its sole and absolute discretion" as the only CP.
  • No pay-to-play, combined with aggressive anti-dilution.
  • Board quorum that cannot be met without the investor director, with no adjournment fallback.
  • An assured-return put option against a non-resident investor (a FEMA problem for you).

Executing the Round: Term Sheet to Money in the Bank

Closing a round in India is a sequence of statutory steps under Section 42 and Section 62 of the Companies Act, 2013, each with its own deadline. Missing one does not merely cause paperwork — Section 42(6) requires refund with 12% interest if you do not allot within 60 days, and the private placement provisions carry penalties that can reach the amount raised. This section is the runbook.

The Articles must mirror the SHAIndian courts have consistently held that a shareholders' agreement does not bind the company unless its terms are incorporated into the Articles of Association. A transfer restriction, pre-emption right or buyback mechanic that exists only in the SHA may be unenforceable against the company or a transferee. Every round must amend the AOA by special resolution and file MGT-14. Verify with counsel; this is settled but fact-specific.

The Section 42 private placement route, step by step

  1. Check authorised capitalCompute the post-round face value of all shares. If it exceeds authorised capital, pass an ordinary resolution to increase it, amend Clause V of the MOA, and file Form SH-7 within 30 days with the fee on the increase. Do this first — it is the most common delay.
  2. Obtain the valuation reportFrom a Registered Valuer (IBBI-registered) under Section 62(1)(c) and Rule 13. If any subscriber is a non-resident, additionally obtain a merchant banker or CA certificate of fair value under the NDI Rules. The report must be dated before the offer and is typically valid for a limited period — confirm with the valuer.
  3. Board meetingApprove: the private placement offer, the list of identified persons (Section 42 permits an offer to a maximum of 200 persons in a financial year, excluding QIBs and employees under ESOP), the PAS-4 offer letter, the SSA/SHA, the opening of a separate bank account, and the calling of an EGM. Issue notice under Section 173(3).
  4. EGM — special resolutionMembers approve the private placement under Section 42 and the further issue under Section 62(1)(c), and approve the amended Articles. File Form MGT-14 within 30 days of the resolution.
  5. Issue PAS-4The private placement offer letter in Form PAS-4, serially numbered and addressed specifically to each identified person, delivered within 30 days of recording their names. Maintain the record of offers in Form PAS-5 (check current Rule requirements — the PAS-5 requirement has been amended over time; confirm at mca.gov.in).
  6. Open a separate bank accountSection 42(6) requires application money to be received in a separate bank account of the company, and it cannot be utilised until allotment is made and the return of allotment is filed. Tell your bank this explicitly; many founders use their operating account and create a contravention.
  7. Receive subscription moneyOnly by cheque, demand draft or other banking channel — not in cash (Section 42(4)). From the subscriber's own bank account. For a foreign investor, through normal banking channels into a designated account, with the AD bank issuing an FIRC / Advance Remittance Certificate and a KYC report on the remitter.
  8. Board meeting — allotmentWithin 60 days of receipt of application money (Section 42(6)). Pass the allotment resolution, approve share certificates, and update the Register of Members. Miss the 60 days and you must refund within 15 days; after that, interest at 12% p.a. accrues from the 60th day.
  9. File PAS-3Return of allotment in Form PAS-3 with the list of allottees, within 15 days of allotment (verify the current timeline — it has been 15 days under the current Rules; earlier it was 30). Attach the board resolution, list of allottees, and the valuation report.
  10. Issue share certificatesWithin 2 months of allotment (Section 56(4)). Signed by two directors (or a director and the CS), with the common seal if the Articles require one, and stamped under the applicable State Stamp Act. Stamp duty on share certificates is a state subject — commonly in the range of 0.005%–0.1% of consideration; verify your state's rate.
  11. Update statutory registersRegister of Members (MGT-1), Register of Transfers, and the Register of Charges if any security was created.
  12. FEMA reportingIf any allottee is a non-resident: FC-GPR within 30 days of allotment on the RBI FIRMS portal. See the next section.
  13. Post-closing corporate actionsAppoint the investor nominee director (board resolution + Form DIR-12 within 30 days), adopt the amended Articles, update the MCA master data, and circulate a fresh cap table to all shareholders.

FEMA and Foreign Investment: The Filings That Fail Diligence

If a single rupee of your funding comes from outside India, you are in the Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 regime. The rules are administered by the RBI through the Foreign Exchange Management (Non-debt Instruments) Rules, 2019 and the Master Direction on Foreign Investment in India, and the filings are made on the RBI's FIRMS portal. Founders routinely skip these, and they are found in every subsequent diligence.

Verify everything in this section at rbi.org.inFEMA rules, reporting forms, timelines and the Late Submission Fee formula are amended frequently through A.P. (DIR Series) Circulars and Master Direction updates. Everything below is stated as at FY 2025-26 / early 2026. Before you file anything, read the current Master Direction — Foreign Investment in India and the current FIRMS user manual on rbi.org.in, and confirm with your AD Category-I bank. Your AD bank is the gatekeeper for every filing and its interpretation is what practically matters.

Press Note 3 (2020) — the land-border rule that catches founders outAn entity of a country which shares a land border with India (China, Bangladesh, Pakistan, Nepal, Myanmar, Bhutan, Afghanistan), or where the beneficial owner of an investment is situated in or is a citizen of such a country, can invest only under the government approval route — regardless of sector. In practice: a US fund with a Chinese LP, a Singapore SPV with a Chinese ultimate beneficial owner, or an angel resident in Hong Kong can all trip this. Ask every foreign investor for a beneficial-ownership declaration before you sign. Approval, where needed, takes months. Verify the current text of Press Note 3 (2020) at dpiit.gov.in.

Tax Matters for Startups and Investors

Four tax provisions decide whether a funded Indian startup keeps its money: the Section 80-IAC holiday, the (now abolished) angel tax, the loss carry-forward rules in Section 79, and the capital gains regime that governs every investor and every secondary sale. All four are in flux because the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaces the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026.

Verify every provision in this section at incometax.gov.inSection references follow the Income-tax Act, 1961. The Income-tax Act, 2025 takes effect from 1 April 2026 and introduces a unified "tax year" in place of "previous year"/"assessment year"; section numbering changes. Rates, surcharge, cess, holding periods and indexation rules change with each Finance Act. Nothing here is advice; confirm with your CA before filing or structuring.

Angel tax — abolished, but read this

Section 56(2)(viib) — abolished from AY 2025-26; verify at incometax.gov.inThe provision taxed, in the company's hands, the excess of share issue consideration over fair market value when an unlisted company issued shares at a premium. The Finance Act 2023 extended it to non-resident investors, which caused significant disruption to Indian fundraising; the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 abolished it entirely with effect from AY 2025-26. What this does not change: (a) a Registered Valuer report is still required under Section 62(1)(c) of the Companies Act, 2013 for a preferential allotment; (b) a merchant banker or CA fair-value certificate is still required under the NDI Rules for any non-resident subscription; (c) Section 56(2)(x), which taxes a recipient who receives property (including shares) for inadequate consideration, is unaffected and routinely applies to share transfers at below FMV — including founder-to-founder transfers and secondary sales; (d) assessments for earlier years remain open and old angel-tax notices are still being litigated.

Carry-forward of losses — Section 79 and the startup relaxation

SECTION 79 - CHANGE IN SHAREHOLDING KILLS LOSSES (normally)

GENERAL RULE for a company in which the public are not substantially
interested: a brought-forward loss can be set off in a year only if
shares carrying NOT LESS THAN 51% OF THE VOTING POWER on the last day
of the year in which the loss was incurred were BENEFICIALLY HELD by
the same persons on the last day of the year of set-off.

WHY THIS BITES A STARTUP:
   Founders own 100% in year 1 and incur Rs 3 crore of losses.
   After Seed + Series A, founders own 47%.
   Common shareholding has fallen below 51% -> the Rs 3 crore of
   brought-forward business loss would be LOST.

*** THE STARTUP RELAXATION ***
   For an ELIGIBLE START-UP as referred to in Sec. 80-IAC, the loss
   is allowed to be carried forward and set off if EITHER
     (a) the 51% continuity test is satisfied, OR
     (b) ALL the shareholders who held shares carrying voting power
         on the last day of the year in which the loss was incurred
         CONTINUE TO HOLD THOSE SHARES on the last day of the year
         of set-off,
   and the loss was incurred during the first N years from
   incorporation (the period has been amended - VERIFY the current
   period, historically 7 years, extended by later Finance Acts).

WHAT CONDITION (b) MEANS IN PRACTICE:
   Dilution is FINE. Founders going from 100% to 25% does not break
   it, as long as every original shareholder still holds the shares
   they held.
   What BREAKS it: a founder EXITING and selling their shares; a
   buyback of a departing founder's shares; an angel selling in a
   secondary.

   *** THIS IS A REASON TO STRUCTURE A FOUNDER DEPARTURE CAREFULLY.
       Extinguishing a departing founder's shares can destroy crores
       of carried-forward losses. Model the tax cost before you
       agree the departure mechanics. ***

ALSO NOTE:
   - UNABSORBED DEPRECIATION (Sec. 32(2)) is NOT subject to Sec. 79
     and carries forward indefinitely regardless of shareholding.
   - Business losses carry forward for 8 assessment years (verify
     under the Income-tax Act 2025).
   - Losses can only be set off if the return was filed within the
     Sec. 139(1) due date. FILE ON TIME.

ESOPs End to End

An ESOP is the only mechanism by which a cash-poor Indian startup can pay senior people what they are worth. It is also the single most frequently botched piece of startup governance in India: pools created without a special resolution, grants with no scheme document, strike prices set by guesswork, exercise windows that make options worthless on resignation, and a tax treatment that can leave an employee paying tax on money they have never received.

The legal framework

ProvisionRequirement
Section 62(1)(b), Companies Act 2013A company may issue further shares to employees under a scheme of employees' stock option, subject to a special resolution of shareholders and prescribed conditions
Rule 12, Companies (Share Capital and Debentures) Rules, 2014Prescribes what the scheme must contain and who is an eligible "employee". Private companies have relaxations — notably, a private company may pass an ordinary resolution if its Articles so permit (verify the current exemption notification), and may grant to promoters and to directors holding more than 10%, which a public company cannot
Who is an "employee"A permanent employee of the company working in or outside India; a director (whole-time or not, but excluding an independent director); an employee or director of a subsidiary, holding or associate company. Not an independent director; not (for public companies) a promoter or a director holding more than 10%

Free transferabilityOptions are not transferable and cannot be pledged, hypothecated, mortgaged or otherwise encumbered. Only the employee may exercise
Consultants and advisorsCannot be granted ESOPs under Section 62(1)(b) because they are not employees. Use sweat equity shares under Section 54 (with its own conditions and valuation requirements), a warrant, or simply issue them shares in a priced allotment
An LLP cannot issue ESOPsThe LLP Act, 2008 has no concept of share capital, options or stock. There is no statutory ESOP mechanism for an LLP. Workarounds used in practice: a profit-sharing arrangement written into the LLP Agreement (taxable as business income, not capital gains, and it gives no ownership upside); admitting the person as a partner with a capital contribution (which creates partner liability and tax complexity); or a phantom equity / shadow unit plan — a contractual cash bonus linked to a valuation event, taxed fully as salary. If equity incentives matter to your hiring plan, do not choose an LLP, or plan to convert to a private limited company under Section 366 of the Companies Act, 2013 before you need to grant.

The scheme document — what it must contain

  • Name of the scheme and effective date.
  • Total number of options reserved under the pool.
  • Definition of "Employee" and the eligibility criteria.
  • Appraisal process for determining eligibility and quantum.
  • Maximum options to be granted per employee and in aggregate.
  • Requirements of vesting and the vesting period, with the statutory minimum of one year.
  • Exercise price or the pricing formula, and whether it is at FMV, at a discount, or at face value.
  • Exercise period and the process of exercise.
  • The lock-in period, if any, on shares issued on exercise.
  • Conditions under which options vest in the employee and may lapse.
  • Treatment on resignation, termination for cause, termination without cause, retirement, permanent disability and death.
  • The right of the employee to exercise all vested options at one time or in instalments.
  • Treatment on a change of control, merger, or sale of the company (acceleration provisions).
  • Whether the company will provide financial assistance for exercise (and the restrictions in Section 67 on a company funding the purchase of its own shares).
  • Administration: the ESOP Committee or the Board, its powers, and the authority to amend the scheme.
  • Statement that options confer no dividend, voting or shareholder rights until exercise.
  • Governing law and dispute resolution.

The Section 192(1C) deferral for eligible start-ups

Section 192(1C) read with Section 140A(1) proviso and Section 191 — verify at incometax.gov.inFor an eligible start-up referred to in Section 80-IAC, the employer may defer deduction or payment of tax on the ESOP perquisite to the earliest of: (a) 48 months from the end of the assessment year in which the shares were allotted; (b) the date the employee sells the shares; or (c) the date the employee ceases to be an employee of that company. The employee gets the same deferral for their own self-assessment tax on that perquisite.

Why it helps less than it sounds: it requires the company to hold the IMB certificate under Section 80-IAC, which only a small minority of DPIIT-recognised startups obtain. And trigger (c) means that if the employee leaves — which is exactly when they must exercise under a short window — the deferral collapses immediately. Confirm your company's eligibility with your CA before promising this to employees.

Three rules for investor updates(1) Send it on the same date every month, even in a bad month — especially in a bad month. Silence is what investors read as failure. (2) Lead with the bad news and your plan; it buys credibility you will need later. (3) Always make a specific ask. "Let me know if you can help" produces nothing. Delivering bad news

  1. Phone first, email secondCall the lead investor before the update goes out. Never let a board member learn something material from a group email.
  2. State the fact plainly in the first sentence"We will miss the quarter by 22%." Not three paragraphs of context first.
  3. Own the causeAttribute it to a decision you made, not to the market. Investors forgive mistakes; they do not forgive the absence of a learning.
  4. Bring the plan and the decision you have already takenNot three options for the board to choose between. You run the company.
  5. State what you needMore time, a bridge, a specific introduction, or permission under a reserved matter.
  6. Re-baseline explicitlyIssue a revised forecast. Do not let the original plan sit there as an unmet standard for the next three quarters.

The Flip and the Reverse-Flip

A "flip" moves the holding company of an Indian startup to Delaware or Singapore, making the Indian company a wholly owned subsidiary. For a decade it was the default advice for anyone raising from US investors. It is now a much closer call, and a visible cohort of Indian companies has paid large tax bills to reverse the decision ahead of an Indian listing.

The flip: shareholders exchange Indian shares for shares in a new overseas holding company. Taxable in India for resident shareholders.

Why startups flip

Investor familiarity

US funds are set up to invest in Delaware C-corps on standard NVCA documents. A Delaware entity removes FEMA, pricing guidelines, FC-GPR, Section 42 and NDI Rules from their process entirely.

Standard instruments

SAFEs work in Delaware. Convertible notes work without a ₹25 lakh floor or DPIIT recognition. Option grants are a board resolution, not a special resolution plus MGT-14.

Customer perception

Some US enterprise buyers and some procurement processes prefer contracting with a US entity. For a SaaS company selling to US mid-market and enterprise, this is a real commercial argument.

Exit market

US acquirers buy US companies more readily. Nasdaq listing requires a US (or foreign private issuer) structure.

Accelerator requirements

Y Combinator and several US accelerators historically required or strongly encouraged a Delaware parent as a condition of the standard deal.

ESOPs for a global team

A single Delaware option plan covering employees in India, the US and Europe is far simpler than parallel plans under three legal regimes.

The Fundraising Process and Materials

Fundraising is a sales process with a long cycle, a small addressable market and a binary outcome per account. Run it like one: a defined list, a pipeline, parallel conversations compressed into a window, a tight narrative, and a data room that is ready before the first meeting. Founders who treat it as a series of coffee chats take nine months and raise on worse terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

I have a working product and two paying customers. Should I raise now or wait?
Wait, in almost every case. Two customers gives you no pricing power in the negotiation and forces you to raise on narrative rather than evidence. The step-up from "two customers" to "₹60–80 lakh of ARR with three months of retention data" is usually 6–9 months of work and typically 2–3x the valuation, with far better terms. In the meantime: get DPIIT recognition (free), build the data room, fix your cap table and vesting, and run a mock diligence. The exception is a genuine land-grab market where a competitor with ₹50 crore will take the category before you can bootstrap into it — and you should be able to name that competitor and show their funding announcement.
An angel wants to give me ₹10 lakh as a convertible note. Is that legal?
Almost certainly not, as a convertible note. The exemption from the deposit rules under the Companies (Acceptance of Deposits) Rules, 2014 requires the company to be a DPIIT-recognised startup and the amount to be at least ₹25 lakh (₹2,500,000) in a single tranche from a single person. A ₹10 lakh convertible note from a non-director, non-member individual is a deposit under Section 73 of the Companies Act, 2013, with penalties on the company and on every officer in default. Your legal options for ₹10 lakh are: (a) a priced issue of equity or CCPS under Section 42, (b) a loan from that person after appointing them a director, with the prescribed declaration that the funds are not borrowed, or (c) ask them to come in at ₹25 lakh alongside another angel in a note. Confirm the current Rules and exemptions at mca.gov.in before doing any of these.
The term sheet says 2x participating liquidation preference but the valuation is 40% higher than the other offer. Which should I take?
Build the waterfall at three exit values before answering. Using the worked example in Section 9: at a ₹120 crore exit, participation transferred ₹17 crore from founders and employees to the fund — and a 2x preference transferred ₹35 crore. A 40% higher headline valuation on a ₹6 crore round saves you roughly 5–6 percentage points of dilution, which at a ₹120 crore exit is about ₹7 crore. The lower valuation at 1x non-participating wins decisively at every exit under roughly ₹400 crore, and most Indian exits are under ₹400 crore. Model your actual cap table at ₹100 crore, ₹300 crore and ₹600 crore and take the offer with the better founder-and-employee number at the exit you actually believe in.
My co-founder wants to leave after 14 months. We never signed anything about vesting. What now?
Legally, their shares are theirs. Without a vesting agreement reflected in the Articles, there is no repurchase right. Your options, in order: (1) Negotiate. Offer a voluntary partial surrender or transfer at cost in exchange for a clean exit, a mutual release, a signed IP assignment covering everything they built, and a reference. Many people accept this because they do not want to be a passive shareholder in a company that will later ask them to sign documents. (2) Offer a structured buyout over 24 months funded from revenue, at a negotiated price. (3) Accept a reduced stake — negotiate them down from 40% to 10–15% rather than to zero. (4) Live with it and disclose it to investors with a clear narrative. What you must get regardless of the equity outcome: a signed IP assignment deed and a confirmation that they hold no claim to further equity or compensation. Separately, check Section 79 — extinguishing their shares can destroy your carried-forward losses. Get a corporate lawyer involved before the first conversation, not after.
How much should I raise?
Enough to reach the next set of milestones plus a 6-month buffer, and no more. Work backwards: define the metrics that make the next round fundable (for an Indian seed-to-Series-A, typically ₹8–15 crore ARR with 2.5–3x year-on-year growth), build a driver-based model of what that costs, add 30% for things going slower than planned, and add six months because the next raise will take 5–7 months. If the answer is ₹6 crore for 18 months of runway, raise ₹6 crore. Raising ₹12 crore "because it was available" doubles your dilution now and sets a valuation you must grow into, and the extra money will be spent — companies spend what they have. The one argument for raising more: if your business has a genuine step-function cost (a regulatory licence, a hardware tooling run, a data acquisition) that cannot be staged.
We took $150,000 from a US angel into our Indian company eight months ago and never filed anything with RBI. How bad is it?
It is a FEMA reporting contravention, and it is fixable, but start now. First establish which of two situations you are in. If the money was received against a proper equity instrument (equity shares or CCPS), allotted within 60 days, at or above fair value, and the only failure is the late FC-GPR, then you file FC-GPR late with a Late Submission Fee through your AD bank and the matter closes — cost is the LSF plus professional fees, timeline 4–10 weeks. If the money was received against a SAFE, an unallotted "advance", an optionally convertible instrument, or was allotted after 60 days or below fair value, then you have a substantive contravention requiring a compounding application to the RBI under Section 15 of FEMA, 1999 — cost is the compounding amount plus ₹1.5–5 lakh in professional fees, timeline 3–6 months. Either way: engage a CS and a FEMA-experienced CA this week, because no institutional investor will complete a round while this is open, and the LSF/compounding amount increases with delay. Verify the current LSF and compounding framework at rbi.org.in.
Is angel tax really gone? Do I still need a valuation report?
Section 56(2)(viib) was abolished for all classes of investors with effect from AY 2025-26 by the Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024 — verify at incometax.gov.in. You still need valuations, for three independent reasons. (1) A preferential allotment by an unlisted company requires a Registered Valuer report under Section 62(1)(c) of the Companies Act, 2013 and Rule 13 — that is company law, not tax law, and it is unchanged. (2) Any issue to a non-resident requires a fair-value certificate from a merchant banker or CA under the FEMA NDI Rules, 2019 — also unchanged, and your FC-GPR will be rejected without it. (3) Section 56(2)(x) taxes the recipient of shares acquired below fair market value, and it still applies to transfers, including secondaries and founder-to-founder transfers. Also note that assessments and appeals for earlier years under the old provision are still live. Budget ₹40,000–₹2 lakh for valuation reports in any round.
A senior candidate is asking what their ESOPs are actually worth. What do I tell them?
Tell them the truth with numbers. Give them: the number of options, the strike price, the current fair market value per share as certified by a merchant banker, the current fully diluted share count, the last round's post-money valuation and date, the vesting schedule, the post-termination exercise window, and whether the company has ever run a liquidity programme. Then show the arithmetic on two scenarios — the company is acquired at 3x the last round, and at 1x — and explain that in the second case a liquidation preference stack may absorb most or all of the proceeds before ordinary shares. Finally, explain the tax: perquisite at exercise taxed as salary, capital gains at sale, and the fact that exercise requires cash out of pocket with no cash in. A candidate who hears this and still joins will not resent you in four years. A candidate who is told "these could be worth ₹2 crore" will.
Should I flip to Delaware? My US investors are asking.
If you are pre-revenue and pre-Series-A, the tax cost of a flip is near zero and it is a commercial decision — flip if your customers, your capital and your eventual acquirers are all in the US. If you have already raised a priced round, the flip is a taxable transfer for every resident shareholder at the fair market value of the shares they receive, with no cash to pay the tax. In the worked example in Section 18, a founder holding 28% of a ₹126 crore company faced roughly ₹5 crore of cash tax on a share swap that put no money in her pocket. On top of that you acquire ODI compliance under the FEM (Overseas Investment) Rules 2022, transfer pricing with Form 3CEB, two audits, and the loss of DPIIT benefits. Push back with a specific question: "Which of our terms would change if we stayed Indian, and what is the rupee value of that change?" Many US funds now invest directly into Indian companies on CCPS without difficulty. Decide with a cross-border tax adviser on your facts.
The investor wants my co-founder and me to personally guarantee that they will get an exit in five years. Is that normal?
A "best efforts" exit obligation on the company is standard in Indian SHAs and you should expect it. A personal obligation on founders to buy back the investor's shares is not something you should accept. It converts limited liability into unlimited personal liability for an amount that could be many times your net worth, and it is exactly the risk a company structure exists to avoid. It is also legally fragile where the investor is a non-resident: an assured-return put option against a non-resident is not permitted under the FEMA NDI Rules, which allow optionality only with a minimum one-year lock-in and exit at fair market value. Counter with: obligation on the company only, "best efforts", seven years not five, exit at independently determined fair value, drag-along as the agreed fallback mechanism, and an express carve-out that the obligation is "subject to applicable law including the Companies Act, 2013 and FEMA".
What does a seed round actually cost us in fees?
For a ₹5–10 crore Indian seed round, budget ₹12–25 lakh all-in: your law firm ₹3–8 lakh, the investor's law firm ₹3–7 lakh (which you pay, so cap it in the term sheet), financial diligence ₹2–5 lakh, a Registered Valuer report ₹40,000–₹1.5 lakh, a merchant banker fair-value certificate ₹75,000–₹2 lakh if any investor is non-resident, CS fees ₹50,000–₹1.5 lakh for the resolutions, MGT-14, PAS-3, SH-7, DIR-12 and FC-GPR, plus MCA fees and state stamp duty on the SH-7, the share certificates and the agreements. Three ways to reduce it: cap the investor's legal costs and make them payable only on completion; use a combined SSHA rather than separate SSA and SHA; and agree a fixed fee with your own counsel upfront rather than hourly billing. Fee ranges are market observations as at early 2026, not quotations.
Can I give equity to my advisors and freelance developers through the ESOP pool?
For advisors who are not employees: no, not through Section 62(1)(b), which is limited to permanent employees and directors (excluding independent directors). Your options are sweat equity shares under Section 54 of the Companies Act, 2013 (which has its own conditions, a valuation requirement, and a special resolution), a warrant, a straightforward priced allotment of shares under Section 42, or a phantom equity contract paying cash on a liquidity event. For freelance developers: the same restriction applies, and there is a much more urgent issue — under the Copyright Act, 1957, in the absence of a written assignment an independent contractor generally retains copyright in what they create. Before you worry about their equity, get a signed IP assignment deed from every contractor who has touched your codebase, including ones you no longer work with. Missing contractor IP assignments is one of the most common deal-stopping findings in Indian technical diligence.
We are three months from running out of cash and the round is not closing. What are the options?
In order of preference. (1) Insider bridge — go to your existing investors today, with a specific amount, a specific runway extension, and a specific plan. They have more to lose than anyone and they move fastest. A convertible note at a 20% discount to the next round takes 2–3 weeks. (2) Cut burn immediately and visibly — a credible 35–45% cost reduction buys months and materially improves the bridge conversation. Do it once, deeply, not in three rounds. (3) Revenue acceleration — offer annual prepaid contracts at a 15–20% discount to your existing customers; this is the fastest non-dilutive cash in any software business. (4) Venture debt — only realistic if you closed an equity round recently. (5) Founder loan — a director's loan is exempt from the deposit rules with the prescribed declaration, and is legitimate, but think hard before putting personal money into a company that could not raise. (6) Accept a structured or down round with anti-dilution modelled first. Whatever you choose, tell your investors and your senior team before, not after. Founders who go quiet in month 10 are the ones nobody bridges in month 12.
How do I know if a fund is actually going to invest or is just gathering market intelligence?
Watch for specific behaviours rather than enthusiasm. Real interest looks like: a partner (not only an associate) in the second meeting; a request for a detailed data pack with a named analyst attached; customer reference calls being scheduled; a question about your round timing and who else is in it; an explicit statement of what their process is and when their investment committee meets. Tyre-kicking looks like: repeated meetings with the same associate; detailed questions about your market and competitors with none about your numbers; no mention of process or timing; and a reluctance to say what would make them lead. The direct question is fair and professional: "What is your process from here, what would you need to see to lead, and when does your IC meet?" A fund genuinely interested will answer precisely. One that is not will be vague, and that answer is your answer.
Our cap table has eleven angels at 0.5–2% each. Is that a problem?
Yes, and it is worth fixing before your next round. Problems it creates: every future round needs consents and signatures from eleven people, some of whom will be unreachable; pre-emptive rights and ROFRs must be waived by each; any one of them can slow a closing by weeks; and institutional investors read a long angel list as a governance risk. Fixes, in order of practicality: (1) ensure the SHA gives a power of attorney or drag mechanism so that a majority can sign on behalf of all small shareholders in a future round — this is the single most valuable clause to add; (2) consolidate future small cheques through a syndicate SPV (LetsVenture, AngelList India) so they appear as one line; (3) offer a secondary at the next round so some angels can exit — but check Section 79 first, because angels exiting can break your loss carry-forward; (4) add a provision that information rights and consent rights fall away below a holding threshold. Do (1) and (4) at the next round regardless.

===== SECTION B: ROUND INPUTS =====
B1  Investment amount                                 [input]
B2  Pre-money valuation                               [input]
B3  Post-money  = B1 + B2
B4  Investor %  = B1 / B3
B5  Target ESOP pool % of POST-money                  [input]
B6  Pool created PRE-money? (1 = yes, 0 = no)         [input]
B7  Convertible notes outstanding - amount            [input]
B8  Note discount %                                   [input]
B9  Note valuation cap                                [input]

===== SECTION C: NOTE CONVERSION (if B7 > 0) =====
C1  Headline price/share   = B2 / A1
C2  Discount price         = C1 x (1 - B8)
C3  Cap price              = B9 / A1
C4  Conversion price       = MIN(C2, C3)
C5  Note shares            = ROUND(B7 / C4, 0)
C6  CHECK: C5 x C4 should approximate B7
C7  FD after note conversion = A1 + C5

===== SECTION D: POOL AND NEW SHARES =====
IF B6 = 1 (pool pre-money, founders bear it):
D1  Fixed non-pool shares  = C7 - A6
D2  Total post-money FD    = D1 / (1 - B4 - B5)
D3  Investor shares        = D2 x B4
D4  Total pool after       = D2 x B5
D5  Pool top-up            = D4 - A5 - A6     (can be negative)

IF B6 = 0 (pool post-money, everyone bears it):
D6  Investor shares        = C7 x (B4 / (1 - B4))
D7  FD after investment    = C7 + D6
D8  Total post-money FD    = D7 / (1 - B5)
D9  Total pool after       = D8 x B5

===== SECTION E: PRICE =====
E1  Price per new share    = B3 / D2        (or B3 / D8)
E2  CHECK: D3 x E1 must equal B1 (within rounding)
E3  Face value per share                              [input]
E4  Securities premium     = E1 - E3
E5  Share capital credit   = D3 x E3
E6  Securities premium cr. = D3 x E4
E7  Authorised capital required = D2 x E3
E8  FLAG: if E7 > current authorised capital -> FILE SH-7
