Why Asset Allocation Dominates Outcomes

Almost everything that gets written about investing is about selection — which stock, which fund, which entry price. Almost everything that actually determines your outcome is about allocation — how much of your money sits in growth assets versus stable assets, for how long, and whether you stayed invested through the two or three ugly stretches that any multi-decade plan contains. Selection is the part that is interesting to talk about. Allocation is the part that is worth money.

The "93% of returns" claim, honestly

You will see a statistic quoted endlessly: "asset allocation explains 93.6% of returns." It comes from Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986), a study of 91 large American pension funds, and it is almost always misquoted. What the study actually measured was the proportion of the variance of quarterly returns over time, within a single fund, that was explained by its policy allocation. That is a different question from "what fraction of the difference between my portfolio and yours comes from allocation," and a very different question from "what fraction of my total return level comes from allocation."

The questionRoughly what the research saysWhat it means for you
What share of the wobble over time in one diversified portfolio's returns is explained by its policy mix?About 90% for a typical diversified institutional fundMostly a statement that markets move together and your portfolio moves with them. True but not very actionable.
What share of the difference between two investors' returns is explained by their policy mixes?Roughly 40–50% in follow-up work (Ibbotson & Kaplan, 2000)Allocation matters a great deal, but selection, costs and timing are not nothing.
What share of the level of return comes from the policy mix?Close to 100% on average, because selection is roughly a zero-sum game before costs and negative afterThis is the version that actually matters. The market return is what the allocation delivers; everything else is a side bet.
What share of a real Indian retail investor's lifetime outcome is explained by allocation?Not measured well, but savings rate and persistence plausibly dominate bothHow much you save and whether you keep going beats both allocation and selection in the first decade.
The honest hierarchyIn descending order of how much they change a 25-year outcome for a salaried Indian engineer: (1) savings rate; (2) not interrupting the plan during drawdowns; (3) equity-versus-everything-else allocation; (4) cost; (5) tax efficiency; (6) allocation within equity (large versus mid versus small, domestic versus international); (7) security or fund selection. Most investor attention goes to (7). Most of the money is in (1) to (4).

What a portfolio actually is

A portfolio is not a list of holdings. It is a set of decisions, most of which you make once and then defend for twenty years. Five decisions do almost all the work:

  1. How much goes into growth assetsThe single largest determinant of both your expected return and your worst drawdown. Everything else is detail by comparison.
  2. How long you can leave it thereHorizon converts volatility from a risk into a nuisance. A 40% equity fall is a catastrophe at an 18-month horizon and an irrelevance at a 20-year one, provided you do not sell.
  3. What you will do when it fallsDecided in advance and written down, or decided in the moment and badly. There is no third option.
  4. How and when you restore the mixRebalancing is the mechanical expression of "buy low, sell high" and the only part of the process that forces you to act against your instincts.
  5. What you will not doNo leverage, no derivatives on the core, no reacting to forecasts, no single position above X%. Constraints are the cheapest risk control available.

The Investment Policy Statement

An Investment Policy Statement (IPS) is a one-page document you write for yourself, in calm conditions, that says what you are trying to achieve, what you will hold, what you will never do, and what you will do when markets fall. Institutions are required to have one. Retail investors almost never do, which is precisely why retail investors behave worse than institutions during drawdowns. It takes ninety minutes to write and it is the highest-return ninety minutes in this guide.

The sections people skip, and regret

Prohibitions

Writing "no derivatives, no leverage, no single stock above 5%" costs nothing on a calm Tuesday. It is worth a great deal on the day a colleague describes a trade that tripled. The function of the list is to make the decision already made.

The drawdown protocol

The only section that will be tested. Specify behaviour at numeric thresholds, not vague intentions. "I will stay the course" is not a protocol. "At −30% I deploy 25% of idle cash and increase the SIP" is.

Required return

Most investors never compute it. If your goals need 9% and your allocation can plausibly deliver 9.5%, you are done — and you have just discovered that you do not need small-cap tilts or derivatives. If they need 16%, no allocation fixes that; the goal or the savings rate must move.

The amendment rule

Without it, the IPS becomes a document you rewrite each time it inconveniences you, which is the same as not having one. A 30-day cooling period and a written justification that may not cite recent returns kills almost every bad amendment.

Risk Capacity, Risk Tolerance and Required Return

Three different quantities get collapsed into the word "risk," and the collapse causes real damage. Capacity is arithmetic: how much you can lose before your life changes. Tolerance is psychology: how much you can lose before your behaviour changes. Required return is a constraint derived from your goals. The correct equity allocation is bounded by the minimum of capacity and tolerance, and informed — not determined — by required return.

The required-return trap

The dangerous case is when required return exceeds what any sane allocation delivers. An investor who needs 17% because they started at 42 with a ₹3 crore (30,000,000 rupee) target and a small corpus has three honest options and one dishonest one.

OptionHonest?Effect
Raise the savings rateYesDirectly lowers the required return. Usually the largest available lever.
Extend the horizon (retire later, phase retirement)YesThree extra years can cut required return by 2–3 percentage points.
Lower the target (smaller house, lower withdrawal, part-time income)YesUncomfortable but arithmetic.
Raise equity to 100% with a small-cap tilt, add leverage, sell options for "income"NoConverts a shortfall risk into a ruin risk. The distribution of outcomes widens in both directions; the left tail is what gets you. This is how people arrive at F&O at 45. See the derivatives guide for what the SEBI loss studies say happens next.
Needing a return does not make it availableMarkets are indifferent to your requirements. The expected return of an asset class is set by prices, cash flows and risk premia, not by what you need. An investor who "needs" 17% and takes the risk required to have a chance at 17% has simply accepted a much wider distribution of outcomes — including the one where the corpus is 60% smaller at exactly the wrong moment. Risk is not a dial that converts into return on demand.

The Asset Classes and the Job Each One Does

Think of a portfolio as a team where each member has one job. Growth assets do the compounding and supply almost all of the long-run return. Stability assets protect the near-term goals and provide the dry powder for rebalancing. Inflation-hedge assets cover the scenario where paper currency disappoints. Liquidity assets exist so that nothing else ever has to be sold at a bad price. Confusion arises when an asset is hired for one job and judged on another — blaming your debt sleeve for underperforming equity is like blaming the goalkeeper for not scoring.

Why each job needs a different asset

Growth cannot do stability's job

Equity has produced, in India and everywhere else, multi-year stretches of negative returns. 2008 took the Indian market roughly 60% down and several years to recover in real terms. If your house down-payment is in equity and the date arrives during such a stretch, the asset has not failed — you hired it for the wrong job.

Stability cannot do growth's job

A 7% nominal FD against 5–6% inflation and slab-rate tax on the interest is, for a 30% bracket taxpayer, approximately a zero or negative real return. Thirty years of that does not fund retirement. Safety in nominal terms is a slow loss in real terms.

Gold cannot be judged annually

Gold produces no cash flow, so it has no intrinsic rate of return to fall back on. It has had decade-long flat periods. Its job is to be uncorrelated when it matters — currency stress, geopolitical shock, severe inflation. If you judge it on three-year returns you will sell it immediately before it does its job.

Liquidity cannot be large

An oversized cash pile is the most common hidden drag in Indian portfolios. Twelve months of expenses is prudent. Four years of expenses "because the market looks expensive" is a 7-percentage-point annual opportunity cost dressed up as caution.

The roles drawn out

Hire each asset for one job and judge it only on that job. Most portfolio disappointment is a job-description error, not a selection error.

Correlation and What Real Diversification Means

Holding ten equity funds is not diversification. It is one bet, purchased ten times, with ten expense ratios and ten sets of paperwork. Diversification only does anything when the things you own are driven by different underlying forces, which shows up statistically as low or negative correlation. Most Indian retail portfolios are far less diversified than their holding count suggests, because the holdings all answer to the same question: what is the Indian equity market doing today?

Correlation in one paragraph

Correlation measures how two return series move together, on a scale from −1 (perfect opposites) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (identical movement). A portfolio's volatility is not the average of its components' volatilities; it is reduced by every pair with correlation below 1. The mathematics is unforgiving: adding a second asset with a 0.95 correlation to the first reduces portfolio volatility by almost nothing. Adding one with a 0.2 correlation reduces it materially even if that second asset is individually more volatile.

The overlap problem, concretely

Two actively managed Indian equity funds in adjacent categories frequently hold many of the same large companies. If a large-cap fund and a flexi-cap fund each hold 30 names and 18 are common, and those 18 account for 55% of each fund's weight, then owning both gives you a modest reduction in manager risk and essentially no reduction in market risk. You can check this: most fund research sites publish portfolio overlap percentages between any two schemes, and fund factsheets publish full holdings monthly (AMFI mandates monthly portfolio disclosure — see amfiindia.com; verify current disclosure norms).

  • Count your distinct bets, not your holdings. Most Indian retail portfolios have 1–2 distinct bets and 8–14 holdings.
  • Check pairwise overlap between any two equity funds before adding the second one.
  • Any new holding must answer: what does this do that nothing I already own does? If the answer is "it is a good fund," that is a selection argument, not a diversification argument.
  • Count employer stock, employer sector exposure and employer-dependent income as part of the equity concentration. A software engineer at a listed IT company with RSUs and an IT-heavy index already has substantial single-sector risk before buying anything.

Correlations are not stable, and they break when you need them

Correlation rises in crisesThe historically reliable observation is that in severe sell-offs, correlations between risk assets converge toward 1. Indian mid caps, Indian large caps, international equity, credit-risk debt funds and REITs can all fall together in a liquidity crisis, because the marginal seller is selling everything to raise cash. The things that held up in March 2020 were sovereign bonds, gold (after an initial fall) and cash — not "diversified equity." Build your drawdown expectations on crisis correlations, not on average-period correlations. This is the single most common way a backtested allocation disappoints in practice.

Strategic Allocation Versus Tactical Allocation

Strategic allocation is the long-run policy mix in your IPS, chosen for your horizon and capacity, changed only when your life changes. Tactical allocation is deviating from it because you have a view on the near-term market. The evidence on retail tactical allocation is bleak, and understanding why it fails is more useful than being told not to do it.

Why tactical allocation usually fails

  1. You need two correct decisions, not oneExiting requires being right about when to leave and when to return. If each has a 55% hit rate — generous — the joint probability is 30%. The move that is always remembered is the exit; the one that destroys the return is the delayed re-entry.
  2. The best days cluster inside the worst periodsThe largest single-day gains in equity markets overwhelmingly occur within weeks of the largest declines, because that is where volatility lives. Being out "until things are clearer" structurally removes the recovery, which is the part you cannot afford to miss.
  3. Costs and taxes are certain; the edge is notEvery switch in a taxable account crystallises capital gains. A 12.5% LTCG payment on a long-held equity position is a permanent 12.5% reduction of the gain that would otherwise have kept compounding. You must beat that, plus costs, plus the drag of being wrong sometimes.
  4. Valuation signals work over a decade, not a quarterMarkets can stay expensive for years. A valuation-based exit in 2016 or 2020 would have been "correct" on the metric and ruinous on the outcome. Signals with multi-year horizons cannot be used for 3-month decisions.
  5. Each successful trade increases position size and frequencyThe behaviourally dangerous outcome is early success, which raises confidence and stake just as the regime changes.
  6. Nobody keeps scoreRetail tactical allocators almost never maintain a record of the counterfactual — what the untouched policy portfolio would have returned. Without that number, the exercise is unfalsifiable and continues indefinitely.

If you must be tactical, bound itSome investors cannot resist. The damage-limiting structure is a hard-capped tactical sleeve: no more than 10% of the portfolio, funded once, never topped up from the core, with every trade logged including the counterfactual. Measure it annually against what the same money would have done sitting in the core. Most people who do this honestly close the sleeve within three years, which is the point of the exercise.

The legitimate exceptions

Three deviations from a static policy are defensible and are not really "tactical" at all, because they follow rules rather than forecasts:

Rebalancing

Mechanically selling what rose and buying what fell. This is contrarian by construction and requires no forecast. It is covered in full later in this guide.

Glide path

Reducing equity as a dated goal approaches. This is a response to shortening horizon, not to a market view, and should be written into the IPS in advance with specific years and percentages.

Valuation-aware contribution routing

Directing new money toward whichever asset is below its band. Identical to rebalancing in effect, zero tax cost, and requires no sale. This is the only form of "buying the dip" worth institutionalising.

Choosing between the models

Start one notch more conservative than you think

The cost of being 10 percentage points under-allocated to equity for five years is roughly 0.45% a year of forgone return. The cost of panicking once out of a too-aggressive allocation is 20–40% of a lifetime outcome, as the opening arithmetic showed. The asymmetry is enormous and runs entirely one way.

Raise equity by experience, not by appetite

A sensible rule: you may increase your equity target by 5 percentage points only after you have continued contributing through a drawdown of at least 20%. This ties allocation to demonstrated behaviour rather than imagined fortitude.

Simplicity beats precision

The difference between a 65/35 and a 70/30 allocation is noise. The difference between an allocation you follow for 25 years and one you abandon in year 6 is everything. A slightly suboptimal plan executed is strictly better than an optimal plan abandoned.

Write the drawdown number into your IPS

Not "I am an aggressive investor" but "this allocation can fall to ₹55 lakh and I have decided in advance that I will keep contributing." The rupee figure is what makes the commitment real.

International Exposure — the Case For and Against

India is roughly 4% of global equity market capitalisation and a far smaller share of global corporate earnings. An Indian investor with 100% domestic equity has made an enormous implicit bet: that one country's equity market, in one currency, driven by one policy regime, will serve them for forty years. That bet may pay off handsomely. It is still a bet, and it is rarely made consciously.

Gold — the Role and the Vehicles

Gold is the asset most Indian households already own and least understand as a portfolio component. It pays no dividend, has no earnings to grow, and its price is ultimately whatever someone else will pay. That makes it indefensible on a cash-flow basis and defensible on a correlation basis. Its job is not to produce returns. Its job is to not fall when everything else does.

What gold does and does not do

Gold is a long-run inflation hedgeOver very long periods (30–50 years) roughly yes; over 5–15 year windows it has repeatedly failed to track inflation. It is a monetary-stress hedge more than an inflation hedge.

Gold hedges rupee depreciationStructurally yes, because gold is priced globally in dollars. For an Indian investor, INR gold return = USD gold return + rupee depreciation. This is a meaningful and under-appreciated part of its domestic appeal.

Practical gold rules

  • Decide a target weight (commonly 5–15%) and treat it as a permanent allocation, not a trade.
  • Count existing family gold you actually control at a conservative realisable value before buying more.
  • Rebalance into and out of it like any other sleeve — which means selling some after a gold rally, which will feel wrong.
  • Never judge it on 1-year or 3-year returns. The judgement window is "did it help in the one bad year out of ten," and you cannot know that in advance.
  • Check the premium or discount to underlying value before buying an exchange-traded gold product; thin trading can make this wide.
  • Verify the current tax treatment of your chosen vehicle before buying — the 23 July 2024 changes altered holding periods and rates for several non-equity assets, and gold vehicles differ from each other.

The Debt Allocation — What It Is Actually For

The most common error in the debt sleeve is treating it as a place to earn returns. It is not. It is a place to store certainty. Every attempt to raise the return of the debt sleeve — longer duration, lower credit quality, more complex structures — imports exactly the kind of risk the sleeve exists to exclude, and it imports it at the worst possible moment, because credit and duration stress correlate with equity stress.

The four jobs of a debt sleeve

1. Funding near-term goals

Money needed in under five years. The asset must be available in full, on the date, regardless of what equity has done. Duration and credit quality are matched to the goal date.

2. Dampening portfolio volatility

A 25% debt sleeve mechanically cuts the portfolio's equity drawdown by roughly a quarter. This is the sleeve's contribution to you not selling.

3. Providing rebalancing ammunition

When equity falls 40%, rebalancing requires something to sell. If the entire portfolio is equity, there is nothing to rebalance from. The debt sleeve is the dry powder.

4. Covering the sequence-of-returns problem

Near and during withdrawal, 2–5 years of spending held in debt means you never sell equity into a bear market to fund living expenses. This is the single most important structural protection in retirement.

Duration matching — the one concept that matters

Duration measures a bond or bond fund's price sensitivity to interest-rate changes. A fund with a modified duration of 6 falls roughly 6% in price if yields rise 1 percentage point, and rises roughly 6% if yields fall 1 point. For portfolio purposes the rule is simple: match the duration of the instrument to the horizon of the goal. If the money is needed in three years, a three-year duration means rate movements largely wash out by the time you need it. A seven-year duration for a three-year goal is a bet on rates, not a plan.

Credit quality — the sleeve where you should be boring

WHY CHASING YIELD IN THE DEBT SLEEVE IS
A BAD TRADE

Suppose two debt options for a Rs 10,00,000
stability sleeve:

  Option 1: sovereign / highest-rated, 7.0%
  Option 2: lower-rated credit, 9.0%

The spread is 2.0 percentage points = Rs 20,000
a year on Rs 10,00,000.

Now the downside. A credit event in a
concentrated low-rated portfolio can mean a
side-pocketed or written-down exposure. Historical
Indian experience includes instances where
investors took losses in the 10-50% range on
specific exposures, and where redemptions were
restricted at exactly the moment money was needed.

Expected value, crudely:
  gain if nothing happens      +Rs 20,000/yr
  loss if a 20% impairment
    hits 15% of the portfolio  -Rs 30,000
  probability needed to break
    even over 5 years           ~3.3%/yr

That is not an obviously good bet. But the real
objection is not the expected value - it is the
CORRELATION. Credit stress arrives with equity
stress and with job stress. The sleeve that was
supposed to be your rebalancing ammunition and
your emergency backstop is impaired at exactly
the moment you need it.

THE RULE: take your risk in the equity sleeve,
where you are paid for it and where you have
chosen it deliberately. Do not take hidden,
uncompensated, correlated risk in the sleeve
whose entire purpose is certainty.

Goal-Based Bucketing — a Worked Three-Goal Plan

A single pooled portfolio with one allocation cannot simultaneously serve a down-payment in four years and retirement in twenty-six. Bucketing solves this by running a separate, horizon-appropriate allocation for each goal. The overhead is modest — typically three to six holdings in total — and the behavioural benefit is large, because a fall in the retirement bucket no longer threatens the house.

If you run a direct-equity sleeve, position sizing is the only risk control that works without requiring you to be right. Stop-losses require a correct exit decision. Rebalancing — Rules, Honest Benefits, and Tax

Rebalancing is the act of restoring your portfolio to its policy weights by selling what has risen and buying what has fallen. It is the only part of portfolio management that mechanically forces contrarian behaviour, it requires no forecast, and it is widely over-sold as a source of return. Understanding what it actually does — risk control first, return second and only sometimes — is what makes you do it consistently.

Tax-aware rebalancing — use inflows, not sales

In a taxable Indian account, every rebalancing sale is a realisation event. The order of preference is therefore strict, and following it eliminates most rebalancing tax in the accumulation phase.

  1. Route new contributionsDirect the entire monthly SIP to the underweight asset until the band is restored. Zero tax, zero cost. This alone handles most drift for anyone still contributing meaningfully relative to corpus size.
  2. Route lumpy inflowsBonus, variable pay, maturing FD, RSU sale proceeds, tax refund. Same effect, larger.
  3. Stop contributions to the overweight assetEven without redirecting, halting inflow to the overweight sleeve slows drift.
  4. Rebalance inside tax-sheltered vehicles firstReallocating within NPS, or adjusting EPF/PPF contribution levels, changes the portfolio mix without a taxable event. NPS allows asset-mix changes subject to PFRDA rules — verify current limits and frequency at pfrda.org.in.
  5. Harvest the exemptionIf a sale is needed, size it so realised long-term gains on listed equity stay within the s.112A exemption of ₹1,25,000 (125,000 rupees) per financial year — as at FY 2025-26, verify.
  6. Select lots deliberatelyIndian mutual fund redemptions are generally FIFO, which may not be what you want. Across multiple folios or demat lots you have some control. Prefer selling units with the highest cost basis, and prefer long-term over short-term lots.
  7. Harvest losses against the gainsIf you hold a position at a loss, realising it in the same year offsets the gain. Observe the set-off rules: short-term capital loss sets off against both STCG and LTCG; long-term capital loss sets off only against LTCG. See the tax guide.
  8. Only then sellAnd when you do, split across two financial years if the amount is large and the band permits.

Rebalancing during a crash

The hardest rebalance is the one that requires buying equity after it has fallen 40%. It is also the one with the largest effect. Three practical supports:

Pre-commit numerically

The IPS drawdown protocol should say exactly what you buy at −20%, −30% and −40%, in rupees or percentages. A decision made in advance is executed; a decision made in the moment is deferred.

Automate what you can

A standing instruction that continues regardless of the market is the single most effective structure. The decision you do not have to make is the decision you cannot get wrong.

Stage the deployment

Splitting the rebalancing purchase into three tranches over six weeks is slightly suboptimal mathematically and substantially better psychologically, because it removes the "what if it falls more tomorrow" paralysis.

When to Sell — the Legitimate Reasons

Most selling is a reaction dressed as a decision. There are, however, genuinely good reasons to sell, and the discipline is to know them in advance so you can test any impulse against the list. If the reason you are considering is not on the list, the correct action is to wait thirty days and write down what has actually changed about the next twenty years.

The legitimate reasons

ReasonApplies toTest
1. You need the money for the goal it was saved forEverythingIs the goal date here? Then sell. This is not a market decision at all.
2. Rebalancing requires itEverythingIs a band breached and can inflows not fix it? Then sell the minimum required.
3. The glide path requires itDated goalsIs this the pre-written year for reducing equity? Then do it on schedule, not on sentiment.
4. The original thesis is brokenDirect equity, active fundsWrite the thesis down at purchase. Sell when a specific, pre-identified element of it fails — not when the price falls.
5. The vehicle changed materiallyFundsMandate change, merger into a different category, a change in the stated process, expense ratio rising materially, or a fund growing so large that its stated strategy is no longer executable.
6. A governance or integrity red flagDirect equityAuditor resignation, repeated restatements, undisclosed related-party dealings, regulatory action on the promoter. These are sell-first-investigate-later events.
7. Tax-loss harvesting with an immediate repurchase intentTaxable positions at a lossRealising a loss to offset gains, with the allocation restored. 8. A concentration limit is breachedDirect equity, employer stockPosition above 5%, employer stock above 10%, sector above 25%. Mechanical.
9. Your circumstances changedEverythingJob loss, illness, a dependant, a divorce, an inheritance, a change in horizon. The allocation serves the life, not the other way round.

The illegitimate reasons

ReasonWhy it fails
"The market has fallen a lot"This is the condition under which future expected returns are higher, not lower. Selling here converts a paper loss into a permanent one and forfeits the recovery.
"The market has risen a lot and looks expensive"Markets can stay expensive for years. Rebalancing already trims the winner mechanically; a discretionary exit is a timing bet with two required correct decisions.

Measuring Performance Properly

Almost every number an investor quotes about their own returns is wrong, usually flatteringly so. "My portfolio is up 40%" conflates absolute return with annualised return, ignores the timing of contributions, and is measured against nothing. There are four different measures, each answering a different question, and using the wrong one leads to the wrong conclusions about whether your plan is working.

The four measures

MeasureAnswersUse whenFails when
Absolute return"How much more money do I have?"Single lump sum, single period, no cash flowsAny multi-period or multi-cash-flow situation. Says nothing about rate.
CAGR (compound annual growth rate)"At what constant annual rate did a single lump sum grow?"One investment, one entry, one exitSIPs, top-ups, withdrawals — i.e. almost every real portfolio
XIRR (extended internal rate of return)"What annualised rate did my actual money, invested on my actual dates, earn?"Any portfolio with irregular contributions and withdrawalsComparing managers, because it is affected by your timing decisions, not just theirs
TWRR (time-weighted rate of return)"How did the underlying investment perform, stripping out the effect of cash-flow timing?"Judging a fund, a manager or an index — which is why published fund returns are TWRRTelling you what you actually earned
The one-line distinctionXIRR measures you. TWRR measures the investment. If your fund's published return is 14% and your XIRR in that fund is 9%, the fund did not lie — your contribution timing (or your interruptions) cost you 5 percentage points. That gap is the single most useful number in personal investing, because it is the part you control. It is usually called the "investor return gap," and across markets it is persistently negative.

XIRR finds the rate r that makes the net present
value of all dated cash flows equal to zero:

  sum over i of  CF_i / (1+r)^(d_i/365)  =  0

Outflows (money you put in) are NEGATIVE.
Inflows (redemptions, and the final value
treated as a notional inflow today) are POSITIVE.

Two XIRR mistakes that produce wildly wrong numbers(1) Forgetting to include the current value as a final positive flow. Without it the function has only negative flows and either errors or returns nonsense. (2) Double-counting a switch. If you redeem from fund A and subscribe to fund B on the same day, that is two flows in the individual fund records but zero net flow at the portfolio level. Including both at portfolio level inflates the invested amount and understates the return.

The Annual Review

Once a year, for two hours, you look at everything properly. The rest of the year you look at almost nothing. The review has a fixed agenda, a fixed order and a fixed output, and critically it separates reviewing the plan (allowed) from reacting to returns (not allowed).

The agenda, in order

  1. Life facts first, before any numberIncome, job stability, dependants, health, planned major expenses, horizon changes. Do this before opening the portfolio so the numbers do not colour the assessment. A new dependant or a planned sabbatical changes the allocation; a good year does not.
  2. Insurance and emergency fundIs term cover still adequate for current liabilities and income replacement? Has health cover kept pace with medical inflation? Is the emergency fund still 6–12 months of current expenses, which have probably risen?
  3. Savings rateCompute actual savings as a percentage of take-home for the year just ended. This is the number with the most leverage. Apply the step-up before lifestyle absorbs the increment.
  4. Goal progressFor each goal: required corpus, current corpus, projected corpus at the current contribution and assumed return, gap. Adjust contributions, not allocations, to close gaps.
  5. Allocation versus policyDrift per asset class, including EPF/PPF/NPS as debt and employer stock as equity concentration. Rebalance per the policy.
  6. Performance measurementPortfolio XIRR, blended benchmark TWRR, the gap, maximum drawdown experienced during the year. Satellite scorecard versus the core counterfactual.
  7. Cost auditTotal expense ratios of every holding, total transaction charges for the year, any regular-plan holdings that should be direct, any accounts charging maintenance fees you forgot about.
  8. Tax reviewRealised gains and losses for the year, carry-forward losses available, whether the s.112A exemption was used, advance-tax position. Do this in February, not March, so there is time to act.
  9. Holdings auditDoes every holding still have a job? Any overlap that has crept in? Any position that breaches a concentration limit? Any fund whose mandate changed?
  10. HousekeepingNominations on every account. KYC status. Contact details current. The document file findable by someone other than you.
  11. Read last year's logWhat did you believe twelve months ago? What did you do? What did it cost or earn? This is the only reliable calibration available.
  12. Write the new log entry and re-sign the IPSIncluding any amendments, with their justifications, which may not reference recent market performance.
The annual review is not the place to change your mind about equityThe failure mode of an annual review is using it as a licence to make the change you have wanted to make all year. Guard against it mechanically: allocation changes require a life-fact change, documented in step 1, written before the portfolio was opened. If step 1 is blank, step 5 cannot change targets — only restore them.

Every structural element of this guide exists because of one empirical observation: investors reliably earn less than the investments they own, and the gap is behavioural. The rules below are not motivational; they are engineering. Each one removes a decision from a moment when you will decide badly.

The specific biases that cost Indian retail investors the most

Recency and performance chasing

Money flows into whichever category just performed best — small caps after a small-cap run, thematic funds at the top of a theme. The flows peak near the top because that is when the past returns look best. Protection: fixed policy weights, and the rule that you may never add a holding because of its recent return.

Loss aversion and the disposition effect

Selling winners to "book profits" while holding losers to "avoid booking the loss." This is tax-inefficient in both directions and systematically keeps the worst holdings. Protection: thesis-based selling, and the explicit recognition that a loss is already real whether or not you have realised it.

Action bias

In conditions of uncertainty, doing something feels better than doing nothing, regardless of expected value. Markets reward inaction more often than any other activity in life. Protection: the 30-day rule, and having a designated non-portfolio activity for when the urge arrives.

Overconfidence after early success

Anyone whose first two investing years coincided with a bull market will conclude they are skilled. Position sizes grow, caution falls, and the first real drawdown arrives at maximum exposure. Protection: the satellite cap and position limits, set before the success.

Anchoring to purchase price

"I will sell when it gets back to what I paid." The market has no knowledge of your cost basis. The only relevant question is whether you would buy it today at today's price. Protection: evaluate holdings as if you held cash instead.

Narrative susceptibility

A compelling story about a sector, a theme or a country is the most reliable predictor of a crowded, expensive position. Protection: ask what price the story is already in, and see the sources guide on how narratives are manufactured and monetised.

The behaviour-gap arithmeticStudies of investor returns versus fund returns across markets consistently find a gap — investors earn less than the funds they hold, because of when they buy and sell. Estimates of the gap vary by study, period and methodology and should be treated with care, but the sign is consistent and the magnitude is material. Assume that your own timing will cost you something, and build the structures that minimise the number of timing decisions you get to make. The investor who automates and ignores is not being lazy; they are removing the main source of loss.

Frequently Asked Questions

Questions that come up repeatedly from Indian investors building a first real portfolio. Answers are general education and are not advice for your situation.

I have ₹10 lakh (1,000,000 rupees) sitting in a savings account. Should I invest it all at once or stagger it?
The arithmetic favours lump-sum roughly two-thirds of the time, because markets rise more often than they fall and staggering keeps money in cash earning less. The behavioural argument favours staggering, because a lump sum invested just before a 30% fall produces the regret that makes people abandon plans. A middle path that most people can live with: invest the portion corresponding to your debt and gold allocation immediately (low variance, little regret risk), and stagger the equity portion over 6–12 months via a systematic transfer, with a written rule that you accelerate rather than pause if markets fall. Decide the schedule in advance and do not renegotiate it.
What is the single most important number in my portfolio?
Your savings rate. For an investor in the first decade, contributions dominate returns completely — a ₹40 lakh (4,000,000 rupee) portfolio earning 11% gains ₹4.4 lakh a year, while raising a monthly contribution from ₹50,000 to ₹70,000 adds ₹2.4 lakh a year with certainty and no risk. After the corpus is perhaps ten times your annual contribution, returns begin to dominate. Until then, optimise savings, not selection.
My portfolio has 14 mutual funds. Is that diversified?
Almost certainly not. If most of them are Indian equity funds, you hold roughly one bet with fourteen expense ratios and fourteen capital-gains statements. Check two things: the pairwise portfolio overlap between your largest holdings, and how much of your total portfolio sits outside Indian equity. Consolidation is worth doing, but do it with tax awareness — sell down over two or three financial years, use the s.112A exemption each year, and prefer to redirect new contributions rather than liquidate everything at once.
Should I invest in a lump sum when the market falls 10%, or keep the SIP going?
Both, and the order matters. Keep the SIP running unconditionally — that is the part that must never be a decision. Separately, have a pre-written rule in your IPS for deploying idle cash at specified drawdown levels (−20%, −30%, −40%). What does not work is waiting for a fall with cash on the sidelines indefinitely; the cash drag while you wait usually exceeds the benefit of the eventual discount, and the fall that finally arrives rarely feels like the right moment.
How many holdings should a portfolio have?
Four to eight for almost everyone: one broad domestic equity exposure, optionally one mid/small, one international, one gold, one or two debt, plus any tax-advantaged retirement vehicle. More than eight and you cannot track them, your overlap rises, and your rebalancing gets complicated for no benefit. If you run a direct-equity satellite, 8–20 names inside a capped sleeve is a separate count.
Is my EPF part of my asset allocation?
Yes, entirely, and it is debt. This is the most commonly missed adjustment in Indian portfolios. An engineer contributing ₹21,000 a month to EPF while investing ₹25,000 a month in equity funds has a 46% debt allocation on new money, not the 100% equity they believe. Pull the EPF passbook once a year, add the balance to the debt line of your allocation tab, and recompute. The usual consequence is that the fresh-money equity share needs to be higher than you assumed.
My employer gives me RSUs. How should I treat them?
As cash compensation that happened to arrive in share form, and as a concentration risk that compounds an existing one. Your salary, bonus, job security and vested stock all depend on the same company. The standard discipline is to sell each tranche promptly on vesting and reinvest into your policy allocation. There is a tax cost to the sale, and there is a possibility the stock rises afterwards — but the scenario that matters is the one where the company struggles, your stock falls 60%, your bonus is cut and hiring freezes, all in the same quarter. Cap employer exposure at 10% of the portfolio and aim lower. The tax treatment of RSUs is complex — perquisite tax at vest, capital gains at sale, and Schedule FA disclosure if the shares are foreign. Have a chartered accountant review it.
How much international exposure is right for an Indian investor?
There is no single answer, and anyone who gives you one without asking about your tax position is guessing. The framing: your entire balance sheet — salary, employer, home, deposits, future earnings — is already overwhelmingly India-exposed, so diversifying the portfolio is not reckless. Against that, the tax treatment of most international routes is worse than domestic equity, costs are higher, and availability has been interrupted by industry-level overseas investment limits. Most practitioners land at 10–20% of the total portfolio. Below 5% it is too small to do anything; above 35% the friction starts eating the benefit. Verify the current tax treatment of your chosen route before committing.
Should I hold gold when it pays no income?
The case for gold is not that it returns well; it is that it returns differently. Its correlation with Indian equity is near zero and occasionally negative, so a 10% sleeve reduces portfolio drawdown by several percentage points at a cost of roughly 0.3 percentage points of expected return — and on a compound basis, the reduced variance roughly offsets even that. The hard part is holding it through the multi-year stretches when it does nothing. If you already hold substantial family gold you actually control, count it and your incremental allocation may be zero.
What should I do when my portfolio falls 30%?
Exactly what your IPS says, which you wrote before it happened. For most long-horizon investors that is: continue the SIP, do not redeem, deploy a pre-specified share of idle cash, rebalance back into equity, write a log entry describing how it feels, and reduce how often you look. What you must not do is make a new decision during the fall. If you find yourself wanting to, invoke the 30-day rule. Historically, the investors who did nothing during severe drawdowns substantially outperformed those who acted — but past behaviour of markets is not a guarantee, and the honest statement is that nobody knows how long any particular recovery takes.
Is rebalancing worth the tax cost?
Usually yes for risk control, usually no as a return enhancer. The way to get the benefit without most of the cost is to rebalance with inflows rather than sales: direct every new contribution to the most underweight asset. That handles most drift for anyone still contributing meaningfully. When a sale is unavoidable, rebalance only to the band edge rather than to target, select the highest-cost-basis lots, keep realised long-term equity gains within the s.112A exemption where possible, and consider splitting a large rebalance across two financial years. Verify the current exemption and rates.
My fund says it returned 16% but my app shows 9%. Who is lying?
Neither. The fund publishes a time-weighted return, which assumes a single investment held for the whole period. Your 9% is a money-weighted return (XIRR) reflecting when your money actually went in. If you invested more after the rally and less during the fall — which is what almost everyone does — your money-weighted return will be lower. The gap is the cost of your timing, and it is the most actionable number in your portfolio because it is entirely within your control.
Should I prepay my home loan or invest the money?
Compare the loan's effective after-tax rate against the expected after-tax return of the investment, then weight the comparison toward prepayment because the loan return is certain and the investment return is not. A 9% home loan against an expected 10.5% equity return pre-tax is not a 1.5-point edge once you account for capital gains tax and the risk that the 10.5% does not arrive. Beyond the arithmetic: a smaller EMI raises your risk capacity, which lets you hold more equity with the rest. Many people reasonably split the surplus. Interest deduction provisions differ sharply between the old and new tax regimes — and most Chapter VI-A deductions are unavailable under the default new regime. Have a chartered accountant model your specific case.
Do I need a financial adviser?
If you can write and follow an IPS, compute an XIRR and resist acting in a crash, probably not for the mechanics — though a one-off paid engagement to sanity-check the plan is cheap insurance. Where advice earns its fee is in behaviour (someone who stops you selling at the bottom), in complex tax and estate situations, and when your own situation involves business income, foreign assets or a family with competing interests. If you engage someone, use a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser who charges a fee rather than earning commission on what you buy, and verify their registration number on the SEBI intermediary portal at sebi.gov.in before paying anything.
What is the simplest portfolio that is actually defensible?
Four holdings: a broad-market domestic equity index exposure, a developed-market international equity exposure, a gold exposure, and a short-duration debt holding — in proportions set by your horizon — plus your EPF counted as debt and an emergency fund held separately. Contributions automated, quarterly band check, annual rebalance and review. That portfolio is boring, cheap, diversified across four genuinely distinct drivers, takes about three hours a year, and will beat the large majority of more complicated efforts because it is one you can actually maintain for twenty-five years.
How do I start if I already have a messy portfolio?
Do not liquidate everything. Work in this order: (1) get a consolidated statement by PAN and list everything that exists; (2) write the IPS from scratch, ignoring what you currently hold; (3) compute current weights against the new policy; (4) stop all contributions to holdings outside the policy and redirect them to policy weights; (5) over the next 18–24 months, sell down the non-policy holdings, using the annual s.112A exemption and harvesting any losses, roughly one or two positions per quarter; (6) set nominations on everything as you go. The transition is boring and takes two years. Doing it in one week for tax-indifferent reasons is the expensive version.

The Portfolio Review Checklist and Annual Calendar

Everything in this guide collapses into a small number of recurring actions. The lists below are deliberately short enough to actually use. Dates relating to tax and compliance are stated as at FY 2025-26 / AY 2026-27 and must be verified at incometax.gov.in each year, since due dates are routinely extended or changed by notification.

Advance-tax instalment months are indicative of the standard 15 June / 15 September / 15 December / 15 March pattern under s.211. Verify applicability and exact dates each year at incometax.gov.in.

Monthly — 15 minutes

  • Confirm every standing instruction executed. A failed SIP is silent and costs a month.
  • Enter the month's transactions into Tab 1 of the tracker, including all charges.
  • Update current values in Tab 2. Do not form an opinion about them.
  • Note any corporate action, dividend, bonus, split or rights that affects cost basis.
  • Nothing else. Do not read the allocation tab. Do not compute returns.

Quarterly — 20 minutes

  • Open Tab 3. Check every asset class against its band.
  • If a band is breached, first redirect the next quarter's contributions; only sell if that cannot restore it.
  • Check concentration limits: largest single stock, employer stock, largest sector.
  • Check that the emergency fund is still intact and still sized to current expenses.
  • One line in the log. No performance judgement.

Annually — 2 hours, each April

  • Step 1: list life changes before opening the portfolio. If none, no target may change.
  • Re-sizing of term and health cover against current liabilities, income and medical inflation.
  • Emergency fund re-sized to current monthly expenses, not last year's.
  • Actual savings rate for the year just ended, computed and written down.
  • Goal-by-goal progress: required, current, projected, gap. Close gaps with contributions, not with risk.
  • Full allocation review including EPF, PPF, NPS as debt and employer stock as equity concentration.
  • Unconditional rebalance to policy targets.
  • Apply the annual SIP step-up in the same week the appraisal lands.
  • Portfolio XIRR, blended benchmark TWRR, the gap, maximum drawdown experienced.
  • Satellite scorecard versus the core counterfactual; apply the closure rule if triggered.
  • Cost audit: every expense ratio, total transaction charges, any surviving regular-plan holdings.
  • Holdings audit: does every holding still have a job? Any mandate change? Any overlap?
  • Nomination status on every demat, folio, bank account, EPF, NPS and insurance policy.
  • KYC status current; contact details and email correct with every institution.
  • Read last year's log entries. Write this year's. Re-sign the IPS with any amendments and their justifications.
